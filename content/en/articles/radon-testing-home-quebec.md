Your pre-purchase inspector will not measure radon. It is not an oversight: the
only measurement Health Canada considers valid requires a device left in place
for at least three months, while an inspection lasts a few hours. Here is how
radon is actually measured in Quebec, and the mechanism the forms provide for a
test that finishes long after you sign at the notary.

## What radon is, and why it concentrates in the basement

**A gas that comes out of the ground.** Health Canada describes it as "a
radioactive gas that comes from the natural breakdown of uranium in soil and
rock". It is invisible, odourless and tasteless. Outdoors it dilutes and poses
no risk. The problem begins with enclosure: it gets in through cracks in
foundation walls and slabs, through construction joints and through the openings
around pipes. That is why the measurement is taken on the lowest lived-in level,
never on the second floor.

On the risk, let us quote the health authorities word for word. Health Canada
writes that "long term exposure to high levels of radon is the leading cause of
lung cancer in non-smokers". The Regie du batiment du Quebec (Quebec's building
authority, the RBQ) reports that in Quebec, 10 to 16% of lung cancer deaths are
associated with radon, which is more than 600 deaths a year.

## The 200 becquerels per cubic metre guideline

Radon concentration is measured in becquerels per cubic metre of air, written
Bq/m³. The Canadian guideline recommends remedial action when the annual average
concentration exceeds 200 Bq/m³ in the normally occupied areas of a building.
Three words in that sentence deserve your attention.

**Annual average.** Not a peak, and not the value of one afternoon, but an
average over the year, estimated from a long term test.

**Guideline.** For an existing home, no law forces you to fix anything. The
Government of Quebec recommends doing it within a year.

**Normally occupied areas.** A crawl space does not count. A finished basement
where a teenager sleeps does.

The Government of Quebec indicates that the average concentration in Quebec
basements is around 37 Bq/m³, but that it can exceed 1,000 Bq/m³. The average is
reassuring. It says nothing about your address.

## Radon in Quebec, region by region: what the public tests show

Health Canada's cross-Canada survey publishes its results by health region. Here
are the Greater Montreal regions, plus one point of comparison.

| Region | Homes tested | % above 200 Bq/m³ |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| Gaspesie and Iles-de-la-Madeleine | 174 | 25.3 |
| Outaouais | 62 | 12.9 |
| Laval | 107 | 12.1 |
| Laurentides | 78 | 10.3 |
| Monteregie | 112 | 8.9 |
| Montreal | 73 | 6.9 |
| Lanaudiere | 76 | 3.9 |

These are raw percentages, drawn from small samples of volunteer homes. For
Quebec as a whole, the survey gives 10.1% of tested homes above the guideline,
and Health Canada adds a sentence that is worth the whole table: "There is no
radon free region in Canada."

Quebec has in fact given up on carving up its territory. The RBQ reports that
mapping by the ministere de la Sante et des Services sociaux (Quebec's health
ministry) shows that every region of Quebec contains, to varying degrees,
sectors with high radon emission potential, without being able to delimit their
extent at the municipal scale. In the Laurentides, a screening campaign by the
INSPQ (Quebec's public health institute) in elementary schools found 4 schools
out of 22 above the federal guideline; in the Outaouais, only 2 schools.

The OACIQ (Quebec's real estate regulator) is blunt about this: nobody should
extrapolate the concentration in one building from results obtained in another,
because of differences in ground conditions and construction. Whether you are
shopping [in Laval](/en/real-estate-broker/laval/) or anywhere else, radon
cannot be deduced from a postal code. It gets measured, one house at a time.

## Testing radon in a Quebec home: three months, in the heating season

Health Canada's measurement guide requires the test to be conducted so that a
minimum of 91 days fall within the heating season. The Government of Quebec
translates that into practice: a passive detector for at least three months,
ideally from October to April. An electronic detector can run for a minimum of
one month, but if the result exceeds 100 Bq/m³ after that month, the measurement
has to be extended over at least three months or validated with a passive
detector. The device goes in the lowest normally occupied area, at least 30
centimetres away from walls and ceilings.

And the 48 hour test sold at the hardware store? Health Canada settles it:
"short term measurements are not appropriate for informing remedial action
decisions". Two days in July with the windows open teach you nothing you can
use. A detector can be bought from the Association pulmonaire du Quebec (the
Quebec lung association), from CAA-Quebec or at a hardware store; otherwise, a
professional certified by the Programme national de competence sur le radon au
Canada, the PNCR-C, will measure for you.

## A three month radon test against the deadline in a promesse d'achat

The OACIQ names the problem directly: "The main issue with radon in the context
of a real estate transaction is the time of year and the time required to carry
out the test." Your inspection condition is counted in days, the test is counted
in months. The two never meet.

There is, however, a mechanism built into the forms. Standard clause 3.21, Radon
measurement test, instructs the officiating notary to hold back a sum out of the
proceeds of the sale. It is remitted to the buyer if a report produced by a
professional certified in radon measurement establishes that the annual average
concentration in the living space is equal to or greater than 200 Bq/m³, and if
a copy of the report is given to the seller within the number of days written in
following the signing of the deed of sale. Past that deadline, the sum goes to
the seller.

Two details change everything, and they are discussed with your notary before
signing. The amount held back and the deadline are blanks to be filled in: 90
days signed in May cover no part of a heating season. And the clause states that
on receipt of that sum, the buyer waives any claim and any other recourse in
that respect. You are trading a recourse for an amount set before you know the
result.

## The result is above 200 Bq/m³: what the fixes involve

The least expensive measures come first, and the OACIQ lists them: sealing
foundation cracks, sealing openings in contact with the ground, covering sumps
and venting them to the outside, improving basement ventilation. If the
concentration is very high, they will not be enough.

The real solution has a name: active sub-slab depressurization. A pipe goes
through the foundation, a fan draws the gas from under the house and discharges
it outdoors before it gets in. Health Canada writes that such a system will
reduce concentrations by more than 80%, that it is installed in one day only,
and for the same price as other common home repairs. The work goes to a
mitigation professional certified by the PNCR-C.

What I cannot give you is an invoice: no government source consulted publishes a
Quebec price range. Treat this item as a reserve to plan for, in the same way as
the other amounts on the [closing costs list](/en/tools/closing-costs/), and
insist on two written quotes.

## New construction, the seller's declaration, and what nobody can quantify

**The construction code.** Since a regulation that came into force on 2 June
2022, with a transition period that ended on 8 July 2023, protection against
soil gases applies across all of Quebec: the notion of "recognized risk zones"
has been removed. A basic conduit, a 100 millimetre pipe running vertically
through the slab on grade, is mandatory everywhere, and the RBQ puts it at 300
dollars for a 90 square metre home. It is a starting point, not a system: the
owner completes it with passive or active extraction if concentrations above 200
Bq/m³ are observed. A [new
home](/en/articles/new-construction-guide-quebec/) therefore has to be tested
too, and the RBQ makes the point: the radon concentration in a building cannot
be known before its construction is finished.

**What the seller has to say.** The results of prior expert assessments,
including detector analyses, must be made available to the buyer through the
answers to clauses D12.1, D12.2 and D13.9 of the Declarations du vendeur (the
seller's declaration form). The listing broker must question the seller to find
out whether tests or corrective work have already been done, and disclose that
information to anyone proposing to acquire the property. A high result kept
quiet is the territory of the [seller's
declaration](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/) and of the [latent
defect](/en/articles/latent-defect-recourse-quebec/).

**And the price?** No public source allows anyone to quantify the effect of a
radon result on the value of a property in Quebec. The only official figure that
touches the subject is a sentence from the RBQ about its own basic conduit
requirement: "This measure has no influence on the resale price of properties."
Anyone who announces a percentage to you is making it up.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can my pre-purchase inspector measure radon during the visit?**
No. The inspection is a visual examination done in a few hours; a valid
measurement takes 91 days in the heating season. The inspector can recommend the
test, and that is all.

**The seller refuses a three month deadline. What now?**
That is the normal case, not bad faith. The route provided for is holding back a
sum with the notary under standard clause 3.21, with an amount and a deadline
that you negotiate. Have the wording reviewed before you sign.

**Can renovations increase radon?**
According to the OACIQ, research conducted abroad shows that energy renovations,
windows, doors, insulation, can reduce air renewal and increase indoor
concentrations. A result is therefore not a permanent licence: after major work,
you measure again.
