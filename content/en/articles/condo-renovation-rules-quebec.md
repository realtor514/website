You want to pull up the carpet and lay hardwood, replace the living room window,
close in the balcony. In a house, you call a contractor and check the municipal
permit. In a divided co-ownership, one question comes before the budget: who owns
the thing you are about to touch. The answer changes from one building to the
next, and it sits in your déclaration de copropriété (the deed of
co-ownership registered against your building).

## Partie privative and partie commune: the line is not where you think

The Code civil du Québec (Quebec's Civil Code) sets out two definitions. Article
1042: a partie privative (private portion) is the part a determinate co-owner
owns and has exclusive use of. Article 1043: a partie commune (common portion)
belongs to everyone and serves for common use.

Between the two sits a third category, the one buyers discover too late. The
second paragraph of article 1043 provides that certain common portions may serve
the use of only some co-owners or of a single one, and that **the rules governing
common portions apply to these parties communes à usage restreint** (common
portions for restricted use). Your balcony, your storage locker, your parking
space can therefore be yours to use and everyone's to own. Article 1064 draws the
financial conclusion: those who have the use of a partie commune à usage
restreint alone contribute to its maintenance and its day-to-day repairs.

Article 1044 then lists a series of presumptions. Presumed to be common portions
are the ground, the yards, **the balconies**, the stairways, the parking and
storage areas, **the main walls and structural framework of buildings** and **the
pipes and conduits, including those passing through private portions**.

A presumption can be rebutted. What settles the question in your building is the
descriptive statement of fractions, which contains the cadastral description of
the private and common portions (article 1055). The principle is the one set out
in [what you are actually buying in a
co-ownership](/en/articles/condo-vs-house-montreal/): you buy a fraction, not
four walls.

## The floor, the window and the balcony

**The floor covering.** The finished floor is generally private, but the slab
carrying it is part of the structural framework, presumed common. Many
declarations impose a minimum acoustic rating or a percentage of the surface that
must stay covered. Those clauses hold: article 1056 allows restrictions justified
by the destination of the immovable, its characteristics or its location. Sound
insulation is one of them.

**The window.** It appears nowhere in the list in article 1044. No presumption
places it on either side: your declaration decides, and both solutions exist in
Quebec. Until you have read the descriptive statement, nobody can tell you who
pays for your window.

**The balcony.** That one is named in article 1044 and presumed common, most
often for restricted use. A replaced railing, a resurfaced floor, an added glass
structure all touch a partie commune, even if you are the only person who ever
sets foot on it.

| Element | Presumed status under the Code civil |
|---|---|
| Balcony | Partie commune (art. 1044) |
| Parking, storage | Partie commune (art. 1044) |
| Structural framework, slab, frame | Partie commune (art. 1044) |
| Pipes, even inside your unit | Partie commune (art. 1044) |
| Partition between two units | Presumed common wall (art. 1045) |
| Window | No mention: the declaration decides |
| Finished floor covering | Private, subject to the by-laws |

## What the déclaration de copropriété requires before the work

Under article 1052, the declaration comprises three documents: the act
constituting the co-ownership, which defines the destination of the immovable and
of its portions (article 1053), the by-laws of the immovable, which set the rules
on enjoyment, use and maintenance of the private and common portions (article
1054), and the descriptive statement of fractions. The clauses on floors,
windows, work site hours and prior written consent live in the by-laws.

Article 1063 closes the loop: each co-owner uses and enjoys his partie privative
freely, **on condition that he observes the by-laws of the immovable and does not
impair the rights of the other co-owners or the destination of the immovable**.
Article 1062 adds that the declaration binds the co-owners and their successors
from the moment it is registered. You never signed yours. It applies anyway.

## Asking the syndicat for permission, in writing

No article of the Code requires authorization for every renovation. It is the
by-laws of the immovable that create that obligation, and most of them do so as
soon as the work touches a common portion, the structure, the acoustics, the
plumbing or the façade.

A request that gets processed quickly contains six things: the exact description
of the work, the plans, the name and licence number of the contractor, the work
site dates, proof of insurance and the list of common portions affected. Send it
in writing and keep the written answer. In any event, the board must send the
minutes of its decisions to the co-owners within 30 days (article 1086.1).

If the work amounts to a transformation, an enlargement or an improvement of the
common portions, the board no longer decides alone: article 1097 requires three
quarters of the votes of the co-owners present or represented. To join two
adjoining units, article 1100 is explicit: possible without the general meeting,
but with the consent of your hypothecary creditor and of the syndicat (the legal
entity formed by all co-owners), followed by amendment of the declaration and of
the cadastral plan, at your expense.

A refusal is not always final. Article 1086.2 allows a co-owner to ask the court
to annul a board decision that is biased or made in contempt of the co-owners'
rights, within 90 days. The same deadline applies to a decision of the general
meeting (article 1103), on pain of forfeiture. That is a lawyer's question, not a
broker's.

## Load-bearing walls, structure and the municipal permit

The structural framework is presumed common. Partitions and walls that are not
part of it and that separate a private portion from a common portion or from
another private portion are presumed to be common walls (article 1045). Even the
interior wall you believe is yours is rarely yours alone.

The municipality then applies its own grid, independent of the co-ownership. In
Montreal, a permit is required if the work touches the foundations, modifies the
structure of the dwelling, **modifies a wall, load-bearing or not**, or increases
the floor area: $9.80 per $1,000 of work, minimum $167.40 for residential. In
Laval, an interior permit is required as soon as there is a change to the
dimensions of the rooms, the number of rooms or the structure: $92 for the first
$20,000, plus $2.50 per $1,000.

The Building Act is blunt. Its article 46 prohibits carrying on the activities of
a building contractor without holding the required licence, and the Régie du
bâtiment du Québec (Quebec's building board) publishes the register of licence
holders. Éducaloi puts it this way: without a licence, the contractor is
operating illegally. Check the number before the deposit, not after.

## The fonds de prévoyance and the carnet d'entretien

The regulation establishing various rules for divided co-ownership (CCQ, r. 8.01)
has been in force since 14 August 2025. Every syndicat must have a carnet
d'entretien (maintenance logbook) drawn up and obtain a study of the fonds de
prévoyance (contingency fund), with three years and one day to do it the first
time.

The logbook describes the common portions, the materials and the equipment,
estimates their remaining useful life, then lists the major repairs and
replacements to plan for over at least 25 years. It is revised every 5 years, or
every 10 years for a building of 8 private portions or fewer, 3 storeys or fewer
above ground, or none of whose common portions are inside a building. The study
of the fonds de prévoyance builds on that logbook, recommends the amount to have
on hand at the start of each year, and is likewise obtained every 5 years.

Why this concerns your kitchen: that document is what announces the roof, the
elevator or the façade, and therefore the possible special assessment. The
figures to ask for are detailed in [condo fees and the contingency
fund](/en/articles/condo-fees-investment-killer/). If the work is aimed at a
sale, start from the current value of the unit: the [value
estimator](/en/tools/home-estimate/) gives you a starting point, not a price.

## Renovating without authorization: the bill arrives at resale

Three mechanisms catch up with undeclared work.

**Insurance.** Article 1073 requires the syndicat to insure the whole of the
immovable, private portions included, **excluding improvements made by a co-owner
to his portion where they can be identified**. And article 1070 requires the
register of the syndicat to contain a description of the private portions precise
enough for those improvements to be identifiable. Your renovation becomes your
insurance risk the moment it departs from the baseline description.

**The sale documents.** Article 1068.1 requires the seller to give the
promisor-buyer an attestation from the syndicat on the state of the
co-ownership, produced within 15 days of a request. The regulation fixes its
minimum content: fonds de prévoyance, contributions and financial statements for
the past 3 years, insurance, claims over the past 5 years affecting the unit
sold, work planned over 10 years, pending litigation and amendments to the
declaration over the past 3 years.

**The form.** The Promise to Purchase for a divided co-ownership published by the
OACIQ (Quebec's real estate regulator) provides, at clause 9.1, for a condition
allowing examination of the declaration, the by-laws, the minutes, the insurance
policy, the financial statements and the **description of the private portion**.
That is where unauthorized work surfaces: the description no longer matches what
the buyer is walking through.

Finally, article 1080 allows the syndicat or a co-owner to apply for an
injunction and, where there is a refusal to obey it, the court may order the sale
of the fraction. The act constituting the co-ownership may also provide for a
penal clause (article 1053).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I rip out the carpet and lay hardwood?**
That depends on the by-laws of the immovable, not on the Code civil. Many
declarations require an acoustic underlay of a minimum rating or a percentage of
covered surface. Read the by-laws before you buy the material: article 1063
obliges you to comply with them.

**Can the syndicat force me to undo everything?**
It may ask the court to enjoin you to comply with the declaration where the
refusal causes serious and irreparable injury (article 1080). Restoration to the
former state is one possible outcome. Have a lawyer assess your file: the result
depends on the facts and on the exact wording of your declaration.

**Who pays to replace my window?**
There is no general answer. The window is not in the list in article 1044, and
the split depends on the descriptive statement and the by-laws of your building.
Ask for the full declaration and have your notary read the clause.

**I closed in my balcony without asking anyone. Is that a problem at sale time?**
Probably, since the balcony is presumed to be a partie commune by article 1044.
The syndicat's attestation, the minutes and the description of the partie
privative examined by the buyer are likely to reveal it. Better to regularize
before listing than to negotiate under pressure while the documents are being
reviewed.
