The number printed on the condo fee line of a listing tells you almost nothing
on its own. What you buy with a fraction of a divided co-ownership is a share in
the upkeep of an entire building, and Quebec has tightened the rules around that
upkeep considerably since 2019. Here is where the law stands, and what you are
entitled to see before you sign.

## What the fees cover, and why two similar buildings differ

Article 1064 of the Civil Code sets the rule: each co-owner contributes, in
proportion to the relative value of his fraction, to the expenses arising from
the co-ownership and the operation of the immovable, and to the contingency fund
and the self-insurance fund. Co-owners who use common portions for restricted
use bear those particular expenses alone.

Two buildings at the same price can therefore post very different fees without
either one being badly run. An elevator, a pool, an underground garage, a flat
roof, central heating included in the charges: each adds an expense today and a
replacement to finance later. The number of units matters just as much, since
that is what the bill is divided among, and the age of the building decides when
the big-ticket items arrive. Before comparing two numbers, compare what they
buy. [Condo or house](/en/articles/condo-vs-house-montreal/) is first a question
of how much you want to delegate.

## The contingency fund belongs to the syndicate

Article 1071 is short and decisive. The syndicate establishes, according to the
estimated cost of major repairs and the cost of replacement of common portions,
a contingency fund that is liquid and available at short notice, allocated
solely to those repairs and replacements. The fund is the property of the
syndicate.

Two practical consequences follow. Your contributions are never handed back when
you resell: they stay in the fund, and their value shows up in the price of the
unit, not in a cheque. And the fund does not pay for routine maintenance.

## What changed on 14 August 2025

That is the date the regulation implementing the 2019 Bill 16 measures came into
force. Two documents became mandatory for every divided co-ownership in Quebec.

**The maintenance logbook**, under article 1070.2 of the Civil Code. The board
has a logbook drawn up describing the maintenance done and to be done. The
regulation sets its content: an inventory of the common portions and materials,
their condition and remaining useful life, the major repairs and replacements
projected over 25 years with their estimated year, and a record of work already
carried out. The board updates it yearly and has it revised every five years.

**The contingency fund study**, built on that same 25-year horizon. It states
the fund balance and the estimated cost of each major repair in its year of
execution, then recommends the minimum that must be available at the start of
each year and the amounts to be paid in annually. The board obtains a new study
every five years.

Neither document is improvised. The regulation reserves their preparation to an
engineer, a chartered appraiser, an architect or a professional technologist,
and the study also to a chartered professional accountant, all independent of
the co-ownership.

The calendar matters. Existing co-ownerships have three years and one day from
14 August 2025, so until August 2028, to obtain a first logbook and a first
study. Many buildings will not have them yet when you visit: that is not a
fault, but it is one less piece of information. Where the study shows the fund
is insufficient, the board adjusts contributions, with a maximum of ten years to
reach the required level. In a newly built co-ownership, until the first study is
obtained, the amounts paid into the fund correspond to 0.5% of the building's
reconstruction value.

## The self-insurance fund and the syndicate's insurance

The contingency fund is not the only one. Since 15 April 2022, article 1071.1
has required the syndicate to establish a self-insurance fund, liquid and
available at short notice, also its property. It pays the deductibles under the
syndicate's policies and repairs property in which the syndicate has an
insurable interest when neither the insurance nor the contingency fund can cover
it. It is capitalized up to the highest deductible in the contract, excluding
any earthquake and flood deductibles. A high deductible is therefore a recurring
cost for everyone, not just a figure in a policy.

Article 1073 governs the insurance itself: the amount insured must allow
reconstruction in compliance with applicable standards and must be appraised at
least every five years by a member of a professional order designated by
regulation. Ask when the last appraisal was done.

## What you can demand before buying

- **The syndicate's attestation on the state of the co-ownership**, article
  1068.1. The seller must give it to you in due course, and the syndicate has 15
  days to produce it. The regulation fixes its content: the fund balance and the
  recommended amount, common expenses and results for the past three years,
  liquidity, the budget, insurance and self-insurance amounts, a five-year record
  of inspections, claims and major work, work planned over ten years, and
  pending litigation.
- **Documents and information**, article 1068.2. A promisor-buyer may ask the
  syndicate for what is needed to give informed consent, and the syndicate must
  provide it diligently, at the buyer's expense and subject to privacy rules.
- **A statement of common expenses owing**, article 1069. Whoever acquires a
  fraction is liable for the common expenses owing at the time of acquisition. A
  promisor-buyer may request that statement, and is liable only if the syndicate
  provides it within 15 days.
- **The syndicate's register**, article 1070: declaration of co-ownership,
  plans, contracts, maintenance logbook, contingency fund study, minutes,
  financial statements, and a description of the private portions precise enough
  to identify improvements made by co-owners. That description is what separates
  the syndicate's insurance from yours when a claim happens.

Your [notary](/en/articles/quebec-notary-process/) and your broker know what to
pull from all of this. It is the very subject of
[Coproprié-T](/en/advantages/coproprie-t/), the condominium law training RE/MAX
Québec developed with CondoLegal.com.

## Special assessments: who decides

Article 1072 leaves it to the board to fix, each year and after consulting the
general meeting, the contributions to common expenses, to the contingency fund
and to the self-insurance fund. Article 1072.1 extends the same logic to special
assessments: the board must consult the meeting before deciding on one.
Consulting is not the same as calling a vote, and the courts have confirmed it.

The remedy exists, but it is narrow. Article 1086.2 allows a co-owner to ask the
court to annul, or exceptionally to amend, a board decision that is biased, made
with intent to injure the co-owners or in contempt of their rights, within 90
days of the decision. Article 1103 offers the same remedy against a decision of
the general meeting. Neither one is there to reopen the wisdom of a decision.

## Low fees are not good news by themselves

This is the most common analytical error. Low charges can reflect a simple,
recent building with no heavy equipment. They can equally reflect an
undercapitalized fund, deferred maintenance, or a board avoiding an unpopular
increase. Only comparing the actual contribution with what the study recommends
settles it. Higher fees that properly finance a well-kept building buy a
predictability the cheaper neighbour does not offer. And documents that never
arrive, or arrive incomplete, are
[a signal worth taking seriously](/en/articles/red-flags-walk-away-property/).

## What your lender does with them

Condo fees count against your borrowing capacity. CMHC provides that 50% of
condominium fees are included in the gross debt service and total debt service
calculations, ratios it caps at 39% and 44%. Every fee increase therefore
shrinks what you can borrow, which is worth building in from
[pre-approval](/en/articles/guide-hypotheque-premier-acheteur-quebec/) onward.
