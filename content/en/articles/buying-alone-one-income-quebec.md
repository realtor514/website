A lender never asks whether you are buying alone. It asks for two ratios, applies a qualifying rate higher than the one in your contract, and calculates with the income on file. When there is only one income, none of those tests loosens. What changes are the levers around the calculation.

## One income, the same rules: GDS, TDS and the stress test

**Both ratios are identical.** For an insured loan, CMHC caps gross debt service at 39% of gross income and total debt service at 44%. GDS covers housing: payment, taxes, heating and a share of the condo fees. TDS adds your other debts on top.

Income sits in the denominator, debt in the numerator. Alone, every debt therefore weighs proportionally more: a $500 monthly car payment eats a far larger slice of TDS in a one-income file. I see financing blocked by one monthly payment too many more often than by an insufficient salary.

**The qualifying rate is not your rate.** Through guideline B-20, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions imposes a minimum qualifying rate on uninsured loans: the higher of the contract rate plus 2 percentage points or 5.25%. For an insured loan, the logic of the higher rate applies as well, and your lender has to confirm which rate it used.

The Bank of Canada policy rate has sat at 2.25% since the announcement of January 28, 2026, unchanged on September 2. A low policy rate still does not qualify you for more: the buffer and the floor hold anyway.

## The tiered down payment, and where insurance begins

CMHC's thresholds do not depend on the number of buyers, only on the price: 5% on the first $500,000 of the lending value, 10% on the portion above that, and no insured loan above $1,500,000.

Below 20% down, mortgage loan insurance is mandatory. The CMHC premium is 2.80% of the loan from 80.01% to 85% of value, 3.10% from 85.01% to 90%, and 4.00% from 90.01% to 95%. Amortization longer than 25 years adds 0.20 of a point.

**Two nuances matter for a solo buyer.** CMHC accepts a down payment from a non-traditional source, a personal loan or a line of credit for instance, for a 1 or 2 unit property in the 90.01% to 95% band, with a solid credit history. The premium then climbs to 4.50%, and the new payment enters your TDS.

The CMHC Premier chez-soi program (First Home) also allows 30 years of amortization when at least one borrower is a first-time buyer, or the property is new, under $1,500,000. In a one-person file, that single borrower is you.

## Cosigner or caution: what it changes, and the real risk

Two very different arrangements travel under the same word.

**The co-borrower.** Their income and their debts enter the calculation, and they become an owner alongside you. In Quebec, two unmarried people who buy together are in indivision, undivided co-ownership, a regime governed by a notarial agreement: see [buying property together and undivided co-ownership](/en/articles/buying-property-with-family/).

**The caution, suretyship.** Article 2333 of the Code civil du Québec is precise: suretyship is the contract by which a person binds himself toward the creditor to perform the debtor's obligation if the debtor fails to do so. Article 2352 is the one relatives read too late: a surety who binds himself with the qualification of solidary surety or solidary codebtor loses the benefits of discussion and division. The creditor therefore does not have to pursue you first, nor to divide the debt. Éducaloi adds the heaviest variant of all: a relative can pledge their own property as security, and on default the institution can demand payment from them, and even seize that property.

What a cosigner does not change: they do not remove the stress test, they do not reduce the insurance premium, they do not erase your debts. And they do not withdraw by email. Taking them off title later takes a new notarial deed and the lender's consent.

## CELIAPP and RAP when only one person is saving

The Canada Revenue Agency sets the amounts. CELIAPP (the FHSA) participation room is $8,000 for the year you open your first account, unused room carries forward, and the lifetime ceiling on contributions and transfers is $40,000. The RAP (the HBP) allows a maximum withdrawal of $60,000 from your RRSPs, repayable over 15 years.

The CRA requires that you not have lived, as a principal residence, in a property you owned during the current year or the four preceding calendar years, and that you occupy the property no later than one year after the purchase. The relief that pushes the start of RAP repayments to the fifth year covers only first withdrawals made between January 1, 2022 and December 31, 2025.

**The real issue is arithmetic.** A couple adds two CELIAPPs and two RAPs. Alone, your theoretical ceiling is $100,000, not $200,000. That gap is often the entire distance between a minimum down payment and 20% down. Opening the CELIAPP early therefore counts for more when you are on your own, since the room accumulates. The other measures are gathered in [first-time buyer tax benefits](/en/articles/first-time-buyer-tax-credits/).

## The owner-occupied plex: an exception, not a shortcut

The idea is appealing: a tenant pays part of the mortgage. CMHC's rules allow it, but narrowly. For 1 or 2 owner-occupied units, the loan can reach 95% of value, with the 5% and 10% tiers. For 3 or 4 units, the minimum down payment rises to 10% and the loan caps out at 90%. Occupancy by the borrower is a condition, not a preference.

**I cannot tell you what share of the rents your lender will retain.** CMHC applies different approaches to rental income for qualification purposes, depending on the type of occupancy and the insurance status, and refers the calculation back to the lender.

The price, for its part, cools the enthusiasm: the median plex in the Montreal CMA sold for $856,000 in August 2026, which means $60,600 of minimum down payment. In Laval there were only 21 plex sales in August, and APCIQ judges the number of transactions insufficient to produce a reliable median for the month; the cumulative figure for the first eight months gives $890,000. If the plex still interests you, start with [the income plex guide](/en/articles/income-property-beginners/).

## Buying alone in Quebec: what you trade, what actually helps

Let us be direct about what you give up. No second income absorbs a renewal shock five years out. Fixed costs do not divide: taxes, insurance, heating, condo fees and maintenance cost one person exactly what they cost two. And a six-month disability hits 100% of the household income, not 50%.

**The lender's insurance is not the only answer.** The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada is clear: you do not need optional mortgage insurance to get your loan, and the lender cannot impose it as a condition, which would amount to coercive tied selling. The FCAC also points out that in mortgage life insurance the beneficiary is the lender: the benefit is paid to the lender, not to your heirs. Compare your employer's coverage and an individual policy whose beneficiary you choose, with a life and health insurance representative.

What actually helps: paying off a debt with a monthly payment before you apply, opening the CELIAPP even if the purchase is three years away, and buying below your approved maximum. That last line is not decorative: it is the only margin you are going to have.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a bank lend less to a single person than to a couple with the same total income?**
The GDS and TDS ceilings are the same. What separates two files is the debt already in place and the stability of the income, not the number of borrowers.

**Can I use the CELIAPP and the RAP for the same purchase?**
Yes, they are two distinct CRA mechanisms: $40,000 of lifetime contributions in the CELIAPP, up to $60,000 withdrawn under the RAP. The first-time buyer conditions apply to both. Have the sequence validated by an accountant.

**What if I buy alone now and add someone to title later?**
That takes a new notarial deed and the lender's consent, and the indivision should be framed by an agreement. What follows on a breakup or a death is covered in [buying property together and undivided co-ownership](/en/articles/buying-property-with-family/).
