On television a flip takes three moves: buy low, renovate fast, sell high. No
one shows you the tax line, no one shows you the GST, no one shows you the
permit. Here is the complete calculation, with the tax rules and the rates
checked at the source for 2026.

I am a broker, not a tax advisor. This is general information: have your project
reviewed by an accountant before you sign anything.

## The 365-day rule changes everything

For dispositions after 2022, the federal government applies a residential
property flipping rule. The profit on a housing unit located in Canada, or on a
right to acquire one, that you owned for fewer than 365 consecutive days is
**deemed to be business income**. Not a capital gain.

Three consequences, all of them against you:

1. The profit is taxed **in full**, not half of it as with a capital gain.
2. **The principal residence exemption does not apply**, even if you lived in
   the house during the work.
3. A loss on a flipped property is **deemed to be nil**. You cannot deduct it.

The rule has exceptions, and they are life events, not strategies: death, a
person joining the household such as a birth, an adoption or an ageing parent,
a marriage or common-law breakdown with at least 90 days of separation, a threat
to personal safety, serious illness or disability, an eligible relocation for
work, involuntary loss of employment, insolvency, and destruction or
expropriation of the property. A job site that went wrong is not an exception.

Revenu Québec has harmonized: the same rule applies provincially since
January 1, 2023, with the same list of exceptions.

## Holding it 366 days does not protect you

This is the most expensive mistake I see. Past the 365-day mark the automatic
rule stops applying, but the Canada Revenue Agency then looks at **your
intention at the time of purchase**. Its position is plain: if you buy with the
main intention of reselling, the profit is business income, and the principal
residence exemption does not apply, because the intention was always to buy,
improve and sell. The 365 days are a floor, not a free pass.

## GST and QST: the major renovation trap

Reselling a previously occupied home is normally an exempt supply: no tax. But a
residential complex that has been **substantially renovated is given the same
treatment as new construction**. The sale becomes taxable, 5% GST and 9.975%
QST on the sale price.

The test is the 90% test: generally, 90% or more of the interior of the existing
building has to be removed or replaced. You do not have to replace the
foundation, the interior and exterior supporting walls, the roof, the floors or
the staircases to meet it. Only livable areas count, finished basements and
finished attics included, never the garage or the crawl space.

On a $720,000 resale, crossing that line adds close to $108,000 of tax to the
deal. You recover the tax paid on materials and labour through input tax credits
and refunds, but the gap stays enormous, and you have to register for the GST
and QST files. A buyer comparing your house "plus taxes" with an exempt resale
next door will take the other one. A lot of projects break exactly there. An
eligible first-time buyer can, however, recover the GST, not the QST: up to
$50,000, in full up to a $1,000,000 price, then on a sliding scale up to
$1,500,000.

## Transfer duties, paid on purchase and never recovered

The 2026 provincial brackets, indexed each year to Quebec's consumer price
index: 0.5% up to $62,900, 1% from $62,900 to $315,000, and 1.5% on the excess.
A municipality may set a higher rate above $500,000, capped at 3%, except
Montreal, which has broader powers.

What that means on a $720,000 property:

| City | 2026 bill |
|---|---|
| Montreal: 1.5% to $552,300, then 2% | $9,749 |
| Laval: 1.5% to $500,000, then 3% | $12,210.50 |

Above $552,300 Montreal keeps climbing in steps to 4%. Estimate your own case
with the [welcome tax calculator](/en/tools/welcome-tax/), and read [the full
welcome tax guide](/en/articles/welcome-tax-quebec-guide/).

## The RBQ licence, and the real price of cash work

In Quebec, anyone who carries out or has construction work carried out for
someone else must hold a contractor's licence from the Régie du bâtiment. The
owner-builder exemption is narrow: renovation, repair or maintenance of your own
building, for less than $20,000, and it does not cover modification work such as
moving a wall, nor electrical, gas or petroleum equipment work.

Working without a licence is an offence punishable by a fine. And an unlicensed
worker gives you no licence security, which is precisely the bond that
compensates a client who has suffered harm. Check every contractor in the RBQ
register, keep written contracts and invoices. With no invoices, you have no
expenses to deduct from the business income the tax authorities will assess.

## The full calculation, line by line

Illustrative figures, to be replaced by yours. The rates are the 2026 ones. A
Montreal house bought at $480,000 and resold at $720,000 after eight months.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | $480,000 |
| Transfer duties, Montreal rates | $5,310.50 |
| Notary on the purchase | $1,800 |
| Pre-purchase inspection | $800 |
| Work, $120,000 plus 20% contingency | $144,000 |
| Renovation permit, Montreal | $1,176 |
| 8 months of carrying: interest, taxes, insurance, power | $19,200 |
| Broker's remuneration at 5%, taxes included | $41,391 |
| Notary and location certificate on the sale | $1,500 |
| **Total** | **$695,177.50** |

Profit before tax: **$24,822.50**. Taxed as business income, at a 40% combined
marginal rate, about **$14,900** is left. For eight months of construction, and
already assuming a $240,000 gain that is anything but guaranteed.

The carrying line is the one people forget. It holds the interest on the loan
for every month you own the building, the municipal and school taxes, which keep
running whether anyone lives there or not, the insurance, which usually costs
more on a vacant building under renovation than on an occupied home, and the
electricity burned by the job site.

Three notes on that table. The permit follows the City of Montreal formula, cost
of work divided by 1,000, multiplied by $9.80, with a minimum of $167.40 for a
strictly residential building. The broker's remuneration is set by no law:
neither the Real Estate Brokerage Act nor the OACIQ imposes a rate, it is
negotiated. Notary fees are not tariffed either, the Chambre des notaires has no
fee schedule: they depend on the time, the complexity and the expertise, and the
notary must tell you the approximate amount in advance. Ask for a written
estimate, expect roughly $1,500 to $3,500. The rest of the costs are in the
[closing costs calculator](/en/tools/closing-costs/) and in [the real cost of
selling a home](/en/articles/real-cost-selling-home-quebec/).

## What makes a flip fail

**Going over budget.** An open wall reveals aluminum wiring, vermiculite, a
collapsed drain. A 20% contingency is not pessimism, it is the minimum.

**The time it takes to resell.** In August 2026, in the Montreal metropolitan
area, APCIQ measured an average selling time of 42 days for single-family homes,
52 days for plexes and 62 days for condominiums, up 12 days year over year for
that last category. Add the delay to the notarized deed: every extra month eats
the margin and moves you dangerously close to the 365-day line.

**A market that slows down.** Your margin depends on a resale price you do not
control. And remember that [not every renovation creates
value](/en/articles/renovations-dont-add-value/): a high-end kitchen in an
entry-level sector does not come back.

## My take as a broker

Flipping is a business, not an investment. The tax authorities say so before I
