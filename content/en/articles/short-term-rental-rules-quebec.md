When you buy a property to rent out by the night, the profitability math comes
last. What decides the project is three authorizations that do not substitute
for one another: the provincial registration, the municipal zoning and, in a
condo building, the declaration. Miss one and the building cannot be rented
legally, even if the other two are in order.

General information, not legal or tax advice. The rules have moved fast since
2023 and several measures came into force in 2026: validate your case with
your notary, your lawyer and the city concerned.

## The registration number: the step nothing replaces

The Loi sur l'hébergement touristique (Quebec's tourist accommodation act)
defines a tourist accommodation establishment as a place where at least one
unit, a bed, a room, an apartment, a house or a cottage, is offered for rent to
tourists for payment for a period not exceeding 31 days. Article 4 is blunt:
operating such an establishment is subject to registration with the minister.
In practice, the number is issued by the Corporation de l'industrie touristique
du Québec (CITQ), Quebec's tourist industry corporation, and it is a six digit
number.

Three details buyers discover too late. Article 5 requires the application to
include a document from a competent authority showing that the operation does
not contravene the urban planning by-law on permitted uses. Article 20 imposes
an annual renewal. And article 7 fits in eight words: no one may transfer the
registration. The seller's number does not follow you to the deed of sale.

The CITQ also requires proof of civil liability insurance of at least
$2,000,000 per claim, the owner's authorization if you are a tenant, and the
syndicate's authorization if the building is held in divided co-ownership.

## Municipal zoning decides whether short term rental is allowed

This is the layer people forget. The registration number creates no right of
use: it assumes the city already allows it.

Article 23 of the act protects one case only, the principal residence
establishment, meaning accommodation offered through a single reservation in
the principal residence of the individual who operates it, to one person or one
group of related people at a time, with no meals served on site. No urban
planning by-law can prohibit that use. With one large exception: a municipality
can still prohibit it through a zoning or conditional uses by-law adopted under
the referendum procedure, with the number of required signatures cut by 50%.

In practice: outside your principal residence, renting to tourists is an
ordinary commercial use. It is allowed where the zoning by-law allows it, and
nowhere else.

## Montreal: summer for your principal residence, four boroughs for the rest

Montreal used the exception. The city allows you to rent your principal
residence to tourists from June 10 to September 10 only, for a maximum of 31
days within that window. Outside it, the rental is prohibited. And it is
prohibited all year in Lachine, Saint-Laurent and Saint-Léonard.

You need an operating permit, renewable every year, with review fees of $350
plus taxes. The permit must be printed and posted so it is visible from the
street. The city also requires a valid CITQ certificate, the owner's
authorization if you are a tenant, and provisions in the declaration of
co-ownership that allow the operation.

**A commercial tourist residence, the one that is not your principal residence,
is an entirely different file.** Montreal allows it in four boroughs only, and
only on street segments named one by one: Côte-des-Neiges and
Notre-Dame-de-Grâce, Le Plateau-Mont-Royal on rue Saint-Denis between Sherbrooke
Est and Gilford, Le Sud-Ouest, and Ville-Marie on rue Sainte-Catherine between
Saint-Mathieu and Atateken. In that last sector, at least 150 metres must
separate two tourist residences. You need a certificate of occupancy, at a cost
of $907, plus $63 for the review of the CITQ compliance notice.

Put those fees in the same table as the welcome tax and the notary's fees, with
the [closing costs calculator](/en/tools/closing-costs/), before you decide
whether the project stands up.

## Laval: the answer is zone by zone in the Code de l'urbanisme

Laval has no stand alone Airbnb by-law comparable to Montreal's. The answer
sits in the Code de l'urbanisme (the city's planning code), and it is precise.
Schedule C defines a tourist residence as a tourist accommodation establishment
where at least one unit, in a dwelling, a house or a cottage, is offered for
rent to tourists for a period not exceeding 31 days. Schedule D gives it use
code 5834 and classifies it in the category "Commerce à incidence (C4)", impact
commerce. An inn or tourist home, code 5833, falls under the subcategory
"Commerce d'hébergement (C2d)", accommodation commerce.

An impact commercial use is not permitted in an ordinary residential zone.
Before buying in Laval to rent short term, verify the exact zone of the address
and the uses permitted there, with the city's Info-règlements tool (its online
by-law lookup) or at the permits counter. I found no Laval municipal page that
summarizes these rules for the general public, which makes the address by
address check all the more necessary. The same reflex applies to [any
investment project in Laval](/en/articles/laval-investment-city/).

## In a condo, the declaration can stop everything

No provincial or municipal permit overrides your declaration of co-ownership
(déclaration de copropriété). Article 1063 of the Code civil requires each
co-owner to comply with the building's by-laws and not to impair the
destination of the building. Article 1056 allows the declaration to impose
restrictions justified by that destination. Many residential building
declarations therefore prohibit rentals of less than 30, 60 or 90 days, in so
many words.

Changing the destination of the building is no formality: article 1098 requires
a three quarters majority of the co-owners representing 90% of the votes. Read
the declaration and the by-laws before the offer, not after, as with the rest
of the [condo file](/en/articles/condo-fees-investment-killer/).

If you are a tenant rather than an owner, the government is clear: you need the
owner's consent. Without it, the owner can ask the Tribunal administratif du
logement (Quebec's rental board) to stop the rental, to award compensation, or
to terminate the lease.

## The fines, and who enforces them

Revenu Québec is responsible for inspections and investigations tied to
enforcing the act. Platforms are targeted too: article 20.1 prohibits them from
publishing an offer without a registration number and a certificate expiry
date, and article 20.2 requires them to verify that the number matches the
establishment and that the registration is in force.

| Offence | Individual | Other cases |
|---|---|---|
| Operating without registration | $2,500 to $25,000 | $5,000 to $50,000 |
| Operating with a refused or suspended registration | $5,000 to $50,000 | $10,000 to $100,000 |
| Failing to show the number in a listing | $1,000 to $10,000 | $2,000 to $20,000 |
| Platform: publishing without a number or verifying poorly | $5,000 to $50,000 | $10,000 to $100,000 |

Two recent tightenings. Since April 9, 2026, the number and the name of the
establishment must appear distinctly in all advertising, including on social
media, and the certificate must be posted at the main entrance. Since September
1, 2026, you must provide two proofs of principal residence at registration and
at renewal, and operators who are tenants or in divided co-ownership must
provide their owner's or their syndicate's authorization every year, on
standardized forms.

## The checks to run before buying for short term rental

In this order, before you lift your conditions:

1. **The zone.** Get the zone of the address and the list of permitted uses
   from the city in writing. In Laval, the use in question is 5834. In
   Montreal, ask for the borough and the exact street segment.
2. **The declaration of co-ownership and the building by-laws.** Look for any
   minimum rental term and any clause on commercial use.
3. **The seller's real status.** The public register of tourist accommodation
   establishments shows the number, the status and the expiry date. An active
   number held by the seller does not transfer.
4. **The municipal fees.** Permit, certificate of occupancy, annual renewals.
5. **The insurance.** A standard home policy does not cover tourist use. Get a
   quote before the offer, with the civil liability the CITQ requires.
6. **The tax side.** Rental income, lodging tax, GST and QST, the effect on the
   principal residence exemption: that is a conversation with an accountant,
   not a checkbox.

And a reminder that applies to this whole text: these rules change, sometimes
from one season to the next. Confirm the municipal by-law in force for your
exact address, on the day you buy.

## Frequently asked questions

**I have my CITQ number, can the city still stop me?**
Yes. The number assumes the use complies with zoning, it does not authorize it.
Article 5 of the act even requires a municipal document to that effect at the
time of the application.

**The seller already rents on a platform. Do I inherit that right?**
No. Article 7 prohibits transferring the registration. You must file your own
application, and nothing guarantees it will be accepted if the situation has
changed since theirs.

**Can I rent my Plateau condo for a week in February?**
No, if it is your principal residence: Montreal only allows that rental from
June 10 to September 10. And if your declaration of co-ownership prohibits it,
the answer is no even in July.

**My syndicate agreed verbally, is that enough?**
No. The CITQ requires the syndicate's authorization on a form, and since
September 1, 2026 it must be provided every year at renewal. A verbal
understanding does not survive a change of board.

**What if I rent long term instead?**
Beyond 31 days, you leave the Loi sur l'hébergement touristique and enter the
residential lease regime, with its own rules. That is the framework described
in the [income plex guide](/en/articles/income-property-beginners/).
