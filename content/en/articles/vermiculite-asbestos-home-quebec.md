Grey-brown granules with a faint sheen, poured loose between the attic joists:
that is often all a buyer sees through the access hatch. The material has a name,
vermiculite, and a question attached to it: does it contain amiante (asbestos)?
Nobody can answer by looking, and that is exactly where decisions go wrong.

## Vermiculite and amiante in a Quebec house: the material and the years

The Quebec government describes vermiculite as an insulation "that comes in the
form of granules whose colour ranges from silver-gold to grey-brown", once used
"to insulate the attics (attics, attics with an access hatch, mansards, etc.) of
houses". Then comes the sentence that counts: "Certain vermiculite insulations
sold between 1920 and 1990 may contain amiante fibres."

The origin is geological. Santé Canada (Health Canada) traces the source to
"vermiculite from the Libby mine in Montana (U.S.) from the 1920s until 1990",
sold "notably under the Zonolite name". The deposit also contained amiante, and
the Government of Canada names the form involved: tremolite.

Two markers frame the question here. The construction industry used these
materials "mainly between the 1930s and 1990", so they can turn up in residential
buildings "built or renovated in that period". And the federal ban on selling,
importing and using amiante only dates from December 30, 2018. A 1962 house and a
1988 house therefore raise the same question. The CNESST adds the detail that
ruins any visual diagnosis: "The presence of amiante is rarely indicated on a
material that contains it."

## Only the analysis of a sample settles it, never a glance into the attic

Quebec regulation reverses the burden. The Règlement sur la santé et la sécurité
du travail (RSST, Quebec's occupational health and safety regulation) presumes
that materials likely to contain amiante do contain it, and there are only two
ways to show otherwise (section 69.4, extended to materials by section 69.12):
verifiable documentary information, such as a technical data sheet, or a sampling
report covering a sufficient number of representative samples.

The threshold is a number: a material contains amiante "when the amiante
concentration is at least 0.1%" (section 69.2). The analysis follows a method
from the IRSST's Guide d'échantillonnage des contaminants de l'air en milieu de
travail (its workplace air contaminant sampling guide), and the laboratory "must
take part in an interlaboratory quality control program" (section 69.5). The
report has to name the person responsible and their qualification, locate each
sample, state the method and identify the laboratory (section 69.7). An email
saying "it was tested" is not a report.

Who takes the sample? The Quebec government asks that the check be done by a
qualified contractor, "who will confirm the presence of amiante and determine
whether certain materials need to be analyzed", or by a specialized laboratory,
because the visual inspection "is not simple to do yourself". If in doubt, treat
the material as though it contains amiante.

## Undisturbed, or disturbed: two situations the law does not treat alike

It is the fibres in the air that create the risk. The Quebec government writes
that "materials that contain amiante and are in good condition do not release
fibres into the air and are therefore generally not dangerous". Santé Canada adds
that "the best way to reduce the risk of exposure to amiante is to avoid
disturbing the insulation".

Conversely, a friable material or one in poor condition releases fibres, and
sawing, drilling, cutting, sanding or breaking it releases them too. The diagram
of household amiante sources published by the Quebec government classes
vermiculite among the "blown, friable, loose fill insulation".

For a suspect attic, the official measures are concrete: limit access, inform
anyone who has to go up there, store nothing in it, seal the cracks between the
ceiling and the attic, caulk the hatch and the perimeter of light fixtures, and
"never try to remove the insulation yourself". For your own situation, public
health authorities and a qualified professional are the ones who answer.

## What the law requires of a contractor before the work

Before work likely to generate dust, the employer "must check for the presence of
amiante in the materials and products likely to contain it" (section 69.11 of the
RSST) and, on a construction site, "determine the types of amiante present in the
materials" (section 3.23.3 of the Code de sécurité pour les travaux de
construction, the construction safety code). Removing friable materials
containing amiante is classed as high risk, with two lighter regimes depending on
the volume of debris.

| Volume of friable debris | Regime under the Code | Some requirements |
|---|---|---|
| 0.03 m³ or less | Moderate risk | Sealed ventilated enclosure, 4 air changes per hour, respiratory protection |
| More than 0.03 m³ up to 0.3 m³ | Section 3.23.16.1 | Sealed ventilated enclosure, disposable coveralls, decontamination before each exit |
| More than 0.3 m³ | High risk | Negative pressure of 1 to 4 Pa, double changing room with shower, one air sample per shift analyzed within 24 h, dismantling prohibited before 0.01 fibre/cm³ |

Two obligations change a project's calendar. The principal contractor must send
the CNESST a written notice of opening of the site at least 10 days before
activities begin, and for an amiante removal that notice must state "the methods
and processes used, along with an attestation that a training and information
program exists" (section 2.4.1). The employer must also keep a safe amiante
management register (section 69.16). The CNESST applies zero tolerance: "Should
any of these rules be breached, the CNESST will halt the work and those at fault
will be liable to penal proceedings."

Keep the scope of these sections in mind: they target the employer and the
principal contractor, not the owner tinkering on his own at home. The official
instruction to a private individual remains not to do this work yourself.

## Remove, enclose or leave: the effect on a renovation project

When a material is likely to emit amiante dust, the options set out are to remove
it, to enclose it entirely in a permanent structure that is tight to fibres, to
coat or impregnate it with a binder, or to cover it with a tight material
(section 69.9). One rule is not negotiable: before a demolition, these materials
must be removed (section 3.23.3.2).

Translate that into a project. Adding insulation on top, finishing a room in the
attic, installing recessed light fixtures, running wires: each of those touches
the attic. A project that does not touch it is a project whose budget does not
move.

**The cost.** No official source consulted lets me put a figure on a vermiculite
removal in Quebec, so I will not give you an amount. What the sources do let you
demand, yes: a written description of the work stating the services, the costs,
the timelines, the insurance and the warranties. And this, which comes as a
surprise: "in Quebec, there is no official accreditation or certification program
for the professionals who can work on sites containing amiante". Failing that,
check the contractor's licence in the Registre des détenteurs de licence de la
Régie du bâtiment (the RBQ licence holders register). That budget comes on top of
the costs already planned at purchase, which the [closing costs
calculator](/en/tools/closing-costs/) works out for you.

## What the seller must declare about vermiculite

The mandatory Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble (the seller's declarations
form) names vermiculite twice, at D7.4 and at D8.5, and an amiante expert
assessment gets declared at D13.2. The form is taken apart in [the seller's
declaration in Quebec](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/).

One detail goes unnoticed. D8.5, on the type of insulation in the attic, offers a
"do not know" box. D7.4, which asks whether materials that may contain amiante
have ever been used, does not: there is "yes" and there is "no". A seller who has
never had his attic analyzed is therefore answering a question of fact he does
not know. The defensible answer goes through section D15.

On the buyer's side, the issue is the warranty. Éducaloi points out that a defect
is latent if it is not apparent, if the buyer did not know about it and if it
existed at the time of the purchase, and that the examination expected of a buyer
"must be serious, attentive and prudent". Visible, declared vermiculite is
therefore not a latent defect, and declared but never analyzed vermiculite is no
guarantee that all is well. The recourses are detailed in [vice caché in
Quebec](/en/articles/latent-defect-recourse-quebec/).

## Vermiculite and amiante: the promise to purchase, the lender, the insurer

The OACIQ is clear on the broker's role: the form helps him meet his duty to
verify, he must disclose any doubt about a factor that could affect the
transaction, and he can advise adding conditions to the promesse d'achat (promise
to purchase). Vermiculite calls for the same reflex: an expert assessment
condition, a realistic period to take the sample, run the analysis and receive a
written report, and a clear outcome if the result comes back positive.

One honest clarification to close. No official source consulted lets me claim
that a Quebec lender automatically refuses to finance a house insulated with
vermiculite, or that an insurer refuses to cover it. What is verifiable is that
proof of insurance remains the item that derails a transaction on the last day,
for the reasons listed in [hard to insure home in
Quebec](/en/articles/hard-to-insure-home-quebec/). Call your insurer with the
address and the year of construction before you draft.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I take a sample myself and send it to the laboratory?**
The Quebec government asks that the check be done by a qualified contractor or by
a specialized laboratory, and writes that you should never carry out the work
yourself. None of the sources consulted recommends sampling by the owner.

**Does all vermiculite contain amiante?**
No. The Quebec government writes that "certain" insulations sold between 1920 and
1990 may contain it. But the regulation presumes amiante is present until a
technical data sheet or an analysis report shows otherwise, with a threshold of
0.1%.

**The seller ticked "no" at D7.4. Is that settled?**
It tells you what the seller believes he knows, not what the insulation contains.
Ask whether an analysis has ever been done, which is what D13.2 covers, then ask
for the report. A "no" with no report is not a laboratory result.
