Plenty of owners believe the deadline went by in the fall of 2025. It did not.
The official text of the Règlement sur la sécurité des piscines résidentielles
(Quebec's residential pool safety regulation), current to 1 May 2026, sets 30
September 2027. And that date does not follow the owner: it follows the pool.
Here are the standards, the permit, and what gets verified before you sign.

## What the Quebec residential pool regulation covers

Section 1 defines a pool as an outdoor artificial basin, permanent or temporary,
intended for swimming, with a water depth of 60 cm or more. A hot tub and a
thermal bath whose capacity does not exceed 2,000 litres are excluded. Three
forms are targeted: the in-ground or semi in-ground pool, the above ground pool
with a rigid wall, and the demountable pool with a flexible wall, inflatable or
not. A wide inflatable pool that reaches 60 cm of water is therefore a pool
within the meaning of the regulation.

**The word that decides everything is "installation."** The regulation covers
the pool and any equipment, construction, system and accessory intended to
ensure its proper operation, to ensure the safety of persons, or to give or
prevent access. The filter, the platform, the ladder and the fence are all part
of it.

## The enclosure, the gate and the one metre clearance

Section 3 states the rule: subject to section 6, every pool must be surrounded
by an enclosure so as to protect access to it. Section 4 describes it. It must
prevent the passage of a spherical object 10 cm in diameter, stand at least 1.2
m high, and be free of any fixing element, projection or openwork part that
could make it easier to climb. The last sentence of the section often takes
people by surprise: a hedge or shrubs cannot constitute an enclosure.

**The gate is the element most often out of compliance.** Section 5 requires it
to have the characteristics of the enclosure and to be fitted with a passive
safety device allowing it to close and lock automatically, either on the inside
in the upper part of the gate, or on the outside at least 1.5 m from the ground.
Laval spells out the mechanics: the gate closes with a spring and the latch
engages on its own.

Section 7 adds the one metre rule. Any apparatus connected to the operation of
the pool must be installed more than one metre from the wall or the enclosure,
its conduits must be flexible, and any fixed structure or equipment that could
be used to climb over respects the same distance, including a window located
less than 3 m from the ground. Section 8 requires all of it to stay in good
working order, and section 8.1 imposes the BNQ 9461-100 standard on any pool
fitted with a diving board.

## Above ground or demountable: the wall, the ladder and the safety gate

Section 6 is the only exemption from the enclosure, and it is conditional. An
above ground pool whose wall measures at least 1.2 m at every point, or a
demountable pool whose wall reaches 1.4 m or more, escapes the enclosure if
access is provided in one of these three ways: a ladder fitted with a safety
gate that closes and locks automatically so a child cannot use it; a ladder or a
platform protected by a compliant enclosure; a deck attached to the residence
whose part opening onto the pool is protected by a compliant enclosure.

**Pulling the ladder away at night is not one of the three options.** The
regulation asks for a device that acts on its own, not for a habit.

| Type of pool | What controls access |
|---|---|
| In-ground or semi in-ground | 1.2 m enclosure, no exception |
| Above ground under 1.2 m | 1.2 m enclosure |
| Above ground 1.2 m and over | Enclosure, or one of the section 6 options |
| Demountable under 1.4 m | 1.2 m enclosure |
| Demountable 1.4 m and over | Enclosure, or one of the section 6 options |

Laval adds a detail that costs money when you learn it late: if a new pool sits
next to a balcony already in place, the existing guardrail has to be modified to
reach 1.2 m.

## The residential pool regulation deadline: 30 September 2027

Section 10 organizes the application. The regulation applies to any new
installation put in place as of 1 July 2021, and also to any installation
existing before that date, with three exceptions: the second paragraph of
section 4, the fourth paragraph of section 7 and section 8.1. Then comes the
sentence that decides everything: an installation existing before 1 November
2010 must comply with the applicable provisions no later than 30 September 2027.

That date has moved more than once: section 10 was amended by decrees 662-2021,
1372-2022 and 120-2026. The OACIQ confirms 30 September 2027 in its fact sheet
updated on 24 February 2026, and Montreal and Laval both publish the same date.
The Société de sauvetage (Quebec's lifesaving society) asked for it to be pulled
back to 1 June 2026, without success. Its report counts 61 drowning deaths in
Quebec in 2025, including 5 among children aged 0 to 5, against 7 in 2024.

**That is why this belongs in the transaction file and not in a summer project
list.** The obligation is attached to the installation, not to the person.
Whoever owns the property on 30 September 2027 is the one who will owe the work.
A buyer signing today is therefore buying a dated obligation, with a cost and a
timeline attached. That gets discussed at the offer, not afterward. And
replacing the pool makes the three excepted provisions applicable to the
existing installation.

## The municipal pool permit, in Montreal and in Laval

Section 9 is blunt: a permit from the local municipality is required to build,
install or replace a pool, to install a diving board, or to erect a
construction giving or preventing access. Bringing the fence up to standard
therefore needs one too.

In Montreal, the fee is the borough's. Ahuntsic-Cartierville asks $9.80 per
$1,000 bracket of work, minimum $167.40. Anjou charges $150 plus taxes.
Côte-des-Neiges-Notre-Dame-de-Grâce asks $465, non-refundable. The documents are
the same everywhere: certificate of location, yard layout plan, contractor's
quote, technical data sheet. Separate permits are added for an access platform,
to modify a balcony that opens onto the pool, and to [cut down a
tree](/en/articles/trees-property-rules-quebec/) in the installation area.

In Laval, the certificate of authorization costs $84 and the posted processing
time is 20 days. It covers in-ground, semi in-ground and above ground pools, the
initial installation of a demountable one, and any spa over 2,000 litres. The
permit to bring the enclosure up to standard costs the same. The certificate of
location has to come from an arpenteur géomètre (land surveyor), and the City
asks for photos of the backyard, the balcony and the rear wall showing the doors
and the windows. By-laws L-280 and L-11807 have been replaced by transitional
by-law L-12907. For the rest of the Laval framework, see the page on the [real
estate broker in Laval](/en/real-estate-broker/laval/).

## Fine, municipal inspection, civil liability and insurance

Section 11 sets the penalty: an owner who contravenes the regulation is liable
to a fine of not less than $500 and not more than $700, raised to $700 and
$1,000 for a repeat offence. The OACIQ points out that each municipality may
adopt stricter sanctions.

The municipal inspection is the moment the file becomes written. In Laval, the
last step of the permit is the inspector's visit, which assesses whether the
work complies. In Montreal, the borough of L'Île-Bizard-Sainte-Geneviève offers
a free inspection and advice service and issues a compliance certificate. A
notice of non-compliance, on the other hand, becomes a document to declare: the
OACIQ specifies that the practice of declaring any notice of non-compliance
liable to generate expenses remains unchanged. The [DV 00001
form](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/) is where that plays out.

Article 1457 of the Code civil du Québec requires every person to abide by the
rules of conduct incumbent on him so as not to cause injury to another, and adds
that a person is also bound, in certain cases, to make reparation for injury
caused by the act of a thing in his custody.

The Insurance Bureau of Canada asks you to contact your insurance
representative before installing a pool or a spa, and indicates that the home
policy covers the holder's civil liability, extended to the spouse and the
children. The amounts are in your contract, not in an article. No official
source lets me state that an insurer automatically refuses to indemnify a claim
because the enclosure was not compliant: put the question in writing. [Hard to
insure homes](/en/articles/hard-to-insure-home-quebec/) are a reminder that the
answer varies from one insurer to the next.

## Buyer and seller: what gets verified before signing

The seller starts with the paperwork: the installation permit, the enclosure
permit if there was one, and any notice received from the City or from the
insurer. The ministère des Affaires municipales et de l'Habitation publishes
self-assessment forms, one per type of pool, which Laval and Montreal both point
to. Fill out yours, then get a priced quote. A known amount gets negotiated; a
doubt gets deducted.

The buyer verifies on site. Measure the height of the enclosure. Let go of the
gate and watch whether it closes and locks on its own. Measure the distance
between the enclosure and the filter, the shed, the fixed furniture. Count the
hedge for zero. Then ask the municipality for the permits, because the
municipality is what judges compliance, not the seller. The [list of signals you
can verify on paper](/en/articles/red-flags-walk-away-property/) rounds out the
visit, and the effect on the promise to purchase gets discussed with your broker
and your notary.

## Frequently asked questions

**My pool dates from 1998. Do I have acquired rights?**
No, not permanently. An installation existing before 1 November 2010 must comply
no later than 30 September 2027.

**Does the seller have to bring the pool up to standard before selling?**
The regulation sets a date, not a moment inside the transaction. In practice the
question gets settled at the offer: work done before signing, a price
adjustment, or the obligation taken on by the buyer. Have whichever solution you
choose put in writing.

**Is an inflatable pool for the kids covered?**
If the water depth reaches 60 cm or more, yes. Laval even requires a permit for
the initial installation of a demountable pool.

**How much does bringing a pool up to standard cost?**
No official source lets me put a number on the average cost of that work in
Quebec. Compare two or three written quotes.
