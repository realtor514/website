In many families, homeownership is not an investment category. It is security:
a home nobody can push you out of, and a cost that does not climb with every
renewal letter. That reading is legitimate, but a conviction does not pay a
down payment.

So here is the inventory, measure by measure, of what actually exists for a
first-time buyer in Quebec in 2026, each one answering two questions: who is
it for, and what does it not solve. I am a broker, not a tax specialist: have
your plan reviewed by an accountant.

Let us be clear from the start: none of these measures makes a home affordable
on its own. Some reduce tax, some speed up the down payment, some refund part
of the costs. What matters is knowing which ones apply to your file, and in
what order.

## The FHSA: the account to open first

The First Home Savings Account lets you contribute $8,000 a year, up to
$40,000 over a lifetime. Unused room carries forward, but never more than
$8,000, which allows at most $16,000 in a single year. Contributions are
deductible, a qualifying withdrawal is not taxable and never has to be repaid.
The account lasts at most 15 years after it is opened.

**Who it is for:** anyone with any saving capacity at all.

**What it does not solve:** room only starts accumulating once the account is
open. Opening early with $100 beats waiting until you have $8,000. And the
FHSA does not create money: it shelters the money you set aside.

## The HBP: a loan you make to yourself

The Home Buyers' Plan lets you withdraw up to $60,000 from your RRSPs tax
free, per person. Repayment is spread over 15 years. For a first withdrawal
made between January 1, 2026 and December 31, 2028, the start of that 15-year
period is deferred to the fifth year following the withdrawal instead of the
second.

**Who it is for:** someone who already has a well-funded RRSP, often through
an employer plan.

**What it does not solve:** this is not a grant. Every missed repayment is
added to your taxable income for that year, and the money withdrawn stops
compounding tax sheltered. The FHSA never has to be repaid, which is why I
fill it first.

The two combine for the same home. An eligible couple can therefore assemble
up to $200,000: $40,000 of FHSA and $60,000 of HBP each, plus the growth
accumulated inside the FHSA. That is the theoretical ceiling and rarely the
real number, but it is worth knowing where the ceiling is before deciding the
project is impossible.

## The two credits in the year of purchase

**Federal (line 31270):** $10,000 at the lowest tax rate, which is 14 % in
2026, so $1,400 (about $1,169 net in Quebec, because of the Quebec
abatement).

**Quebec (line 396):** $10,000 at 14 %, so $1,400 as well.

**Who they are for:** any first-time buyer who owes tax.

**What they do not solve:** both are non refundable. They reduce tax payable
and nothing more: with no tax owing, they are worth nothing. And they arrive
the following spring, not on signing day.

## Quebec's refundable home access tax credit

This is the new one in 2026, and the only measure on this list that pays you
even if you owe no tax. It refunds up to $5,875 of the transfer duty: 100 % of
the first $5,000 and 25 % of the next $3,500 band. The credit is reduced by
2.35 % of the amount by which the home's value exceeds $750,000, and
disappears at $1,000,000. Advance payments are planned.

**Who it is for:** first-time buyers, and people acquiring a more accessible
home because of a severe disability.

**What it does not solve:** the municipality still sends its bill, and you pay
it first. Estimate it with the [welcome tax
calculator](/en/tools/welcome-tax/) and read [how the welcome tax is
calculated](/en/articles/welcome-tax-quebec-guide/).

## The first-time buyers' GST rebate, on new homes

Up to 100 % of the GST, capped at $50,000, on a new home of $1,000,000 or
less, then a declining amount up to $1.5 million. You must be a first-time
buyer, a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, the first occupant, and make
it your principal residence. With a builder, the agreement must have been
entered into on or after March 20, 2025 and before 2031, construction must
begin before 2031 and be substantially completed before 2036.

**Who it is for:** the buyer of a new condo or house. See our [new
construction guide](/en/articles/new-construction-guide-quebec/).

**What it does not solve:** it does not touch the QST, and it does not apply
to resales, meaning the vast majority of Greater Montreal transactions.

## The financing rules, often the real obstacle

The minimum down payment is 5 % up to $500,000, then 5 % on the first $500,000
and 10 % on the portion between $500,000 and $1.5 million. From $1.5 million
up, it is 20 %. Below 20 %, mortgage loan insurance becomes mandatory: the
CMHC premium runs from 2.80 % to 4.00 % of the loan amount depending on the
loan-to-value ratio (4.50 % with a non-traditional down payment), and in
Quebec provincial sales tax is added on top and cannot be rolled into the loan.

Since December 15, 2024, 30-year amortization is available to all first-time
buyers as well as to buyers of newly built homes, and the ceiling for insured
mortgages rose from $1 million to $1.5 million.

**What it does not solve:** a longer amortization lowers the monthly payment
and raises the total interest paid. CMHC also adds 0.20 % to the premium for
an amortization longer than 25 years. See also [the 20 % down payment
myth](/en/articles/down-payment-myth-debunked/).

## What none of these measures solve

None of them touch your borrowing capacity. That depends on your income, your
debts and your [credit file](/en/articles/credit-score-home-buying-quebec/). A
file at 650 usually needs a year of targeted work, not five years of
resignation. Run the numbers with the [affordability
calculator](/en/tools/affordability/) and our article on [the salary needed to
buy in Montreal](/en/articles/what-salary-to-buy-montreal/).

None of them cover the cash you need on closing day either. The welcome tax
credit is refunded later, the two purchase credits arrive with your tax
return, and the notary, the inspection and the adjustments are all paid in the
weeks around the signature. A plan that leaves nothing for those weeks is not
a plan, however well the down payment is assembled.

And one barrier no program funds: language. Quebec's notarial system, OACIQ
oversight, mortgage insurance rules and the mechanics of civil law are
genuinely unfamiliar territory for a newcomer, however educated. Being able to
handle these subjects in your own language is not a comfort: it is what makes
it possible to ask the questions you would not otherwise dare to ask. Our
[newcomers'
guide](/en/articles/guide-achat-immobilier-nouveaux-arrivants-quebec/) takes
the path from the start.

## When the gap is still too wide

Sometimes all of these measures added together are not enough. Three levers
remain, and none of them is a government program.

The first is geography: the same budget does not buy the same thing in
Montreal, Laval, Terrebonne or on the South Shore. The difference between
"impossible" and "not yet" is rarely obvious when you look at your own file
alone.

The second is the type of building: a duplex or triplex where you occupy one
unit brings rental income into the lender's calculation, which sometimes
changes the whole equation.

The third is buying with others, a parent, a sibling or a friend. It works,
but only properly structured: see [buying property together in
Quebec](/en/articles/buying-property-with-family/), because the legal setup
matters as much as the financing.

## The order I would take these in

The FHSA first, because it never has to be repaid. The HBP next, if the RRSP
already exists. Then
[pre-approval](/en/articles/guide-hypotheque-premier-acheteur-quebec/) to
learn the real number, before looking at properties. The tax credits are
claimed afterward, and the full budget is built with [the true cost of a first
purchase](/en/articles/vrai-cout-achat-premiere-maison-quebec/).
