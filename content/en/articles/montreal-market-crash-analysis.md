The question comes back the moment a headline mentions a slowdown: is the
market going to crash? I am not going to answer that, because nobody can. What
I can do, and what is more useful, is show which numbers exist, what they
actually measure, and where they stop letting you conclude anything.

Everything below comes from public sources, dated and named. You will find no
price prediction here, no advice about timing, and no scenario.

## A Word With No Statistical Meaning

Crash has no official definition. APCIQ publishes no crash threshold. Neither
does Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation, and neither does Statistics
Canada. The word circulates in the media, not in the tables.

What does exist in the tables is four distinct measures: number of sales,
number of active listings, median price by property category, and average days
on market. None of them is the market on its own, and they do not move
together.

## A Sales Slowdown and a Price Decline Are Two Different Things

This is the most common confusion, and the August 2026 data takes it apart in
one stroke.

In the Montreal census metropolitan area, APCIQ recorded 2,853 sales in August
2026, which is 13% fewer than in August 2025. In that same month, active
listings reached 20,128, up 18% year over year.

And median prices, in that same month, were all higher than a year earlier:
$650,000 for single family homes, up 3%, $437,250 for condominiums, up 4%, and
$856,000 for plexes, up 2%. Those figures come from the monthly statistics
APCIQ publishes through the Centris system, released on 4 September 2026.

Fewer transactions, considerably more choice, and median prices that are not
falling. Sales and prices are two different series. A decline in one does not
mechanically show up in the other, not in the same month and not in the same
direction.

## The Indicators You Can Check Yourself

You do not need me to follow a market. You need the right numbers at the right
geographic level.

**Average days on market.** In August 2026, in the Montreal CMA, it stood at
42 days for single family homes, 62 days for condominiums and 52 days for
plexes. The year over year change says more than the level: up 1 day for single
family homes, up 12 days for condominiums, down 4 days for plexes. Three
categories, three directions. I break that measure down in [how long it
actually takes to sell in
Montreal](/en/articles/how-long-to-sell-house-montreal/).

**Active listings.** This is available supply. The 20,128 listings of August
2026 broke down into increases of 17% for single family homes, 19% for
condominiums and 15% for plexes. Read it alongside new listings for the month,
5,874, up 7%.

**Median price by category and by sector.** A median is the midpoint of closed
transactions, not the value of your property. It also moves when the mix of
what sold changes.

**The ratio of asking price to selling price.** This is the indicator buyers
ask for most, and it is not in the APCIQ monthly release. It is calculated
sector by sector from closed Centris transactions. Ask your broker for it for
your sector and your category, and ask how many transactions it is based on. A
ratio with no sample size is worth nothing, including when it suits my
argument. Same logic as with [bidding
wars](/en/articles/bidding-wars-truth-montreal/).

## Why a National Headline Describes Your Market Badly

Take that same month of August 2026 and compare two geographic levels, both
published by APCIQ.

For the province of Quebec as a whole: 6,502 sales, down 7%, and a median price
of $497,501 for single family homes. For the Montreal CMA alone: 2,853 sales,
down 13%, and a median price of $650,000 for single family homes.

Same month, same source, same definitions. Six points of difference on the
sales change, and more than $150,000 on the median price. A headline saying the
market slowed by 7% is not wrong, it is simply talking about something other
than your neighbourhood.

Splitting by category widens the gap further inside the CMA: single family
sales fell 9% in August 2026, condominium sales fell 18%. That is the same
market inside the same newspaper sentence.

## Two Common Claims the Data Contradicts

**"Population is exploding, so demand is guaranteed."** The Institut de la
statistique du Québec published its 2025 review on 13 May 2026. Quebec's
population stood at 9.03 million on 1 January 2026, down 9,600 people over the
year, or 0.1%. Natural increase was negative, with 78,200 births against 80,450
deaths. Total net migration was minus 7,150, made up of net international
migration of 450 people and net interprovincial migration of minus 7,600. The
number of non permanent residents in Quebec was estimated at 514,050 on
1 January 2026, down 51,400 from a year earlier.

That data covers all of Quebec, not the Montreal CMA, and says nothing about
prices. But it removes the foundation from the demographic argument as it is
usually stated.

**"Employment is collapsing."** According to the Statistics Canada Labour Force
Survey released on 4 September 2026, the unemployment rate in the Montreal CMA
was 6.5% in August 2026, almost unchanged both from the previous month and from
a year earlier. The Quebec rate was 5.6% and the Canadian rate 6.4%. That same
release notes, however, that the month's employment losses were concentrated in
the Montreal CMA, down 21,000 or 0.9%. Both facts sit in the same document and
deserve to be read together.

## What the Data Does Not Let You Say

None of the series quoted here projects prices for the next twelve months. None
tells you whether a given month marks a turn or a pause. A year over year
change depends on the reference month as much as on the observed one. A
category median does not describe one specific property. And a single month is
a small sample as soon as you drop down to one neighbourhood.

The same caution applies in the other direction. A headline announcing a record
is describing one month of one series in one territory, not a market, and a
strong month is no more predictive than a weak one.

Anyone announcing a specific percentage decline or increase for next year is
giving you an opinion, not data. Brokers included.

## What a Cycle Actually Changes Over a 10 Year Horizon

If you are buying to live somewhere for ten years, the cycle moves three
concrete things, and none of them is the future price.

**Time and negotiating room.** More listings and longer days on market in a
category means more showings you can attend, less pressure to sign the same
evening, and an inspection condition that is easier to keep in the offer.

**Entry costs.** Welcome tax, notary, inspection, moving: they are paid once
and spread across the holding period. Over ten years they weigh far less than
over two. That point, not a forecast, is what justifies a long horizon. The
[rent versus buy calculator](/en/tools/rent-vs-buy/) puts those costs in the
balance.

**Renewals.** Over ten years you will renew several times. The gap between your
approved maximum and your real budget matters more than the price you paid at
the start.

same data, see also [what the numbers say about the Montreal
