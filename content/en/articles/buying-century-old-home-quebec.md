A house built in 1915 gets visited like any other and bought differently. What
changes the file is not the finishes: it is the foundation, the wall nobody
opens, the wire behind the plaster, the pipe under the sidewalk, and the window
whose model is chosen by a permit. Here is where an ordinary inspection stops
being enough.

## Century old home: what the phrase means in Quebec

The marker is not sentimental, it is legal. The Insurance Bureau of Canada
writes that in Quebec, a house called "old" or "heritage" is a house built
before 1940 under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel, Quebec's cultural heritage
act. It also notes that the "inventoried" status, the one "most commonly
assigned," carries no government conservation requirement. Century old
therefore does not mean protected. The mandatory Déclarations du vendeur, the
seller's declarations form, puts the question at D2.9: the property is covered
by an act or regulation on heritage protection. Those statuses and their
consequences are laid out in [buying an old or heritage
building](/en/articles/heritage-property-montreal/). What follows is about the
age of the building, not its status.

## Period foundation and framing: what a closed wall will not tell you

The seller's form hands you the vocabulary before the visit. At D5.1, the seller
picks the foundation type: stone, wood, concrete block, concrete, or "do not
know." At D5.2, basement problems are declared, among them cracks in the
foundation and rot. At D3.1, soil problems: landslide, subsidence, earth
movement, instability. At D3.2, work already done to the foundations: pieutage,
the driving of steel piles under the footings, and travaux en sous-oeuvre,
underpinning.

A crack tells you movement happened. It tells you neither when, nor whether it
is still going on. That is the exact limit of a pre-purchase inspection. Under
the standard of practice of the Association des inspecteurs en bâtiments du
Québec, the AIBQ, Quebec's building inspectors association, the inspection "is a
visual and attentive inspection, which is however not technically exhaustive"
(art. 7) and covers components that are installed and readily accessible
(art. 8). The inspector describes the foundations (art. 17.1) and probes
structural components that appear deteriorated (art. 18), but does not have to
enter part of a building if doing so "could cause damage" (art. 11.1), nor to
give an opinion on the capacity or expected performance level of the structural
system (art. 22.2).

Nobody opens a wall before the purchase. What an open wall reveals: openings
enlarged without reinforcement, joists notched to let a pipe through, a repair
made with whatever was on hand that day. Active movement, or pieutage already
done, is read by a structural engineer, not by a generalist. And before you
open, know what the materials contain: Quebec regulation presumes the presence
of asbestos until an analysis proves otherwise, as explained in [vermiculite and
asbestos in a Quebec home](/en/articles/vermiculite-asbestos-home-quebec/).

## Knob and tube, fuses: the century old home as the insurer sees it

The Insurance Bureau of Canada puts the age and size of your home into the
premium calculation, along with materials, heating and location. And it writes
this: an insurer may not offer insurance products for older homes where specific
protection requirements apply.

No official source consulted lets me say that a Quebec insurer automatically
refuses knob and tube wiring or a fuse panel. What the insurer judges is what
you can prove. And the proof exists: the Régie du bâtiment du Québec writes that
the electrical contractor or owner-builder must declare to the Régie the work
covered by chapter V of the Construction Code, except in particular work
"involving a power of not more than 10 kW that does not require replacing or
adding wiring." Ask for the declaration of work and the licence number, not an
approximate date. The seller, for their part, answers at D10.2, with the example
given: a fuse or breaker that blows repeatedly. One last piece of IBC advice is
worth its weight in gold: get from the previous owner the contact details of the
company that insured the home. The rest of the file is in [hard to insure home
in Quebec](/en/articles/hard-to-insure-home-quebec/).

## Galvanized plumbing and the entrée d'eau en plomb

Two different problems, often confused. Galvanized pipe gives itself away
through the symptoms listed at D9.1: rust in the water, pressure or flow that
varies significantly, a pipe that freezes. The inspector describes the materials
of the supply and distribution piping (art. 30.1 and 30.3), but does not have to
observe water quality (art. 36.4).

The entrée d'eau en plomb, the lead water service line, can be checked and
costed. The City of Montreal describes the pipe: grey, it produces no resonance
when struck, it scratches, and it does not attract a magnet. The buildings
concerned are mostly those of eight dwellings or fewer built before 1970, and
the City publishes a screening map by address.

Under by-law 20-030, the owner must replace the private portion within 24 months
of receiving the City's notice of non compliance. When the City carries out the
work, the estimated average amount, based on its 2022 to 2025 data, is between
$1,200 and $1,400 per linear metre replaced. The bill goes out the following year
and can be spread over 15 years on the tax account. Then comes the sentence
aimed straight at a buyer: the amount is attached to the building and will be
passed on to the next owner if the property is sold. Add that line to your day
one costs, which the [closing costs
calculator](/en/tools/closing-costs/) totals for you.

## With no vapour barrier, insulating badly costs more than not insulating

A wall from 1920 has neither an air barrier nor a vapour barrier in the modern
sense. It dries because nothing holds the moisture in. Blow insulation into that
cavity and change nothing else, and you move the condensation point inside the
wall.

Natural Resources Canada is blunt: insulating must include air sealing, the
vapour barrier must be installed on the warm third of the finished wall, and you
have to account for the location and condition of old vapour barriers, which may
be nothing more than plaster walls covered with several coats of paint. The
order of operations is named: deal with moisture or structural problems before
insulating. The warning sign is declared at D7.1: has regular and significant
condensation ever formed in winter? A check mark next to "walls" is not a
comfort detail.

The pre-purchase inspection will not decide this for you. The AIBQ standard
requires the inspector to describe the insulation and vapour barrier seen in
unfinished spaces (art. 55), then states that the inspector does not have to
rule on the building's compliance with standards, nor on the uniformity,
sufficiency or necessity of the insulation (art. 56). An old wall assembly
insulated from the inside is designed by a building envelope specialist. For a
heritage building, the Régie du bâtiment du Québec does provide a procedure for
equivalent or differing measures.

## Windows: in a cited building, the permit chooses the model

In Montreal, replacing exterior doors and windows with models of the same
dimensions requires a permit in several boroughs, and the underlying rule does
not vary: replaced elements must have the shape and appearance of those of the
original building, and must also respect the typology of the building, including
the type of opening, the subdivisions or the colour. In a heritage sector or
site, additional requirements may apply. One borough writes that sliding windows
are not permitted in sectors deemed significant.

The fees are published: $9.80 per $1,000 of work, minimum $167.40 for a
residential building, non refundable. They go up if the building or the sector
has heritage value, and a site planning and architectural integration programme
adds fees and delays.

In Laval, roughly 2,600 buildings inventoried between 2015 and 2022 are covered
by by-law L-12950: the exterior envelope, the roof, the foundation, the doors
and the windows must be in good condition and fulfill their function. The City
may require work by written notice, then register a notice of deterioration in
the Registre foncier, Quebec's land register, under section 145.41.4 of the Loi
sur l'aménagement et l'urbanisme, the planning act.

No official source consulted puts a figure on the cost of a window. What is
verifiable is that the permit restricts the model before your first quote, and
that the seller declares any window replacement at D2.11.

## Buying a century old home: the expert first, then the negotiation

The AIBQ standard says it in its introduction: visual building inspection is not
an expert assessment. Article 10 adds that the inspector must recommend a
technically exhaustive examination by a specialist when the signs warrant it.

| What the pre-purchase inspection does not cover | AIBQ standard | Who can answer |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity of the structural system | art. 22.2 | Structural engineer |
| Buried elements, including foundation drains | art. 12.6 | Camera inspection |
| Remaining service life and cost of corrective work | art. 12.1, 12.2 | Written quotes |
| Insulation and vapour barrier: sufficiency | art. 56 | Building envelope specialist |
| Compliance with municipal by-laws | art. 12.10 | Permits counter |
| Insurability of the building | introduction | Insurer |

**What is negotiable.** The delay first: the inspection condition has to be long
enough to fit a second expert assessment. Then the documents: the electrical
declaration of work, the water service invoice, the citation by-law if there is
one, the name of the previous insurer.

**What you budget for.** An annual reserve, not a list of jobs. Two items can be
costed right now in Montreal: the doors and windows permit and the entrée d'eau
en plomb.

## Frequently asked questions

**My house dates from 1918 and has no status. Can I renovate freely?**
Not necessarily. The Insurance Bureau of Canada points out that an old house
with no status provided for by law may still be subject to municipal
regulation. In Montreal, a permit is still required to replace doors and windows
in several boroughs. In Laval, roughly 2,600 listed buildings are covered by
by-law L-12950.

**The inspector wrote "stone foundation in apparently good condition." Is that enough?**
That is what the standard asks of them: observe, describe, note the condition.
It forbids them from ruling on the capacity of the structural system (art. 22.2)
and on remaining service life (art. 12.1). For those two questions, you need an
engineer.

**The seller says the City replaced the entrée d'eau en plomb. What should I check?**
The invoice and the date. In Montreal, it goes out the following year and can be
spread over 15 years on the tax account: the balance is attached to the building
and passes to the next owner.
