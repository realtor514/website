The sales representative announces an insurance discount, sometimes a precise
percentage. Nobody shows you the grid that number rests on. Here is what is
published in Quebec, what is not, and the municipal bill almost nobody calculates
before signing a monitoring contract.

## No Quebec insurer publishes a quantified discount

Let us start with the essential point, which contradicts the sales pitch. No
official Quebec source puts a figure on the home insurance discount tied to an
alarm system.

The Autorité des marchés financiers is the most direct on the subject: "Some
insurers will offer you a discount if your home is equipped with an alarm system.
Ask what the amount of that discount is before installation." Some, not all. Ask,
because nobody publishes it. Before installation, because the reverse order takes
away all your leverage.

The Bureau d'assurance du Canada says the same thing without a number. Installing
loss prevention devices, including a burglar alarm system, "can provide additional
protection and reduce your premiums."

The reason for that silence is simple: every insurer has its own grid. The
Autorité points out that the premium varies considerably from one insurer to
another, and that a broker must be able to obtain quotes from at least three
insurers, whereas an agent offers the products of only one. The only number that
counts is the one your insurer writes on your file.

## Connected to a central station or a local alarm: the distinction insurers look at

What is not quantified is still named. In its consumer guide, the Bureau
d'assurance du Canada writes, among its tips for paying less: "Equip your home
with an alarm system that would be connected to a central station." Not an alarm
system. A system connected to a central station. The Montreal agglomeration by-law
makes the same separation: a system may be "local or connected to an alarm
monitoring station." A siren in an empty street calls no one. A central station
validates the alert and passes on the call.

**Connecting to a central station is a regulated activity.** The Bureau de la
sécurité privée, Quebec's private security board, is clear: alarm response is a
guarding activity within the meaning of the Loi sur la sécurité privée, the
private security act. The business must hold an agency licence, and every person
carrying out the activity an agent licence. The BSP's register of licence holders
tells you in real time whether the licence is valid, and not suspended, revoked or
expired. Then keep your monitoring contract: it is the document that proves the
connection the day your insurer asks for it.

## False alarms: the municipal bill, in Montreal and in Laval

Here is the cost official sources do quantify, and it is rarely mentioned at the
point of sale. The Service de police de la Ville de Montréal responds to close to
30,000 alarm calls a year, 95% of which are unfounded. The agglomeration council
adopted by-law RCG 25-011 on May 15, 2025, in force since May 21, 2025. Laval, for
its part, bills police interventions, and governs fire alarms through by-law
L-13252.

| City and alarm type | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | Following |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Montreal, residential burglary | no charge | $96 | $146 | $191 |
| Laval, burglar, residential | notice | $114 | $145 | $177 |
| Montreal, fire, 1 or 2 dwellings | no charge | no charge | $500 | $500 |
| Laval, fire, fewer than 6 dwellings | notice | $250 | $500 | $1,000 |

The counting rules differ. In Montreal, an unfounded alarm triggered more than a
year after the previous one counts as a first, and the charges are payable within
30 days of the invoice. In Laval, the city bills interventions made within the 183
days following a first unfounded intervention, the second one only if it occurs
after a delay of ten days, and the following ones after 24 hours.

In Montreal, the person responsible is the owner of the building, except in divided
co-ownership, where the syndicate takes over when the intervention does not relate
to any specific fraction. The city also provides for a reimbursement of up to 90%
for anyone who has their fire alarm system repaired or replaced after paying their
invoices, on request within 180 days.

## Declaring the alarm system to your insurer: article 2408

Article 2408 of the Civil Code is the basic rule: the client must declare all the
circumstances known to him which are likely to materially influence an insurer in
the setting of the premium, the appraisal of the risk or the decision to cover it.

Article 2466 covers the rest of the contract: the insured must promptly declare
the circumstances that aggravate the risks and that result from his own acts. The
insurer may then cancel the contract or propose a new rate in writing, which the
insured has 30 days to accept and pay, failing which the policy ceases to be in
force.

The price of silence is written down too. Article 2410 allows the insurer to ask
for nullity of the contract in case of a false declaration, "even with respect to
losses not connected with the risk so misrepresented." The Autorité des marchés
financiers says it plainly to the general public: declare even without a direct
question, otherwise the insurer could refuse to indemnify even if the loss has no
connection at all with your omission.

So an alarm system cuts both ways: it may be worth a discount, and having
unplugged it or left it without a monitoring contract also gets declared. An
insurance refusal collapses a buyer's financing days before signing: [what makes a
home hard to insure](/en/articles/hard-to-insure-home-quebec/) can be verified
before the offer, and the [buyer's guide](/en/buyer/) gives the order of the
checks.

## An outdoor camera and your neighbour's privacy

Here is a belief worth correcting: the Commission d'accès à l'information is not
the arbiter of your neighbour's camera. It oversees compliance with the access act,
which covers public bodies, and with the private sector act, which covers
businesses. Its fact sheet on video surveillance is titled "Practical advice for
public bodies and businesses." A private individual is neither one nor the other.

The framework that applies between neighbours is the Civil Code. Article 35 sets
out the right to respect for privacy. Article 36 lists the possible invasions, and
two of them bear directly on a camera: "Using his image or voice while he is in
private premises" and "Keeping his private life under observation by any means."
The recourse goes before the courts, and [recourses in a neighbour
dispute](/en/articles/neighbour-disputes-quebec/) follow the same logic of
measurement and proof.

The criteria the Commission imposes on businesses remain the best yardstick for
judging yourself: a legitimate, important, urgent and real objective, a
proportionate invasion, a field of view limited to the necessary places, and
retention of the images that it generally considers sufficient at 30 days. A video
doorbell framed on your own door passes that test. The same camera framed on the
neighbour's yard does not.

## Smart locks: what nobody puts a number on

I found no official Quebec source stating that a theft without breaking and
entering is excluded from a residential home insurance policy. The Bureau
d'assurance du Canada in fact places "theft or attempted theft" among the risks
covered by most policies. So I will not put a figure on a risk nobody documents.

What is verifiable is more down to earth. A smart lock produces a log: which code,
at what time, which door. That log is evidence, for you or against you. And every
code handed to a cleaner, a contractor or a former partner is an access you no
longer control. When that changes the real exposure of the house, we are back to
article 2466: circumstances that result from your own acts.

## Selling or buying with a leased alarm system

A leased system is not an inclusion. It is a contract, and the OACIQ forms treat
it as one. The exclusive brokerage contract to sell (CCVE 00001, version 06/2022)
provides, at clause 4.6, for "Service and rental contracts for appliances and
equipment to be assumed by the buyer," with three boxes: water heater, alarm
system, propane tank. The Promise to Purchase (PA 00001, version 06/2022) repeats
the same list at clause 11.7.

So the box exists from the first day of the listing. Checked, it means the
equipment stays in place and the buyer assumes the contract. The seller also
completes the [Déclarations du vendeur sur
l'immeuble](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/).

**What the buyer demands before signing.** A copy of the contract, not a verbal
summary. The term remaining and the monthly payment. Written confirmation that it
is transferable, because not all of them are. The cancellation penalty, and who
owns the equipment at the end of the term. Finally, the transfer of the account
with the central station: a contract left in the seller's name sends the calls to
the wrong person.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much is the discount worth if I connect my system to a central station?**
No official source puts a figure on it, and no percentage quoted by a salesperson
binds your insurer. The Autorité des marchés financiers recommends asking for the
amount of the discount before installation, in dollars.

**Do I have to register my alarm system with the city?**
No. Laval indicates that no permit is required for the installation, and the
Montreal agglomeration by-law provides for no registration. Both cities bill the
interventions arising from unfounded alarms instead.

**My neighbour has a camera filming my driveway. Do I complain to the Commission
d'accès à l'information?**
That is not the right counter: the laws it applies cover public bodies and
businesses. Between neighbours, it is articles 35 and 36 of the Civil Code, before
the courts. Start with a written request to reposition the camera, and keep a copy
of it.

**Who pays the false alarm bill when the house sells during the year?**
In Montreal, the person responsible is the owner of the building at the time of
the intervention: an invoice issued before the sale follows the seller. Ask anyway
whether there have been recent interventions, because the counter only resets
after a year with no unfounded alarm.
