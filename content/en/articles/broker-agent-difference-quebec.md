"Broker", "agent", "Realtor". The three words circulate as synonyms in everyday
conversation. In Quebec, only one of them corresponds to a licence issued by a
regulator, and the distinction is not cosmetic: it determines what protections
you have.

## The Quebec framework: the OACIQ

Real estate brokerage in Quebec is regulated by the **OACIQ**, the Organisme
d'autoréglementation du courtage immobilier du Québec. It issues licences, sets
professional standards, inspects practices and handles complaints.

Its mission is explicitly the protection of the public, not the defence of
brokers. That distinction matters: the OACIQ is not a trade association, it is
the body that can suspend or revoke a licence.

## Courtier immobilier: the only title backed by a licence

To practise, a broker must have completed the recognized real estate brokerage
training, passed the OACIQ certification exam and hold a valid licence. The
licence has to be renewed and maintained through mandatory continuing education.

Two useful details:

**The chartered real estate broker.** A higher licence level, which allows the
holder to act as an agency director or to practise on their own account.

**The agency director.** The professional responsible for the agency's operations
and compliance. They answer to the OACIQ for the conduct of the brokers attached
to their agency.

## Real estate agency: the business, not the person

An agency is a company holding its own licence, bringing together brokers and
supervising their work. RE/MAX Du Cartier is an agency.

That structure adds a layer of accountability: your brokerage contract is signed
with the agency, and the agency is required to supervise the broker serving you.

## Careful: mortgage brokers are no longer regulated by the OACIQ

Many people do not know this. **Since May 2020, mortgage brokerage falls under
the Autorité des marchés financiers, not the OACIQ.**

Two professions, two licences, two different regulators. The real estate broker
guides you through buying or selling a property. The mortgage broker shops your
financing among lenders. One person can hold both licences, but they are indeed
two licences.

So when you verify a licence, verify in the right register, according to the
service the person is providing.

## What about "Realtor"?

Realtor is a trademark. In Canada it belongs to the Canadian Real Estate
Association, and only its members may use it.

Membership is voluntary, not a legal requirement. A Quebec broker can be
excellent without being a member, and membership neither replaces nor duplicates
the OACIQ licence. In other words: Realtor is a subset, not a synonym, and in
Quebec it is the OACIQ licence that defines your rights.

## Why "real estate agent" is inaccurate in Quebec

In several Canadian provinces, "agent" refers to a licensed salesperson working
under a broker's supervision. That vocabulary does not exist in Quebec
regulation, where everyone is a courtier.

When you hear "agent" in Quebec, it is informal usage borrowed from English or
from other provinces. The person is either licensed as a courtier, or has no
licence at all.

## What the licence actually gives you

This is where the distinction stops being about vocabulary.

**A duty to advise.** The broker must inform you of any relevant fact, even when
that fact hurts the transaction. They must tell you what you need to know, not
only what helps close.

**A verification obligation.** The broker must verify the information they pass
along. They cannot simply repeat what the seller claims without validating it,
which protects you directly.

**Mandatory professional liability insurance.** Every practising broker is
covered. If a professional error causes you harm, there is coverage behind it.

**A compensation fund.** The real estate indemnity fund can compensate victims of
fraud, dishonest dealing or misappropriation of funds by a licence holder.

**A real recourse.** A complaint to the OACIQ is not a courtesy email. The syndic
investigates, and a discipline committee can impose sanctions up to revoking the
licence.

None of those protections exist in a transaction handled by someone without a
licence.

## Verify the licence, it takes thirty seconds

The OACIQ maintains a public register of licence holders. You search the name and
you get the licence type, the agency, whether the licence is valid, and any
disciplinary sanctions.

Do it before the first meeting, not after signing. A broker in good standing has
no problem whatsoever with that check.

## One last thing the title does not tell you

A licence sets a floor, not a ceiling. It guarantees training, legal obligations
and a recourse. It does not guarantee the quality of the marketing, the rigour of
the pricing analysis, or the ability to negotiate.

That is why verifying the licence is the first step, not the last. The
[questions to ask before hiring a broker](/en/articles/questions-hire-broker/)
cover the rest: the method, the plan and the verifiable results.
