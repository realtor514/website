In January 2026, in the Montreal metropolitan area, APCIQ counted 2,364 residential sales, the thinnest month of its year. So there were fewer buyers standing beside you. There were also fewer houses listed, and a good part of each one was under snow. Here is what winter gives you, what it hides from you, and how to compensate for both.

## Buying a home in winter in Quebec: what the numbers say

The Association professionnelle des courtiers immobiliers du Québec (APCIQ, Quebec's real estate brokers association) publishes sales, listings, median prices and days on market every month, drawn from the Centris system. Four months are enough to set the scene for a buyer in the Montreal census metropolitan area (CMA).

| Month | Sales | New listings | Active listings | Active single-family per sale |
| - | - | - | - | - |
| December 2025 | 2,831 | 2,529 | 15,532 | 4.1 |
| January 2026 | 2,364 | 6,550 | 16,798 | 5.6 |
| February 2026 | 3,930 | 7,629 | 18,695 | 3.6 |
| June 2026 | 4,012 | 6,648 | 20,894 | 3.8 |

The last column is the calculation that matters to you: single-family homes listed, divided by single-family homes sold that month. In January 2026, APCIQ counted 6,498 active single-family listings against 1,152 sales; in June 2026, 8,070 against 2,129. Fewer choices in January, but even fewer buyers competing for them. For condominiums, the same calculation gives 8.8 in January against 7.1 in June.

**The real lull is short.** December and January, not "winter." By February 2026 the ratio falls back to 3.6, below June's: sellers are already back, 7,629 new listings, and the average time on market for a single-family home had come back down to 39 days against 50 in January.

## Fewer buyers, but a price that does not drop because of it

The median price of a single-family home in the Montreal CMA was $615,000 in January 2026 and $649,000 in June 2026. Do not read that gap as a winter discount: a median describes the properties that sold that month, not the same house at two points in time. Nobody can promise you a lower price because you are buying in February.

What winter changes is the posture. A seller who lists on January 8 is generally moving for a reason: a job transfer, a separation, an estate, a house already bought elsewhere. The one who lists on May 8 is sometimes testing the market. And a property that has been listed for 50 days, January's average, gets negotiated differently from one listed 12 days ago. The other side of that calendar is in [when to list your property in Montreal](/en/articles/best-time-sell-home-montreal/).

Set your ceiling before the Saturday visit in a snowstorm: the [affordability calculator](/en/tools/affordability/) gives you an order of magnitude, your lender gives you the real number.

## What winter shows that June hides

This is the least discussed advantage, and the most concrete: in January, the house is being tested in front of you.

**The heating is running at full load.** You feel the temperature gap between the ground floor and the upper floor, the room above the garage, the basement slab. Ask for the last twelve months of energy billing, not an average from memory. The OACIQ Déclarations du vendeur form (the seller's declarations) already gives the energy source of the main system (D12.1), the year the furnace was installed (D12.3) and the problems that have already occurred (D12.7).

**Drafts and condensation.** The same form asks a question that only makes sense in cold weather: has regular and significant condensation ever formed in winter (D7.1). In June, the answer is theoretical. In January, you are looking at the windows.

**Ice dams.** The Bureau d'assurance du Canada (Insurance Bureau of Canada) describes them as a ridge of ice that forms at the edge of the roof or in the gutters, when snow melts, runs off, then freezes again. The causes it names: inadequate attic insulation, poor attic ventilation, snow accumulation and blocked gutters. A row of icicles across the front is therefore not decorative: it is information about the attic space that you will never get in summer.

**The snow has to go somewhere.** In Montreal, pushing snow into the street, onto the sidewalk or near fire hydrants is prohibited; the city asks you to shovel it onto your own land. Some boroughs allow you to apply for a paid authorization to deposit snow on public property for a residential building of 4 dwellings or fewer. In Laval, the rule has no nuance: depositing snow on streets, sidewalks or medians is strictly prohibited, and a first offence under by-law L-6070 costs $107. Look at where the driveway snow physically goes, and how many cars still fit there in February.

**On-street parking.** Montreal spreads abrasives as soon as the roadway is slippery, plows from 2.5 cm of accumulation and hauls from 10 to 15 cm. Every hauling operation moves the cars, and without off-street parking, winter is the only time you can measure it.

## What the snow prevents an inspection from seeing

The professional standard of practice of the Association des inspecteurs en bâtiments du Québec (AIBQ, Quebec's building inspectors association) is explicit. Under article 11.3, the inspector is not required to move earth, snow, ice or debris that blocks access or interferes with visibility. Under article 11.1, neither is the inspector required to walk on the roof where doing so could cause damage or present a risk.

The obligations remain: describe the condition of the roof coverings, the roof drainage systems and the flashings (article 26), state in the report the methods used to observe the roof (article 27), and report ground slopes, water drainage installations and retaining walls where they are likely to affect the building (article 23.7).

In January, then, five items fall outside the field of view: the roof covering, the foundation at grade, the grading and water runoff, the condition of the driveway surface, the landscaping. The most important page of a winter report is the limitations page. Read it before the photos.

OACIQ reminds brokers that they must recommend a full inspection by a recognized expert, and that the inspector must carry professional liability insurance, use a recognized service agreement, inspect according to a recognized standard of practice and hand over a written report. The selection criteria are in [choosing a home inspector](/en/articles/choosing-home-inspector-quebec/).

## Buying in winter: what to ask the seller

What the inspector cannot see, the file can sometimes tell you. Ask, in writing, before you make your offer:

- **The Déclarations du vendeur, read line by line.** The year the roof covering was installed (D8.2) and the documents supporting it (D8.3). Water infiltrations that have already occurred and where, basement, roof, terrace, balcony, skylight (D4.1). The presence of a drain français (weeping tile) and of an access to inspect and clean it (D9.4). Stabilization work already carried out on the foundations (D3.2).
- **The invoices and warranties** for the roof, the drain, the windows, the heating system.
- **The summer photos.** A previous listing, the seller's own photos, a recent aerial view: the lot, the driveway and the roof are all visible in them.
- **The snow removal contract**, what it costs and what it covers.

The seller declares to the best of their knowledge, which does not replace a verification. What the form does and does not do is explained in [the seller's declaration](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/).

## The clauses that cover what could not be examined

The OACIQ promesse d'achat (promise to purchase) provides at clause 8 for an inspection by a person designated by the buyer, and the form carries this warning: a promise without an inspection clause is appropriate only in particular circumstances. The deadline is written in by the parties. If the inspection reveals a factor likely to significantly reduce the value, the buyer notifies the seller in writing and gives them a copy of the report no later than the 4th day following the expiry of that deadline, before 8 p.m. Failing that, the buyer is deemed to have waived the condition.

It is clause 12.1, "Other declarations and conditions," that serves to write down what winter prevented you from verifying. A useful clause names three things: the precise object, for example the roof covering, the grading and the surface drain; a trigger, for example the complete melting of the snow; and an agreed consequence.

Be clear-eyed about the third one. A condition that suspends the sale for three months is rarely accepted. What actually gets negotiated is more modest: moving the inspection to after a thaw, asking the seller to clear a section of roof or the perimeter of the foundation, or agreeing in writing to a holdback of funds at the notary until a spring verification. None of it is automatic, and the wording has to be validated by your notary or your lawyer.

Talk to your insurer too, before you commit. The Bureau d'assurance du Canada specifies that sudden and accidental water damage caused by an ice dam is generally covered, whereas continuous or repeated damage is treated as a long-term maintenance problem and might not be. The grounds for refusal are detailed in [hard to insure home in Quebec](/en/articles/hard-to-insure-home-quebec/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do you pay less for a house in winter?**
No public data lets you say so. The median price of a single-family home in the Montreal CMA was $615,000 in January 2026 and $649,000 in June 2026, but a median describes the properties that sold that month, not a seasonal discount. What changes is the number of competitors and the average time on market.

**Can the inspector go up on the roof in February?**
The AIBQ standard of practice does not require it where the operation could cause damage or present a risk, and does not require the inspector to move snow or ice either. The inspector must, however, state in the report the methods used to observe the roof. That statement is what tells you what was actually seen.

**Does the seller have to lower the price because the roof could not be inspected?**
No, no rule requires it. What does exist is your right to write a condition into clause 12.1 of the promesse d'achat and to negotiate its consequence before acceptance. Once the deed of sale is signed, that door is closed.

**Does a visit in January replace a visit in June?**
No, they do not show you the same house. Winter shows thermal behaviour, runoff, ice and snow management. Summer shows the roof, the ground, the drainage and the lot. Plan a return visit in the spring, even once you own the place.
