It is the most predictable unpleasant surprise in Quebec real estate. You signed at the notary, you moved in, everything is fine, and three to six months later your municipality sends you a bill for several thousand dollars.

Those are the real estate transfer duties, which everyone calls the welcome tax.

## What it actually is

Despite the nickname, it has nothing to do with being new in town. It is a municipal tax levied on the transfer of ownership of an immovable, set out in Quebec's Act respecting duties on transfers of immovables (CQLR, c. D-15.1).

The name comes from Jean Bienvenue, the minister who introduced the measure in the 1970s. It is a coincidence of surname, not a welcome message.

## How the amount is calculated

The tax is calculated on the tax base, which is the greatest of the following three amounts:

1. The price paid for the property
2. The consideration stipulated in the deed
3. The market value of the property at the time of transfer, which the municipality derives from the assessment roll multiplied by the comparative factor for the year

That third point is the one that surprises people. If you buy a property well below its assessed value, the tax is still calculated on the adjusted roll value, not on the price you paid.

The calculation is then progressive, in brackets. Each portion of the tax base is taxed at its own rate, like income tax: only the part above a threshold is taxed at the higher rate.

## The 2026 and 2027 brackets

The first two thresholds are indexed every year. On July 31, 2026, the Ministère des Affaires municipales et de l'Habitation confirmed indexation of 2.4173% for 2027. The new thresholds apply to transfers made on or after January 1, 2027.

| Portion of the tax base | Rate | 2026 | 2027 |
|---|---|---|---|
| First bracket | 0.5% | up to $62,900 | up to $64,400 |
| Second bracket | 1% | $62,900 to $315,000 | $64,400 to $322,600 |
| Above | 1.5% | over $315,000 | over $322,600 |

These are the rates set by the Act. They apply everywhere in Quebec up to $500,000.

## Worked examples at the provincial rates

| Tax base | Duty 2026 | Duty 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| $300,000 | $2,685.50 | $2,678 |
| $400,000 | $4,110.50 | $4,065 |
| $500,000 | $5,610.50 | $5,565 |

The detail for $400,000 in 2027: $64,400 at 0.5% gives $322, the next $258,200 at 1% gives $2,582, and the remaining $77,400 at 1.5% gives $1,161. Total: $4,065.

Indexation lowers the bill slightly from one year to the next for the same base, because more of the price falls into the low-rate brackets. It does not offset a higher price: a house that costs $20,000 more costs up to $300 more in duties, at the 1.5% rate.

## Above $500,000: each city sets its own rate

The Act allows a municipality to set, by by-law, a rate higher than 1.5% on the portion above $500,000, up to 3%. The City of Montreal is the only one not subject to that cap. The $500,000 threshold is not indexed.

**Montreal in 2026.** According to the City of Montreal, the 2026 brackets are:

| Portion of the tax base | Rate |
|---|---|
| Up to $62,900 | 0.5% |
| $62,900 to $315,000 | 1% |
| $315,000 to $552,300 | 1.5% |
| $552,300 to $1,104,700 | 2% |
| $1,104,700 to $2,136,500 | 2.5% |
| $2,136,500 to $3,113,000 | 3.5% |
| Over $3,113,000 | 4% |

The example published by the city, for a $700,000 base in 2026, comes to $9,349. Montreal's 2027 brackets are set with its 2027 budget: the first two thresholds will follow the Act, $64,400 and $322,600.

**Laval in 2026.** According to the Courrier Laval of February 9, 2026, the city's 2026 budget raised to 3% the rate on any amount above $500,000. On a $630,000 base, the median price of a single-family home in Laval in the third quarter of 2026 according to the QPAREB, the calculation gives $5,610.50 up to $500,000, plus $3,900 on the next $130,000: $9,510.50. City council also asked staff to study spreading the payment over several instalments starting with the 2027 fiscal year. The 2027 rate and terms depend on the city's 2027 budget.

**Elsewhere.** Several municipalities in the Laurentides, Lanaudière and Montérégie have adopted their own by-law for the portion above $500,000. For a property in that price range, ask the municipality, or your notary, for the by-law in force before you make your offer. ## When you receive it

This is the part that catches the most people. The bill does not arrive at signing. It arrives when the municipality processes the transfer, which generally takes three to six months after the transaction, sometimes longer.

The duty becomes payable on the 31st day after the municipality sends the bill, under section 11 of the Act. Some municipalities allow more than one instalment, others do not.

The trap is simple: you drained your cash for the down payment, notary fees and the move, you think the hard part is behind you, and a bill for several thousand dollars arrives when your account is at its lowest.

**Set the money aside at the time of purchase.** That is the only advice that matters here. Calculate the amount before you make your offer, and keep it in a separate account until the notice arrives.

## Who pays it

The purchaser, in virtually every case. It is a tax that hits the buyer, not the seller.

The notary does not collect it at signing and does not include it in the statement of disbursements. The notary transmits the transfer notice to the municipality, and the municipality then bills you directly.

So do not expect this amount to show up in the notary's accounting. It comes afterward, separately.

## The exemptions that genuinely exist

The Act provides certain exemptions, mainly for transfers between related parties: between spouses, in the direct ascending or descending line, and certain transfers involving closely related corporations. Even when the transfer is exempt, the municipality may, by resolution, charge a compensatory duty (droit supplétif) of at most $200.

The conditions are precise and the notary verifies them at the time of the transaction. If your situation resembles one of these cases, mention it to your notary early, not after.

**What about a first purchase?** The tax is still owed, but since 2026 Quebec refunds part of it. The refundable tax credit for access to home ownership, announced in April 2026, gives first-time buyers back 100% of the first $5,000 of transfer duties and 25% of the next $3,500, up to $5,875, for a home acquired on or after January 1, 2026. It shrinks above a value of $750,000 and disappears at $1,000,000. It replaces the equivalent municipal aid: Montreal closed its own program on July 7, 2026. Conditions and the first-time buyer definition: [first-time buyer tax advantages](/en/articles/first-time-buyer-tax-credits/). This is money many eligible buyers may never claim.
