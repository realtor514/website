The water arrives in the spring, in a finished basement bought the previous
fall. The first reflex is to call a contractor and have it repaired. That is
often the exact move that kills the recourse. Before any work, there are four
conditions to verify and one letter to send.

## The four conditions of a vice caché, one by one

Article 1726 of the Civil Code of Québec sets the rule. The seller warrants
that the property and its accessories are, at the time of the sale, free of
latent defects, vices cachés, which render it unfit for the use for which it
was intended, or which so diminish its usefulness that the buyer would not have
bought it, or would not have paid so high a price, had he been aware of them.
The second paragraph excludes two things: a latent defect already known to the
buyer, and an apparent defect.

The OACIQ draws four characteristics from this, and they are cumulative. The
defect existed at the time of purchase. It was unknown to the buyer. It was not
apparent. It is serious enough that, had he known of it, the buyer probably
would not have bought, or would have asked for a reduction in price. One
condition missing, and the file collapses.

| Condition | What it requires | What defeats it |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden | Nothing reveals it on a serious examination | A visible trace on the day of the visit |
| Serious | It changes the price or the decision to buy | Normal wear, deferred maintenance |
| Prior | It already existed at signing | A breakdown that happened after the sale |
| Unknown | The buyer was unaware of it | A defect listed in the Declarations by the seller |

One point surprises sellers: the OACIQ writes that the fact the seller is
unaware of the defect, or that the defect only shows up after the sale, does
nothing to reduce the warranty. One clean exception remains, article 1731: a
sale made under judicial authority gives rise to no warranty of quality.

## A prudent and diligent buyer: what the inspection actually proves

An apparent defect, under article 1726, is one that can be discovered by a
prudent and diligent buyer without the need to resort to an expert. Éducaloi
draws the consequence: the law does not oblige you to hire an inspector, it
obliges you to act with prudence, and not having an inspection done, or not
having it done in a prudent and diligent manner, amounts to accepting the
property with every defect that could have been detected.

The inspection is visual. The inspector does not open up walls. But he must
draw your attention to anything that could justify a more thorough inspection,
and it is that sentence in the report that decides the file later: if it is
there and you did not follow up on it, the defect was flagged.

The OACIQ imposes four requirements on the professional a broker may recommend
to you: professional liability insurance covering faults, errors or omissions,
a recognized service agreement, a recognized standard of practice, and a
written report. On the Régie du bâtiment du Québec certificate, be precise: the
RBQ has been issuing it since 1 October 2024, and it will become mandatory for
all inspectors on 1 October 2027. In 2026, an uncertified inspector is still
practising legally. The rest is in [pre-purchase inspection in Quebec: what it
costs and when to do it](/en/articles/home-inspection-cost-timing-quebec/).

## What serious means in practice

Éducaloi defines the defect as a significant flaw that reduces the quality of
the property and prevents the owner from using or enjoying it normally. Then it
sets the real test: you have a recourse if the defect is serious to the point
that you would have demanded a lower price had you known. That is a test on
your decision, not on the size of the invoice. A defect that makes a basement
uninhabitable passes it. A window at the end of its life on a 1975 house does
not, because predictable wear is not a defect. No official source sets a
minimum dollar amount, and be wary of anyone who quotes you one.

## Proving the defect existed before the sale

This is the most contested condition, because the burden of proof sits on you.
Only one presumption exists in the Code, and it is narrow. Article 1729 applies
to a sale by a professional seller: the existence of the defect at the time of
the sale is presumed where the property malfunctions or deteriorates
prematurely compared with identical property or property of the same type,
unless the defect is due to improper use by the buyer.

Your seller still has to be one. Éducaloi puts in that category the individual
who earns income from selling immovables on a regular basis, and the one who
built the building in order to sell it. Not professional: the individual
selling his own house, the person who sells immovables occasionally, the lender
selling a foreclosure, and the liquidator of a succession.

In front of a private seller, then, nothing is presumed. Anteriority has to be
demonstrated: the age of a crack, the nature of a stain, the date of an
installation. That is a building expert's work, and his report is the
centrepiece of the file. No official source supports the claim that a defect
appearing three or six months after the sale would be presumed to predate it.

## Written notice: the step that loses solid files

Article 1739 is short and it decides everything. A buyer who ascertains that
the property is defective must give notice in writing to the seller within a
reasonable time after discovering it. Where the defect appears gradually, that
time runs from the day the buyer could have suspected its seriousness and
extent. One case is reserved: the seller may not invoke late notice if he knew
of the defect or could not have been unaware of it.

How long is reasonable? Éducaloi answers that a reasonable time is assessed
according to the circumstances, that you have to count the time needed to
identify and measure the seriousness of the defect, and that generally a period
of 6 months to a year will be considered reasonable, exceptionally longer where
the defect shows up seasonally. That is room to get an expert opinion, not room
to hesitate.

And here is the recurring mistake. Éducaloi is explicit: if you do not want to
bear the cost of the work, do not start the repairs at your own expense. The
seller must have the opportunity to verify the existence and seriousness of the
defect, to see the damage, and to repair or replace. A basement redone before
you wrote to the seller is destroyed evidence. One exception only: urgent
repairs, where the defect is dangerous or risks seriously damaging the building
or causing its loss.

You can also send a mise en demeure, a formal demand letter, rather than a
simple notice. It contains the description of the defect, your demands, your
conditions and the deadline to reply, and it is the prerequisite to a lawsuit.
Note what your broker cannot do here: the OACIQ forbids him to give a legal
opinion, on deadlines for example, and to draft a mise en demeure.

## Your recourses: price reduction, cancellation, damages

Éducaloi lists what the warranty can obtain depending on the situation: a
reduction in price, reimbursement of work done or needed to remedy the defect,
compensation for damage suffered in certain circumstances, or cancellation of
the sale with return of the property and refund of the price paid.

Damages have their own article. Article 1728 provides that if the seller knew
of the defect or could not have been unaware of it, he is bound, in addition to
restoring the price, to make reparation for the injury suffered. His knowledge
does not create the warranty, then, it enlarges the bill. To put a number on a
price reduction, you need a value reference before subtracting the cost of the
work: [the property value estimator](/en/tools/home-estimate/) gives the
starting point, the contractor gives the rest.

One last element is checked in the title. The OACIQ points out that an earlier
sale made at the buyer's own risk and peril breaks the chain through which the
warranties are passed on: from that point, they are reset. Your recourse
against previous owners therefore depends on the earlier deeds, and one sale
without legal warranty anywhere in that chain is enough to end it.

## Three years to act, and the court that will hear you

Article 2925 sets at three years the prescription of a personal action whose
period is not otherwise determined, and Éducaloi specifies the starting point
for a vice caché: three years from the discovery of the defect, and not from
the date of purchase. Article 2926 adds that where the injury appears gradually
or later on, the period runs from the day the injury first appears. Those three
years do not replace the notice under article 1739: they are two clocks, and
the short one is the one that loses files.

The Small Claims Court (Cour des petites créances) hears claims of $15,000 or
less, interest excluded, and Éducaloi gives as an example a claim against the
seller for the cost of the work for a latent defect. You act alone there,
without a lawyer, and you can reduce your claim to $15,000 to get in even if
the work costs $21,000. Free mediation is offered. Above that, you need a
lawyer, and no official source lets me put forward an average cost.

## The first week after the discovery

1. **Photograph and date** before touching anything at all.
2. **Repair nothing**, except in an emergency. Stopping the water, yes.
   Redoing the basement, no.
3. **Pull out your documents**: the pre-purchase inspection report, the
   Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble (the seller's declarations on the
   property), the deed of sale and its warranty clause.
4. **Write to the seller**, with proof of receipt: the defect, the date of
   discovery, and your request that he come and see it for himself.
5. **Get an expert opinion** from a building expert, on anteriority and on the
   cost of the work.
6. **Consult a lawyer or a notary** on the mise en demeure and on the exact
   wording of your deed of sale.
7. **Log everything**: dates, calls, replies. That file is what will count in
   front of the judge, not your memory.

## Frequently asked questions

**I had an inspection done and the inspector saw nothing. Do I still have a
recourse?** Yes, in principle. Éducaloi points out that an inspection does not
guarantee the absence of a defect. Reread the report, though: if it recommended
a more thorough check that you did not carry out, that sentence will be held
against your diligence.

**Can I do the work and send the invoice to the seller?** That is the riskiest
scenario. Éducaloi asks you not to start the repairs at your own expense,
because the seller must be able to verify the defect, see the damage and repair
it himself. Only urgent repairs are an exception.

**The defect comes from the owner before last.** The warranty is passed along
the chain of sales, but an earlier sale made at the buyer's risk and peril
breaks it, the OACIQ notes. Ask the notary to check the earlier deeds before
choosing who you act against.
