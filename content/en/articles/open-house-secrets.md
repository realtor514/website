An open house looks like neutral ground. The door is open, the feature sheets
are on the counter, someone greets you and lets you wander. It is not neutral.
The person greeting you is there because a seller hired them, and anything you
say that day is information handed to the other side of the table. This is not
a trap. It is how real estate brokerage works in Quebec, and it takes ten
minutes to understand.

## Who is the person at the door

The OACIQ reminds brokers that a broker or an agency may advertise an open
house only if the seller's broker is present during that open house, or is able
to have another broker stand in. The reason is simple: it is the broker's duty
to protect and promote the interests of the seller, who is the client, and to
be there to give relevant and accurate information about the property.

The regulator adds that, for the security and protection of the property, the
broker should verify the identity of visitors and record their names in a
visitors' register. Identifying yourself on the way in is therefore expected
practice.

And at the earliest opportunity, that broker owes you two things: to ask
whether you are already represented by a broker, and to tell you at the same
moment that they represent the seller's interests and not yours.

## Represented, or treated fairly

The OACIQ sets out a buyer's options in three lines, and the distinction
deserves a slow read.

- Buyer's broker: represents the buyer, a brokerage contract to purchase is
  signed, defends the buyer's interests and advises the buyer.
- Seller's broker: represents the seller, and treats the buyer fairly.
- Collaborating broker without a brokerage contract to purchase: does not
  represent the buyer, and treats the buyer fairly.

Or, in the regulator's own shorthand: fair treatment equals no written
brokerage contract, which equals no representation, which equals no advice
based on your needs and criteria. For a broker to represent you, a written
brokerage contract has to exist. A broker who shows you the house, answers your
questions and seems genuinely likeable is still not representing you.

The OACIQ brochure puts it in two phrases. Fair treatment: objective
information relevant to completing a transaction, respecting the rights and
obligations of every party involved. Representation: strategic information, an
advisory role, and the ability to negotiate in your interest.

## What fair treatment still gets you

This is not nothing, and it should not be caricatured. A seller's broker who
treats you fairly must, among other things, inform and advise you objectively,
demonstrate the accuracy of the information provided to you, inform you of any
adverse factors affecting the property, and complete forms for you according to
your wishes and present them to the seller immediately.

And they cannot require any remuneration or fees from you: their remuneration
is set by the brokerage contract the seller signed.

## What they will not do, and that is not a flaw

The same regulator is equally clear about the limits. Without a brokerage
contract to purchase, the broker cannot advise you on the price, on comparable
homes sold, on the conditions, or on the drafting of clauses in the promise to
purchase, except for the usual inspection and financing clauses. They cannot
negotiate on your behalf to obtain a lower price or better terms based on your
criteria. They may, however, show you the property.

If you decide to make an offer through them anyway, the OACIQ describes the
role precisely: complete the promise to purchase with you without giving you
their opinion or advising you on the various elements, while informing you
objectively about the usual clauses to use, then present it to the seller while
remaining neutral and objective.

They also remain bound to their client by an obligation of loyalty. Unless the
seller instructs otherwise, the broker will keep certain information
confidential: the desired price, the negotiation margin, the reasons for the
sale. And they may in no case disclose the content of other promises to
purchase, or promote yours.

## What you should keep to yourself

Here is the heart of it. The confidentiality that shields the desired price and
the negotiation margin belongs to the seller, because the seller is the client.
Fair treatment does commit the broker to respect the confidentiality of
information entrusted to them, and to use the personal information they obtain
only for the purposes for which you gave it. That is a real protection, but a
narrow one: it does not turn that broker into your advisor.

So on the floor of an open house, avoid saying out loud how high you can go,
the date you absolutely have to move, or that this is exactly the house you
have been dreaming about. That is strategic information, and it travels in the
wrong direction.

What you can do instead is ask factual questions and listen closely to the
answers: age of the roof, heating source, work done with permits, known adverse
factors, documents available. The broker has to be able to demonstrate the
accuracy of what they tell you. Our [first visit
checklist](/en/articles/first-visit-property-checklist/) covers what to look at,
room by room.

## The form that puts all of this in writing

There is a recommended OACIQ form for exactly this situation: the Notice from
the seller's broker to the unrepresented buyer. It invents nothing. It states
in plain language that the agency or broker bound by a brokerage contract with
the seller does not represent and does not defend your interests, that you will
be treated fairly, and that you have been advised you could be represented by
the licence holder of your choice. You acknowledge receipt by signing it.

Its counterpart on the collaborating broker's side is the Notice from the
broker without a brokerage contract to purchase. The OACIQ attaches one
sentence to it that is worth memorizing: a collaborating broker without a
brokerage contract to purchase is de facto working for the seller.

## Double representation: banned since 2022

The amendments to the Real Estate Brokerage Act have been in force since June
10, 2022. They prohibit double representation and verbal brokerage contracts in
residential real estate transactions in Quebec. One broker cannot be bound by a
brokerage contract to both the seller and you, save in exceptional situations.

If it does happen, the OACIQ spells out the broker's path: inform the parties,
recommend that the buyer work with another broker, and terminate one of the two
contracts under the parameters of the regulations, with no remuneration
claimable after that termination.

## The remuneration trap

There is one more very practical reason to announce at the door that you have a
broker. Determining which broker first drew your interest to a specific
property is central to a broker's right to remuneration. It is called the
efficient cause of the sale. The rules are developed by the APCIQ, and the
OACIQ does not intervene in remuneration disputes between brokers or agencies.

Visiting alone with the seller's broker and then coming back with your own can
therefore create a dispute that is not yours but that lands on your
transaction. If you are already represented, the OACIQ advises telling your
broker before the open house. If they cannot come with you, they can notify the
seller's broker that you are coming. And if you could not reach them, say so on
arrival and hand over their business card.

## The short version

An open house is still an excellent way to see a lot of properties quickly. Go,
look at everything, ask precise questions. But walk in knowing who works for
whom, say immediately whether you are represented, and save your numbers and
your deadlines for the person who signed on to defend your interests. If
something worries you during the visit, reread [the red flags that justify
walking away](/en/articles/red-flags-walk-away-property/).
