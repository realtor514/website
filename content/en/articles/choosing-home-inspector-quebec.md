Nothing stops someone from calling themselves a home inspector tomorrow morning in Quebec. The trade has started to be regulated, but the certificate issued by the Regie du batiment du Quebec (Quebec's building authority, the RBQ) does not become mandatory until October 1, 2027. Until then, the vetting that no professional order does on your behalf is vetting you do yourself. Here is what to ask, and which answer to accept.

## Choosing a home inspector in Quebec: why the decision is yours

A real estate broker holds a licence issued by the OACIQ, the body that regulates real estate brokerage in Quebec. An architect and an engineer belong to a professional order that can strike them off. The home inspector, today, has neither a professional order nor a reserved title. Two people can charge the same amount for the same house with skills that have nothing in common.

That is why two buyers standing in front of the same property can receive two reports that look nothing alike. Price tells you nothing about this: for cost and timing, read [what an inspection costs and when to do it](/en/articles/home-inspection-cost-timing-quebec/). Here, one question only: how to choose the person.

## The RBQ certificate is voluntary until September 30, 2027

On February 14, 2024, the Conseil des ministres adopted the regulation governing inspectors of residential buildings. It came into force on October 1, 2024, with a three year transition period. During that period, an inspector already in practice may keep working without a certificate. From October 1, 2027, any inspection carried out as part of a real estate transaction will require a valid RBQ certificate.

So we are in the last year of the old regime: in 2026, the certified inspector already exists, and the uncertified inspector is perfectly legal.

The certificate comes in two categories. Category 1 covers buildings of one to six dwellings and the private portions of a co ownership. Category 2 covers every type of residential building. To obtain it, the RBQ requires a college attestation in building inspection, compliance with the BNQ 3009-500 standard, and professional liability insurance specific to inspection work: one million dollars in category 1, two million in category 2. The certificate is renewed every year.

A transitional route runs until August 2, 2027, and every version of it goes through refresher training. An inspector holding a college attestation begun in 2020 or later only has to pass that training. One without an attestation, or whose attestation predates 2020, must also document experience: three years within the last five for category 1, five years within the last eight for category 2, supported by insurance specific to the work of an inspector. Experience alone is never enough.

**So the question to ask is a simple one.** Do you already hold the RBQ certificate, and in which category? A no is not a disqualification in 2026, but it leaves you with every check that follows.

## What your broker must do, and what your broker cannot do

Section 81 of the Reglement sur les conditions d'exercice d'une operation de courtage, sur la deontologie des courtiers et sur la publicite, the regulation that sets out how brokers must practise and advertise, governs this. The broker must recommend a complete inspection of the building. And if the broker gives names, the broker must give more than one.

The recommended inspector has to meet four conditions:

1. carry professional liability insurance covering faults, errors and omissions;
2. use a recognized service agreement;
3. perform the inspection according to a recognized building inspection practice standard;
4. provide a written report.

The OACIQ has been explicit in public: no form of conflict of interest is tolerated, and a breach exposes the licence holder to revocation of that licence and to heavy fines.

**The conflict worth thinking about.** An inspector who gets most of his mandates from a single source has an economic interest in the relationship that sends them. That is not automatically a fault, and excellent inspectors have worked that way for twenty years. But the question gets asked out loud, the same way you ask it of a broker before hiring one: see the [five questions to ask before hiring a real estate broker](/en/articles/questions-hire-broker/). You are entitled to go and find a name that nobody in the transaction suggested to you.

## Professional liability insurance: ask for the certificate

This is the first of the four conditions, and it is the one that gets verified worst if you settle for a sentence on a website.

Two coverages are at stake: general civil liability insurance, and professional liability insurance for faults, errors and omissions. With an inspector certified by the RBQ, the minimum amounts are one million dollars in category 1 and two million in category 2. With an uncertified inspector, no minimum amount applies at all.

What you are asking for is not a statement, it is a certificate of insurance issued by the insurer, in the name of the inspector or the inspector's company, with the validity dates and the amount. A service contract that complies with the regulation names the insurer anyway.

Second question, less obvious: if a claim arrives two years from now over an inspection done today, does the policy in force at that moment respond? That depends on the type of insurance contract, and it is up to the inspector to explain it to you clearly.

## The service contract and the practice standard

A certified inspector who agrees to perform an inspection covered by the BNQ 3009-500 standard has to enter into a service contract with the client. That contract must contain the inspector's contact details, certificate number, insurer information, and the full address of the building inspected. It must also tell you in advance which elements will be assessed in the report, which avoids the painful argument afterwards about what was and was not supposed to be looked at.

The standard is BNQ 3009-500, Residential building, Inspection practices in a real estate transaction context. It was developed by the Bureau de normalisation du Quebec at the request of the RBQ, and it describes how the examination of observable systems and components is to be carried out. Ask which standard applies to your inspection, and have it written into the contract.

That leaves the question of associations. The AIBQ, one of Quebec's building inspector associations, asks a regular member for a college attestation in building inspection, liability and errors and omissions insurance taken out through its own broker, and a minimum of ten hours of recognized training a year. That is serious. It is not a state licence, and it is not the RBQ.

| What you ask for | What it proves | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| RBQ certificate | Training, exam, minimum insurance, the BNQ standard | That the report will be good |
| Association membership card | The conditions of that association | Public oversight |
| Certificate of insurance | Coverage in force, an amount | That a future claim will be paid |
| Written service contract | The agreed scope of the inspection | What will be found on site |

## The liability limitation clause: what the law says about it

Plenty of buyers sign, without reading it, a clause that caps any recourse against the inspector at the amount of the inspector's fee. Seven hundred dollars against a foundation that has to be redone. This is where disputes are decided, and this is where a widespread belief gets corrected.

According to Educaloi, the Quebec non profit that explains the law in plain language, an inspector's service contract may not contain a clause allowing the inspector to limit that liability. It may list the elements of the building that will not be inspected, which is a different thing: that is defining the mandate, not escaping its consequences.

Then there is the general rule. When you are a consumer dealing with a merchant, the law prohibits the merchant from limiting or excluding liability. Between private individuals, such a clause can only apply to material damage, and only if you knew it was there, if it is clearly written, and if you accepted it. It never protects someone who caused the damage intentionally or through gross carelessness, recklessness or negligence, and it never covers physical or psychological injury.

**What you do with that.** You read the contract before signing, not in the car. If a clause caps recourse at the fee, you ask for it to be removed and you note the answer. You keep your signed copy: in a dispute, it is the first document your lawyer will want to see. On the budget side, the inspection sits alongside the other costs to plan for before signing at the notary, which the [closing costs calculator](/en/tools/closing-costs/) puts numbers on.

## The questions to ask before hiring a home inspector

Ask them on the phone, before retaining anyone. The tone of the answers will teach you as much as their content.

- Do you hold the RBQ residential building inspector certificate, and in which category?
- Which practice standard do you follow, and is it named in the contract?
- Can you send me your professional liability insurance certificate, and the service contract, before the inspection?
- Does your contract contain a clause limiting your liability? If so, which one?
- How many inspections have you done on this type of building, a 1960 plex or a ten year old condo for example?
- Who sends you your mandates, and what share comes from real estate professionals?
- Can I attend the inspection from start to finish, and when does the written report arrive?

An inspector who sends you the documents without hesitating, before you have paid anything at all, has just answered half the questions.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can my broker give me a single inspector's name?**

No. Section 81 of the regulation requires a broker who provides references to give more than one. And the final choice stays yours, including the choice to take a name you found on your own.

**Is an inspector without the RBQ certificate risky in 2026?**

It is not illegal: the certificate only becomes mandatory on October 1, 2027. But its absence leaves every check on your shoulders: training, insurance, standard, contract. A certificate already obtained is one more verifiable fact.

**The inspector refuses to remove the limitation clause. What do I do?**

Choosing someone else is the simplest answer. If you want this inspector, keep the signed contract and talk to a lawyer before relying on that clause. I am not qualified to tell you whether a specific clause would hold up in court.

**Can I rely on the inspection report supplied by the seller?**

Read it, you will learn things from it. But it was ordered and paid for by the selling side, under a mandate you did not define. An inspection done for you is still the only one whose scope you control.
