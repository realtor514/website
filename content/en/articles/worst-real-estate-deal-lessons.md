A Quebec residential transaction almost never breaks for an original reason. It
breaks in the same places, each covered by a written rule: an article of the
Civil Code, a clause of the mandatory OACIQ form, a 15 day deadline. Almost
always, the cause is a document requested too late.

## 1. A charge that surfaces at the notary

Article 1723 of the Civil Code is blunt: the seller must warrant the property
free of all rights except those he declared, and must discharge the hypothecs
charging it. Clause 10.3 of the promise to purchase repeats it. The notary is
the one who checks, through the title search at the Land Register.

**The legal hypothec of the syndicate of co-owners.** Article 2724 limits legal
hypothecs to four claims, including the syndicate's claim for common expenses.
Under article 2729, it charges the fraction of a co-owner in default for more
than 30 days, from the registration of a notice covering the amount due that
day plus the charges for the current year and the two that follow.

**The legal hypothec of construction.** Article 2726 reserves it for those who
took part in work requested by the owner, from the architect to the
subcontractor, with no publication needed. Under article 2727, it subsists for
30 days after the work is completed, is preserved by a notice registered within
that period and served on the owner, and lapses six months after that end.

**Unpaid municipal and school taxes.** These are prior claims, article 2651, 5°,
and article 2654.1 makes them a real right that follows the immovable into
whosever hands it passes: they follow the property, not the seller.

And the closing date? Clause 10.5 governs it: an irregularity of title
disclosed before signing gives the seller 21 days to notify in writing that he
is remedying it at his expense or not, then 5 days for the buyer to buy anyway
or void the promise. A charge found just before closing therefore moves the
signing; the costs of radiation stay with the seller, clause 10.4.

## 2. The co-ownership file nobody asked for

Take a typical situation: a special assessment voted a few weeks before the
listing, discovered at the notary by a buyer who asked for no documents, when
it had appeared in a set of minutes long before his offer. Three articles
answer this, and all are asked for early.

- **The syndicate's certificate on the state of the co-ownership**, article
  1068.1: the seller gives it to the promisor buyer in due course, and the
  syndicate produces it within 15 days. Its content is set by the regulation in
  force since 14 August 2025.
- **Documents and information**, article 1068.2: the promisor buyer may ask the
  syndicate for what lets him give informed consent, and the syndicate must
  provide it diligently, at the buyer's expense.
- **The statement of common expenses due**, article 1069: the acquirer owes all
  expenses due at the time of acquisition, with interest, unless he asked for
  the statement and the syndicate provided it within 15 days.

Add the Request for information to the syndicate of co-owners, a recommended
OACIQ form whose question 3.4 is the one people forget: are there assessments
voted and not yet payable, or expected?

If those documents do not arrive before the promise is drafted, clause 9.1
makes it conditional on your review of them: the seller delivers them within
the period written in, and you have seven days after it expires to notify in
writing, failing which you are deemed to have waived the condition. The
maintenance logbook and the fund study are covered in [condo fees and the
contingency fund](/en/articles/condo-fees-investment-killer/).

## 3. A certificate of location that no longer tells the truth

Clause 10.3 requires the seller to provide a certificate reflecting the current
physical state of the premises, and the form gives the examples: heat pump,
deck, fence, shed, pool. An addition, a pool or a shed added afterwards leaves
it silent on what matters.

The Chambre des notaires has settled the question of age: a lifespan of less
than 10 years is an essential criterion for the useful life of a certificate.
It adds that a more recent certificate may also no longer describe the current
state, and that the officiating notary's judgment remains necessary. Providing
it is the seller's obligation and his cost, and the delay is the real problem:
the OACIQ puts preparation at four to six weeks on average, and the 2026
suggested fee of the Ordre des arpenteurs-géomètres du Québec places an
ordinary residential certificate at around $1,630 plus taxes. Ask for its date
at the first visit.

## 4. The inspection condition mishandled

This one is entirely procedural. If the inspection reveals a factor capable of
significantly reducing the value or income of the immovable or increasing its
expenses, clause 8.1 requires the buyer to notify the seller in writing and
give him a copy of the report, no later than the fourth day following the
expiry of the inspection period, before 8 p.m.

Three details decide the file: the notice goes to the seller, it comes with the
full report and not an excerpt, and it arrives before 8 p.m. on the right day.
Otherwise the buyer is deemed to have waived the condition. And the notice ends
the promise the moment it is received, so any renegotiation happens before you
send it. The rest is in [pre-purchase inspection: what it costs and when to do
it](/en/articles/home-inspection-cost-timing-quebec/).

## 5. The financing condition treated as a formality

A pre-approval is not an approval. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada puts
it plainly: the pre-approved amount is a maximum, it does not guarantee the
loan, and a lender can refuse a pre-approved buyer. Before approving, the
lender verifies that the property meets certain standards, and the approved
amount will depend on its value and on your down payment.

If the lender's appraisal puts that value below the accepted price, the loan
follows the value, not your offer. CMHC then recommends talking to your lender
and your broker to look at the options.

Clause 6.2 requires you to give the seller, within the period written in, a
copy of a lender's commitment to grant the loan described in clause 6.1.
Without that proof, clause 6.3 gives the seller five days to require a new
application with a lender he designates or to void the promise; otherwise it
becomes void anyway.

## 6. Latent defects, and the sale at the buyer's risk

Article 1726 requires the seller to warrant the property free of latent
defects, but he warrants neither a latent defect known to the buyer nor an
apparent defect: one a prudent and diligent buyer can discover without an
expert.

Article 1732 allows the parties to reduce that warranty or exclude it entirely,
though the seller can never be released from his personal acts: that is clause
12.1, sale without legal warranty, at the buyer's risk. But article 1733
forbids him to exclude or limit his liability if he did not disclose defects he
knew of or could not have been unaware of, except where the buyer buys at his
own risk from a non professional seller.

Even then, he must answer the Declarations by the seller of the immovable form
accurately: a false answer is a personal act, not silence. And article 1739
requires a buyer who discovers a defect to give written notice within a
reasonable time. The other red flags you can verify on paper are
[here](/en/articles/red-flags-walk-away-property/).

## 7. The deposit mistaken for a right to walk

Many buyers believe they can give up their deposit and walk. Article 1711 says
the opposite: any sum paid in connection with a promise of sale is presumed to
be a deposit on the price, unless the contract provides otherwise. It is not
forfeit money, and article 1712 adds that failure to pass title lets the other
party obtain a judgment standing in its place.

Clause 4.3 follows the money: remitted to the broker acting as trustee, placed
in trust, then applied to the price at the notary; if the promise becomes null
and void, it is refunded to the depositor without interest, on written request.
Getting out of a promise is therefore not paying to leave, it is invoking a
condition within the period and in the manner provided, as [buying with cash in
Quebec](/en/articles/cash-buyers-advantage/) explains.

## What to actually do

**Documents to ask for early**, before the offer if possible, otherwise by
naming them in clause 9.1: the certificate of location with its date, the
Declarations by the seller, the municipal and school tax bills, the permits and
invoices for recent work. In a co-ownership, add the syndicate's certificate,
two years of minutes, the financial statements, the maintenance logbook, the
fund study and the statement of common expenses due.

**Who to bring in.** The notary first, chosen before you sign the promise so
his name appears in it, since he is the one who examines the titles and settles
the charges out of the sale proceeds: [his work follows a precise
order](/en/articles/quebec-notary-process/). A building inspector, present with
you. Your lender or a mortgage broker certified by the Autorité des marchés
financiers. A lawyer as soon as a dispute appears over title or a defect.

None of these seven failures calls for instinct. Each is settled by a dated
document, a written notice and a deadline respected.
