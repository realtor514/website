A sunroom gets shopped for like a piece of furniture: a model, a frame colour, an
installation date. The Régie du bâtiment du Québec classifies it differently. For
the board, "the construction of a sunroom is an increase in floor area." That one
word changes the permit, the distances to respect, the structure that has to be
calculated, and the tax bill that will follow.

## A sunroom or a veranda is an extension

The RBQ is explicit: because the sunroom forms an integral part of the building, a
new installation has to comply with the requirements of the Code de construction
du Québec, chapter I, Bâtiment, and the board specifies that the Code's
requirements apply even when the work is an addition to an existing building.

Ville de Laval says the same thing on the municipal side: adding a sunroom or a
veranda requires a permis de construction-amélioration, a construction and
improvement permit, before the work begins, and "this type of addition is
considered an extension of the main building." The city in fact lists it among its
examples of an extension, alongside adding a second storey and an attached garage.

**The sentence that should decide everything.** The RBQ writes that there is no
acquired right where compliance with the regulations is concerned, and that a
sunroom that did not comply with the regulations in force when it was built will
have to be modified or demolished. Time regularizes nothing.

## Three season or four season: the line that changes the permit

Laval defines the solarium as a glazed room with no permanent insulating
component, and the véranda as an uninsulated glazed room, seasonal, generally
unheated, often built on an existing deck.

Heating is the real border. The RBQ provides that when the sunroom is heated, its
doors and windows must comply with the Code's requirements on resistance to
intrusion and air leakage. And if the permit application was filed after August
30, 2012, or if construction began after November 28, 2012, the heated sunroom
has to comply with part 11 of the Bâtiment chapter, insofar as the building area
after the work is no more than 600 square metres, the height no more than 3
storeys and the building houses only dwellings. Beyond that, it is the National
Energy Code for Buildings 2015 as amended for Quebec.

| What you are adding | What defines it | The treatment observed |
|---|---|---|
| Three season sunroom | Glazed room with no permanent insulation | Construction permit, 30 day announced timeline in Laval |
| Four season sunroom | Heated and insulated, usable in winter | Construction permit, 50 day announced timeline in Laval |
| Veranda | Uninsulated glazed room, seasonal, unheated | Construction permit, extension of the main building in Laval |
| Permanent shelter | Uninsulated, unheated space, attached without forming part of the building | Certificate of authorization in the borough of Rivière-des-Prairies-Pointe-aux-Trembles |

## Setbacks, lot coverage and floor area

A heated sunroom adds floor area, and that area counts toward what the lot can
carry. Montreal sums up the standards for an extension in one line: respect the
maximum lot coverage of the building on the land. The city adds that to extend an
area or a height, you may be limited depending on the intended use, in particular
under acquired rights.

Distances, for their part, are read by-law by by-law. In the borough of
Rivière-des-Prairies-Pointe-aux-Trembles, the Info-permis sheet on permanent
shelters gives benchmarks: the shelter cannot be installed in a yard located in
front of a facade facing a public road, it must respect the setbacks in the zoning
by-law, and it cannot be erected less than 1.5 metres from a lot line. Its maximum
height is 3 metres from the floor of the projection, and 5 metres from the ground.
Above 12 square metres, or under 80% glazing, the project goes to the site
planning and architectural integration committee. Those numbers belong to that
borough: they hold neither for Anjou, nor for Laval, nor for your street.

## A sunroom permit in Laval, an extension permit in Montreal

**In Laval.** The published fee is $92 for the first $20,000, plus $2.50 per
$1,000 bracket or part of a bracket. The announced timelines are 30 days for a
three season sunroom and 50 days for a four season one, and the city specifies
that the clock only starts once every required document has been received. On the
list: a scale site plan, a certificate of location prepared, mandatorily, by a
land surveyor, a complete architectural plan, a foundation plan. Before any of
that, check in Info-règlements whether your address is subject to a PIIA, a site
planning and architectural integration programme. For an extension to a single
family dwelling, Laval also requires a plan signed and sealed by an architect
under section 16 of the Loi sur les architectes where that applies, and a
structural plan signed and sealed by an engineer.

**In Montreal.** There is no single rule: the city refers you explicitly to the
borough. The common analysis fee is $9.80 per $1,000 of work, and several boroughs
post a minimum of $167.40 for a residential building. The basic documents: a
certificate of location, a scale plan of the proposed siting, a building data
sheet and structural plans prepared by an engineer. In
Rivière-des-Prairies-Pointe-aux-Trembles, you also need a document signed by a
member of the Ordre des ingénieurs du Québec confirming that the structure can
support the loads and live loads provided for in the Code. The fine for a first
offence there runs from $100 to $300 for a natural person, double for a legal
person. And [the constraints winter puts on a renovation
permit](/en/articles/winter-renovation-permits-montreal-laval/) add to those
timelines.

## Structure, snow loads and the contractor's licence

This is the part the owner sees least and the inspector sees first. The RBQ is
precise: footings must be frost-proof, and the roof structure must be designed to
resist the various loads described in the Code, including wind and snow, whether
it is of a conventional type or not. Load bearing elements must have the fire
resistance required for the building, combustible construction is permitted only
if the building itself may be so, and the sunroom must be fitted with the same
fire protection equipment as the building, which can mean sprinklers.

The licence is not optional either: the RBQ writes that building a sunroom
requires a general or specialized contractor's licence. How to verify it:
[choosing a renovation
contractor](/en/articles/choosing-renovation-contractor-quebec/).

## The notice of alteration to the roll, and the added value nobody quantifies

Montreal puts it in black and white: the Loi sur la fiscalité municipale, the
municipal taxation act, provides that property assessment rolls must be kept up to
date to reflect the changes made to properties, and extension work is among the
work that can bring a rise in value. Conversely, regular maintenance such as
redoing the roof or painting will probably have no effect.

Two details change the budget. The update is retroactive to the date of the change
or the date the work ended, without going back further than the fiscal year
preceding the current one. And you receive a notice of alteration explaining the
changes and the reasons. To contest it in Montreal, the deadline is the later of
May 1 following the coming into force of the roll and the sixtieth day following
the sending of the notice; fees start at $90 up to $500,000 of value, and the next
recourse goes to the real estate division of the Tribunal administratif du Québec,
Quebec's administrative tribunal. The procedure is in [contesting your municipal
assessment](/en/articles/contest-property-assessment-quebec/), and the effect on
the bill in [property taxes in Montreal and
Laval](/en/articles/property-tax-explained/).

**On added value, no official source quantifies it.** No Quebec public body
publishes a recovery percentage for a sunroom. What is documented is the other
side: the value on the roll goes up, so the taxes do too. And that value is
established according to the market 18 months before the roll comes into force,
that is July 1, 2024 for the Montreal 2026-2027-2028 rolls. It is not a resale
price, and a [home value estimator](/en/tools/home-estimate/) remains a starting
point.

## Insurer, certificate of location and seller's declarations

The Bureau d'assurance du Canada recommends notifying your insurer before the work
starts, so it can adjust the protections for the duration of the project, and
points out that you always need an amount of insurance that reflects the real
value of the house. If you carry out the work yourself, an endorsement can cover
your materials, but no contractor may then be directing the site.

The certificate of location is the point where an undeclared sunroom blocks a
sale. The OACIQ points out that the seller must provide a certificate describing
the current state of the premises, that a certificate more than ten years old has
to be redone because of the prescription in article 2917 of the Civil Code, that
you should count on four to six weeks on average to obtain a new one, and that the
notary must review it at least twenty days before the deed is signed. I see that
timeline blow up notary dates every spring.

The Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble form, the seller's declarations on the
property (DV 00001, version 06/2022), closes the loop. Section D14.4 asks whether
significant work has been carried out and, if so, whether the required permits
were issued for each of those jobs. Section D2.11 does not name the sunroom, but it
covers the addition or modification of, in particular, porches, decks "or others,"
which is exactly the projection a sunroom gets built on, and a positive answer can
require a new certificate of location. The detail is in [the seller's declaration
in Quebec](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/).

## Frequently asked questions

**My three season sunroom is not heated. Do I still need a permit?** In Laval,
yes: the city requires a permis de construction-amélioration for a sunroom as for
a veranda. In Montreal, the answer depends on the borough and on the type of
structure, an unheated permanent shelter going through a certificate of
authorization in Rivière-des-Prairies-Pointe-aux-Trembles.

**Does a sunroom raise my municipal assessment?** Montreal names extension work
among the work that can bring a rise in value, and the update is retroactive to
the date the work ended. You will receive a notice of alteration. How much, only
your city's assessor can say.

**I bought a house with a sunroom added by the previous owner. What do I check?**
The permit or certificate of authorization issued by the municipality, and the
presence of the sunroom on the certificate of location. If it does not appear
there, the land surveyor will have to redo one, and compliance becomes your
problem: there is no acquired right in this area.

**Can I build it myself?** The RBQ writes that building a sunroom requires a
general or specialized contractor's licence. Validate your case with the board and
your insurer before you buy anything.

municipal assessment in
Quebec](/en/articles/contest-property-assessment-quebec/).
