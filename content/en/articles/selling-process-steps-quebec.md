Between the first meeting with a broker and the signing at the notaire, a
residential sale goes through about ten steps. Each one is attached to a
specific document, and it is that document, not the habits of the parties, that
decides what you have to do and within what deadline. Here is the real sequence,
form by form.

## The process of selling a house in Quebec starts with two forms

The first meeting commits you to nothing. The day you sign, you fill out two
documents.

The first is the contrat de courtage vente (the listing brokerage contract), a
mandatory OACIQ form. Its term is not imposed on you: you are the one who writes
in the end date, and if that box is left blank, the contract ends 30 days after
it is entered into. Read
[the listing brokerage contract clause by clause](/en/articles/listing-brokerage-contract-quebec/)
before you sign.

The second is the *Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble* form, the DV. The
OACIQ guideline is explicit: the broker fills it out with you when the contrat
de courtage is taken, then annexes it to the fiche descriptive (the listing
sheet) and to any promesse d'achat (promise to purchase) received. He has to
question you whenever an answer is vague, obtain the invoices and permits that
support your declarations, and update it as soon as something new comes up. What
you write there follows the file all the way to the notaire:
[the DV form in detail](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/).

## The certificat de localisation, the item that delays the most files

**As soon as the contrat de courtage is signed**, the broker has to verify
whether you hold a certificat de localisation (a land surveyor's certificate of
location), how old it is, what has been added since it was issued, and examine
its content. If there is none, if it is no longer valid, or if it does not
reflect the current state of the premises, he has to recommend that you have a
new one produced.

Why so early. Because clause 10.3 of the *Promesse d'achat* form requires you to
give the buyer an authentic copy of your deed of acquisition and of your
certificat de localisation, and that certificate must reflect any cadastral
operation or renovation, the current physical state of the premises, private law
limitations such as a servitude, and public law ones. The form gives the
examples: heat pump, deck, fence, shed, pool. A buyer who wants a different
certificate pays for it.

A certificate more than ten years old raises a separate problem: the acquisitive
prescription period under article 2917 of the Code civil is 10 years, and the
OACIQ ranks a stale certificate among the situations to raise as soon as the
contract is taken. Having a new certificate produced by an arpenteur-géomètre
(land surveyor) takes time, and that time varies. That is why it is settled when
the contrat de courtage is signed, not the day a promesse d'achat arrives.

## Fiche descriptive, exposure and showings: the actual listing

The fiche descriptive is the first source of information the public consults.
The OACIQ is clear: the information published must be accurate, verified and up
to date, and the broker has to be able to demonstrate that it is accurate. He
verifies it before publication, not after.

**The listing sheet is not frozen.** As soon as a relevant fact is brought to
the broker's attention, an inspection report, an expert opinion, a special
assessment, he has to correct the fiche and the Déclarations du vendeur using a
*Modifications* form.

Then come the distribution to the inter-agency services, the showings and the
feedback. The number of showings and the time before a first promesse d'achat
vary from one area to the next and from one month to the next; no average
applies to your property. Before you set the asking price, situate your starting
point with a [home value estimate](/en/tools/home-estimate/). On timing, read
[what makes selling time vary](/en/articles/how-long-to-sell-house-montreal/).

## The promesse d'achat: accept, refuse, or counter

The buyer binds himself irrevocably until an hour and a date he writes in
himself at clause 14.1. If you do not accept within that window, the promise
becomes null. The form adds two sentences many sellers discover too late: any
refusal on your part renders the promise null and void, and any counter-proposal
has the same effect as a refusal.

In practice, you cannot counter and then go back to the initial offer. Each
counter-proposal cancels the previous ones, and the last one has to list every
item included and excluded that was absent from the initial promise. The
agreement that binds you will be the promise as modified by that last
counter-proposal.

The OACIQ recommends responding to every promise received, even to refuse it.
The broker, for his part, has to present all of them to you.

## The buyer's conditions, and the written deadline behind each one

An accepted promise is not a sale. It opens a period during which the buyer
carries out his conditions, each with its own clock. Deadlines left blank are
negotiated and vary from one transaction to the next; those printed on the form
are not negotiable.

| Step | Document | What sets the deadline |
|---|---|---|
| End of the contrat de courtage | Contrat de courtage, clause 2.1 | The date you write in, 30 days if the box is blank |
| Certificat de localisation | Norme de pratique, article 2 | Verified as soon as the contract is signed; production time varies |
| Response to the promise | Promesse d'achat, clause 14.1 | The hour and the date written by the buyer |
| Inspection | Promesse d'achat, clause 8.1 | Negotiated deadline, then 4 days for the notice and the report, before 8 p.m. |
| Financing | Promesse d'achat, clauses 6.2 and 6.3 | Negotiated deadline, then 5 days for the seller to react |
| Review of documents | Promesse d'achat, clause 9.1 | Negotiated deadline, then 7 days for the buyer's notice |
| Defect disclosed before the deed | Promesse d'achat, clause 10.5 | 21 days to remedy, then 5 days for the buyer |
| Deed of sale | Promesse d'achat, clause 11.1 | The date written into the promise |

**The inspection.** Clause 8.1 makes the promise conditional on the buyer having
the property inspected within an agreed number of days. If the inspection
reveals a factor likely to significantly reduce the value or the income of the
property, or to increase its expenses, the buyer has to notify you in writing
and give you a copy of the report no later than the fourth day following the
expiry of that period, before 8 p.m. The promise then becomes null as soon as
you receive that notice together with the report. If he does not give notice
within the period provided, he is deemed to have waived the condition. A
negative report therefore opens a way out, not an automatic price reduction: an
adjustment goes through a new signed document.

**The financing.** The buyer undertakes to make good faith efforts to obtain his
loan, and has to provide you within the agreed period with a copy of a lender's
commitment. That commitment often depends on the appraisal the lender has done
on the property, which is the lender's own and not the market's. If there is no
commitment, you have five days after the expiry of the period to react in
writing: either require a new application to a lender you designate, or render
the promise null. If you do nothing within those five days, it becomes null
anyway.

**The documents.** At clause 9.1, you deliver the listed documents within the
agreed period. A buyer who is not satisfied, or who has not received them, has
to notify you in writing within seven days following the expiry of that period.
These notices are recorded on the *Avis et suivi de réalisation de conditions*
form (notice and follow-up on the fulfilment of conditions), so that neither
party can claim later to have received nothing.

## The last step of the selling process: at the notaire

Once the conditions are fulfilled, the deed of sale is signed before the notaire
identified at clause 11.1, on or before the date written in. The notaire first
examines the titles. He verifies that the title is unassailable, that you are
the true owner, that you have the right and the capacity to sell, whether your
spouse has to consent, and what mortgage charges encumber the property. With the
certificat de localisation, he verifies the dimensions of the land, that the
building is indeed erected on the lot being sold, compliance with zoning,
encroachments and rights of way.

Two articles of the Code civil govern this step. Article 1719 requires you to
hand over the titles you hold, a copy of your deed of acquisition, of the
previous titles and of the certificat de localisation. Article 1723 requires you
to warrant that the property is free of all rights except those you have
declared, and to discharge it of the hypothecs that encumber it. That is where
the release comes from: your lender has to consent to the cancellation of its
hypothec.

He then calculates the adjustments provided at clause 11.3: general and special
property taxes, fuel reserves, income and expenses, as of the date of signing or
of occupancy. Clause 11.4 gives him the irrevocable instruction to pay the
broker's remuneration out of the sums coming to you, after the prior and
hypothecary claims. And he holds the funds until the deed is registered at the
Bureau de la publicité des droits (the land registry office).

**What the seller brings.** Identification, deed of acquisition, certificat de
localisation, the latest municipal and school tax bills, the mortgage balance
statement, rental contracts taken over by the buyer under clause 11.7, invoices
for the inclusions, banking information, and the spouse's consent if the
property is the family residence. Until occupancy, you remain responsible for
keeping the premises in the state they were in at the time of the showings:
[how the signing at the notaire unfolds](/en/articles/quebec-notary-process/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I refuse a promesse d'achat with no consequences?**
A refusal renders the promise null and void, and you do not have to give a
reason. But if it complied with the conditions written into your contrat de
courtage, the refusal makes the broker's remuneration payable. Have the
compliance verified before you answer.

**The inspection turned up a problem. Can the buyer demand a price reduction?**
No, not on the basis of clause 8.1, which only allows him to render the promise
null by notifying you in writing with a copy of the report within the period
provided. A price adjustment is a separate agreement, and you are free to refuse
it.

**Does a defect discovered after the conditions are lifted cancel the sale?**
Not automatically. Clause 10.5 gives you 21 days from the written notice to
remedy the defect at your expense or to advise that you will not remedy it. The
buyer then has five days to buy anyway, with the declarations reduced
accordingly, or to render the promise null. Have a notaire read the notice
before you answer.
