Between the first meeting with a broker and the signing at the notary, a
residential sale goes through nine steps. Each one is attached to a specific
document, and it is that document, not the habits of the parties, that decides
what you have to do and within what deadline. Here is the real sequence, form
by form.

## The 9 steps to sell a house in Quebec

1. **Sign the contrat de courtage vente**, a mandatory OACIQ form.
2. **Fill out the Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble** (DV) with the broker.
3. **Check the certificat de localisation** and gather the titles.
4. **Set the asking price** from objective elements and comparables.
5. **Publish the listing sheet** and organize the showings.
6. **Respond to promises to purchase**: accept, refuse or counter.
7. **Follow the fulfilment of the buyer's conditions**: inspection, financing,
   review of documents.
8. **Prepare the documents for the notary**, including the mortgage balance.
9. **Sign the deed of sale at the notary** and vacate the premises on the
   occupancy date.

## The process of selling a house in Quebec starts with two forms

The first meeting commits you to nothing. The day you sign, you fill out two
documents.

The first is the contrat de courtage vente (the listing brokerage contract), a
mandatory OACIQ form. Its term is not imposed on you: you are the one who writes
in the end date, and if that box is left blank, the contract ends 30 days after
it is entered into. Read
[the listing brokerage contract clause by clause](/en/articles/listing-brokerage-contract-quebec/)
before you sign.

The second is the *Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble* form, the DV. The
OACIQ guideline is explicit: the broker fills it out with you when the listing
contract is taken, then annexes it to the fiche descriptive (the listing sheet)
and to any promise to purchase received. The broker has to question you
whenever an answer is vague, obtain the invoices and permits that support your
declarations, and update it as soon as something new comes up. What you write
there follows the file all the way to the notary:
[the DV form in detail](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/).

## What the law requires of the seller

Article 1716 of the Civil Code sets the base: the seller is bound to deliver the
property and to warrant its ownership and quality. These warranties exist by
operation of law, without having to be stipulated in the contract. The
*Promesse d'achat* form repeats it at clause 10.1: the property is sold with the
legal warranty of ownership and quality, unless otherwise stipulated at clause
12.1. A sale [without the legal
warranty](/en/articles/sale-without-legal-warranty-quebec/) therefore has to be
written down.

Article 1719 requires the seller to hand over the titles, a copy of the deed of
acquisition, of the previous titles and of the certificat de localisation in the
seller's possession. Article 1723 requires the seller to warrant that the
property is free of all rights except those declared. These three articles
explain the order of the steps that follow.

## The certificat de localisation, the item that delays the most files

**As soon as the listing contract is signed**, the broker has to verify whether
you hold a certificat de localisation (a land surveyor's certificate of
location), how old it is, what has been added since it was issued, and examine
its content. If there is none, if it is no longer valid, or if it does not
reflect the current state of the premises, the broker has to recommend that you
have a new one produced.

Why so early? Because clause 10.3 of the *Promesse d'achat* form requires you to
give the buyer an authentic copy of your deed of acquisition and of your
certificat de localisation, and that certificate must reflect any cadastral
operation or renovation, the current physical state of the premises, private law
limitations such as a servitude, and public law ones. The form gives the
examples: heat pump, deck, fence, shed, pool. A buyer who wants a different
certificate pays for it.

A certificate more than ten years old raises a separate problem: the acquisitive
prescription period under article 2917 of the Civil Code is 10 years, and the
OACIQ ranks a stale certificate among the situations to raise as soon as the
contract is taken. Having a new certificate produced by an arpenteur-géomètre
(land surveyor) takes time, and that time varies. That is why it is settled when
the listing contract is signed, not the day a promise to purchase arrives.

## Listing sheet, exposure and showings: the actual listing

The asking price is set before publication. Under the OACIQ guideline, the
broker helps the seller establish market value from objective elements and
comparables (section 76 of the brokerage regulation). Situate your starting
point with a [home value estimate](/en/tools/home-estimate/).

The fiche descriptive is the first source of information the public consults.
The OACIQ is clear: the information published must be accurate, verified and up
to date, and the broker has to be able to demonstrate that it is accurate. It is
verified before publication, not after.

**The listing sheet is not frozen.** As soon as a relevant fact is brought to
the broker's attention, an inspection report, an expert opinion, a special
assessment, the broker has to correct the fiche and the Déclarations du vendeur
using a *Modifications* form.

Then come the distribution to the inter-agency services, the showings and the
feedback.

## How long it takes to sell a house in Quebec

The total time is made up of three distinct periods.

**Before the first accepted promise.** The APCIQ's Centris statistics publish
the average number of days on the market every month, by area and by property
type. In September 2026:

| Area | Single-family | Condo |
|---|---|---|
| Montreal metropolitan area | 41 days | 65 days |
| Laval | 44 days | 57 days |

These averages change from month to month and do not predict the time for a
specific property: the asking price, condition and area make it vary. See
[what makes selling time vary in Laval](/en/articles/how-long-to-sell-house-laval/)
and [in Montreal](/en/articles/how-long-to-sell-house-montreal/).

**Between acceptance and the lifting of conditions.** These are the deadlines
written into the promise to purchase, detailed below, plus the deadlines printed
on the form.

**Until the deed of sale.** This is the date written at clause 11.1, negotiated
with the buyer, and the occupancy date at clause 11.2.

## The promise to purchase: accept, refuse, or counter

The buyer is bound irrevocably until an hour and a date the buyer writes in at
clause 14.1. If you do not accept within that window, the promise becomes null.
The form adds two sentences many sellers discover too late: any refusal on your
part renders the promise null and void, and any counter-proposal has the same
effect as a refusal.

In practice, you cannot counter and then go back to the initial offer. Each
counter-proposal cancels the previous ones, and the last one has to list every
item included and excluded that was absent from the initial promise. The
agreement that binds you will be the promise as modified by that last
counter-proposal.

The OACIQ recommends responding to every promise received, even to refuse it.
The broker, for their part, has to present all of them to you.

## The buyer's conditions, and the written deadline behind each one

An accepted promise is not a sale. It opens a period during which the buyer
carries out the conditions, each with its own clock. Deadlines left blank are
negotiated and vary from one transaction to the next; those printed on the form
are not negotiable.

| Step | Document | What sets the deadline |
|---|---|---|
| End of the listing contract | Contrat de courtage, clause 2.1 | The date you write in, 30 days if the box is blank |
| Certificat de localisation | Norme de pratique, article 2 | Verified as soon as the contract is signed; production time varies |
| Response to the promise | Promesse d'achat, clause 14.1 | The hour and the date written by the buyer |
| Inspection | Promesse d'achat, clause 8.1 | Negotiated deadline, then 4 days for the notice and the report, before 8 p.m. |
| Financing | Promesse d'achat, clauses 6.2 and 6.3 | Negotiated deadline, then 5 days for the seller to react |
| Review of documents | Promesse d'achat, clause 9.1 | Negotiated deadline, then 7 days for the buyer's notice |
| Defect disclosed before the deed | Promesse d'achat, clause 10.5 | 21 days to remedy, then 5 days for the buyer |
| Deed of sale | Promesse d'achat, clause 11.1 | The date written into the promise |

**The inspection.** Clause 8.1 makes the promise conditional on the buyer having
the property inspected within an agreed number of days. If the inspection
reveals a factor likely to significantly reduce the value or the income of the
property, or to increase its expenses, the buyer has to notify you in writing
and give you a copy of the report no later than the fourth day following the
expiry of that period, before 8 p.m. The promise then becomes null as soon as
you receive that notice together with the report. If the buyer does not give
notice within the period provided, the condition is deemed waived. A negative
report therefore opens a way out, not an automatic price reduction: an
adjustment goes through a new signed document.

**The financing.** The buyer undertakes to make good faith efforts to obtain the
loan, and has to provide you within the agreed period with a copy of a lender's
commitment. That commitment often depends on the appraisal the lender has done
on the property, which is the lender's own and not the market's. If there is no
commitment, you have five days after the expiry of the period to react in
writing: either require a new application to a lender you designate, or render
the promise null. If you do nothing within those five days, it becomes null
anyway.

**The documents.** At clause 9.1, you deliver the listed documents within the
agreed period. A buyer who is not satisfied, or who has not received them, has
to notify you in writing within seven days following the expiry of that period.
These notices are recorded on the *Avis et suivi de réalisation de conditions*
form (notice and follow-up on the fulfilment of conditions), so that neither
party can claim later to have received nothing.

**A defect discovered after the conditions are lifted.** The sale is not
cancelled automatically. Clause 10.5 gives you 21 days from the written notice
to remedy the defect at your expense or to advise that you will not remedy it.
The buyer then has five days to buy anyway or to render the promise null. Have a
notary read the notice before you answer.

**If you have to buy at the same time.** The form has no printed box for a
condition tied to the sale of the buyer's property, or of yours. Such a
condition, if negotiated, is written at clause 12.1 or in an annex, and the
occupancy date at clause 11.2 can be aligned with your own purchase. The options
are set out in [selling before buying: the
clauses](/en/articles/sell-before-buying-clauses-quebec/).

## Selling a house that still has a mortgage

This is the most common case, and it does not block the sale. Article 1723
requires the seller to discharge the property of the hypothecs that encumber
it. Clause 11.4 of the promise gives the notary the irrevocable instruction to
pay the broker's remuneration out of the sums coming to you, after payment of
all prior and hypothecary claims and of the notary's fees, notably for the
cancellation of those claims. The lender is therefore repaid out of the sale
price.

**The prepayment penalty.** According to the Financial Consumer Agency of
Canada, a lender may charge a penalty if you pay back your entire mortgage
before the end of the term, including when you sell your home. It is usually the
higher of two amounts: three months' interest on what you still owe, or the
interest rate differential. If you are buying another property, ask the lender
whether the mortgage can be ported, which keeps the rate and the terms. Ask for
the calculation in writing before you set your net price:
[breaking a mortgage and the penalty](/en/articles/breaking-mortgage-penalty-quebec/).

## The last step of the selling process: at the notary

Once the conditions are fulfilled, the deed of sale is signed before the notary
identified at clause 11.1, on or before the date written in. The notary first
examines the titles: that the title is unassailable, that you are the true
owner, that you have the right and the capacity to sell, whether your spouse has
to consent, and what mortgage charges encumber the property. With the
certificat de localisation, the notary verifies the dimensions of the land, that
the building is indeed erected on the lot being sold, compliance with zoning,
encroachments and rights of way.

The notary then calculates the adjustments provided at clause 11.3: general and
special property taxes, fuel reserves, income and expenses, as of the date of
signing or of occupancy. The notary obtains the lender's release, consenting to
the cancellation of its hypothec, and holds the funds until the deed is
registered at the Bureau de la publicité des droits (the land registry office).

**What the seller brings.** Identification, deed of acquisition, certificat de
localisation, the latest municipal and school tax bills, the mortgage balance
statement, rental contracts taken over by the buyer under clause 11.7, invoices
for the inclusions, banking information, and the spouse's consent if the
property is the family residence. Until occupancy, you remain responsible for
keeping the premises in the state they were in at the time of the showings:
[how the signing at the notary unfolds](/en/articles/quebec-notary-process/).
