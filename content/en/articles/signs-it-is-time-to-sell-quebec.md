Nobody sells a house because an index moved. You sell because something changed at home: one bedroom more or one bedroom fewer, a job somewhere else, a letter from the lender, a staircase that has become long. Here are the six signals that come up most often with sellers in Laval and Montreal, and the document that lets you verify each one instead of just feeling it.

## Sell or keep your home in Quebec: six signals that come from your own life

Some deadlines come from outside and impose their own calendar. Five of them are covered in [five cases where keeping costs more than selling](/en/articles/keep-or-sell-property-quebec/). The signals below are the opposite: nothing forces you, nobody follows up with you, and that is exactly why they get postponed for years.

In all six cases, the signal arrives as an impression: the house is "too big", the commute has "become long", the maintenance is "heavy". An impression cannot be weighed against the proceeds of a sale. A number can.

| Signal | What confirms it on paper |
| - | - |
| Household size | The rooms actually used each week |
| A job elsewhere | Commute time, measured at rush hour |
| Renewal | The lender's letter: balance, term, new payment |
| Maintenance | The hours and the invoices of the last twelve months |
| Mobility | The list of rooms that require a staircase |
| Attachment | The comparative analysis of recent sales |

## The household changes faster than the dwelling does

**The household moves, the house stays.** Statistics Canada released the 2021 Census data on households on July 13, 2022. One person households rank first in the country, at 29%, a little under three households in ten. Quebec shows the highest proportion of adults living alone in private households, at 19%. The movement also runs the other way: 986,400 Canadian households in 2021 were multigenerational, held more than one family, or were a family living with other people, an increase of 45% over twenty years.

These figures do not describe your house. They describe how fast a household recomposes, while the number of bedrooms stays exactly where it was.

A Statistics Canada study on internal mobility released on February 16, 2026 ranks the reasons for moving: a larger or better quality dwelling comes first, cited by 25.3% of households that moved, followed by becoming a homeowner at 19.2%, then, tied at 13.5%, a more pleasant neighbourhood and a change in household size.

The test takes one week: every evening, write down the rooms someone actually used. A guest room occupied three nights a year, a basement nobody goes down to any more, and a double garage for one car are square metres you heat, insure, maintain and pay taxes on.

## A job elsewhere, and a commute that stretches

In the same February 16, 2026 study, work dominates as soon as the distance grows: 42.5% of people who changed province cited a new job or a transfer, and 27.6% cited being closer to family. Dwelling quality, the leading reason for moves within a province, falls to 4.5% there.

The commute, for its part, can be measured. According to the commuting data released by Statistics Canada on August 26, 2025, 82.6% of employed people travelled to work in May 2025, the average commute lasted 26.7 minutes, and the share of people working mainly from home fell back to 17.4%, from 18.7% a year earlier.

**That retreat of remote work is the real signal.** Many homes bought between 2020 and 2022 were bought assuming two or three days a week at home. When the employer goes back to five, the house has not changed, but the calculation that justified it has. Add it up over a year: minutes added per day, times days on site, times weeks worked. No public data lets me say at how many minutes a commute should make you sell a house. It is your annual total that answers, not a national average.

## The renewal that moves your monthly number

CMHC (SCHL) published the spring edition of its Residential Mortgage Industry Report on May 12, 2026. It shows that the number of borrowers renewing in 2026 is 13% lower than in 2025, and that the typical monthly mortgage payment went from $1,784 in the fourth quarter of 2024 to $1,852 in the fourth quarter of 2025, a rise of 3.81% over one year.

Those figures are national. Yours is in the renewal letter: balance, remaining amortization, proposed payment. Run the numbers again with [the mortgage calculator](/en/tools/mortgage/), then ask the real question: what do you stop doing in order to pay the difference?

A payment that goes up is not, on its own, a reason to sell. It becomes a signal the day it eats into what the house was supposed to make possible: the savings, the holidays, the margin for a repair. The rate, the penalty and the portability of the loan are discussed with your lender or with a mortgage broker certified by the Autorité des marchés financiers, not with a real estate broker.

## The maintenance you no longer want to carry

The 2021 Census Dictionary defines dwelling condition as the need for repairs, and specifies that the concept "does not include desirable remodelling or additions". Major repairs cover dwellings "with defective plumbing or electrical wiring" and those "needing structural repairs to walls, floors or ceilings".

**Note what that definition leaves out.** It measures what is broken, not the fatigue of the person who shovels, caulks, opens and closes the pool and chases a contractor. No public statistic puts a number on that fatigue, which is nevertheless one of the reasons a seller eventually names out loud.

The document that makes it visible is one you already have: the invoices of the last twelve months, and your hours. Add the two together, then compare them with the service life calendar set out in [home maintenance costs in Quebec](/en/articles/home-maintenance-cost-quebec/). If the roof, the siding and the drain all come due together within the next five years, the question is no longer "is this heavy", it is "do I want to finance this work here".

## Sell or keep when the house no longer follows your mobility needs

CMHC devotes a section to aging in place, which it defines as the ability to remain in your dwelling safely and independently. It covers housing options for seniors, home adaptation, and financing for people aged 55 and over. Its consumer page gives no Quebec statistic, and I will therefore put none forward.

The inventory itself takes ten minutes: the list of rooms you cannot reach without going up or down a staircase. A bungalow whose only full bathroom is in the basement is not the same problem as a third floor condo with no elevator. One is settled with work, the other with a move.

The cost of an adaptation depends on the work and on the condition of the building, and no public fee schedule holds for every house. Have it assessed by an occupational therapist and by a contractor holding a licence from the Régie du bâtiment du Québec, then compare that quote with the cost of selling and buying again. Two invoices, not a judgment on your independence.

## The attachment that skews the asking price

This is the hardest signal, because it does not present itself as a signal. It presents itself as a price. "I put $60,000 into the kitchen." "The neighbour is asking this much." "I am not giving it away."

OACIQ is clear on the method. To determine fair market value, the broker must in particular carry out a comparative analysis with other similar properties recently sold in the same area, and the price must reflect the reality of the market. When advising a seller client on the value of an immovable, the broker must give an opinion that is "based and justified in accordance with usage and the rules of the art": that is section 76 of the Règlement sur les conditions d'exercice d'une opération de courtage, sur la déontologie des courtiers et sur la publicité (the regulation on brokerage practice, broker ethics and advertising). OACIQ adds that, for the duration of the contract, the broker must advise the seller so that the price is adjusted as the market evolves.

The market gives a dated benchmark. In its September 4, 2026 release, APCIQ reports for August, in the Montreal metropolitan area, 2,853 residential sales, down 13% year over year, and 20,128 active listings, up 18%. Median selling times: 42 days for the single-family home, 62 days for the condominium, which is 12 more than a year earlier, and 52 days for the plex. These are past medians, for one given month. They predict neither your timeline nor your price.

The method for auditing a price is detailed in [how to audit your neighbour's comparables](/en/articles/real-estate-comparables-quebec/). Keep this much: attachment is not corrected by reluctantly cutting the price after eight weeks. It is corrected by looking at concluded sales before you list.

## Frequently asked questions

**Our last child is moving into an apartment. Should we sell right away?**
Nothing requires it. Do the one week inventory of the rooms used, then add up the annual cost of the square metres that have become useless: heating, taxes, insurance, maintenance. If that total surprises you, the conversation is worth having.

**My payment goes up at renewal. Is that a signal to sell?**
Not on its own. CMHC shows a rise in the typical payment across the country, but the figure that counts is the one in your letter. Compare the annual gap with your budget margin, and confirm your term and lender options with a mortgage broker before concluding anything about the house.

**I find the suggested price too low for my house. What should I do?**
Ask for the comparables. The broker must support that opinion with recent sales of similar properties in the same area, and be able to justify it. If those sales do not convince you, discuss the adjustments item by item rather than the final figure.

**We want to take in an elderly parent. Extend the house or change houses?**
Both can be costed. Get a quote drawn up, check the municipal by-law, then compare with the total cost of a sale followed by a purchase. Zoning and the permit often settle the matter before the budget does.
