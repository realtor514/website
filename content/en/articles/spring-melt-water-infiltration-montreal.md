In March, snow piled against a foundation melts faster than frozen ground can
absorb it. The water then takes the shortest route down, and that route often
runs through an opening nobody looks at in July. Here is where it gets in, what
you can check from outside in fifteen minutes, and why your insurer treats an
infiltration and a refoulement d'égout, a sewer backup, as two separate claims.

## Where water gets in during the melt: five entry points

The City of Laval gives the simplest definition there is: when water from
outside enters a building, that is water infiltration. It names three main
causes: poor sealing of the foundation, of the windows or of a garage door;
poor drainage of rainwater on the land around the house; and a water table close
to the surface. The melt concentrates those three causes into the same month.

The five entry points:

1. The foundation, through a crack or through the joint between the wall and the slab.
2. The basement window, when its window well fills with snow.
3. The reverse slope driveway, which carries water from the street downhill.
4. The roof, under a barrage de glace, an ice dam.
5. The land itself, when the grade brings water back toward the building.

One point is deliberately missing: refoulement d'égout, which comes back up
through the plumbing rather than through the building envelope. The drain, the
backwater valve and the Montreal grant are covered in our article on
[a sagging sewer drain in Montreal](/en/articles/sagging-sewer-drain-montreal/).
Here we stay with the water that comes from outside.

## Grading: basement water infiltration starts outdoors

**Grading decides almost everything.** CMHC's checklist on moisture and mould is
direct: make sure the ground around the house slopes so runoff drains away from
the house, and make sure the slope of the soil, the lawn and the driveway
carries water away from the house. Three surfaces, not just the grass.

In Laval, the division of roles is spelled out. Correcting the flow around the
building, or making the foundation watertight, is the owner's job. The City
steps in when the infiltration is caused by inadequate drainage on the public
right of way, or by a water main or a storm sewer in poor condition.

**The driveway that slopes down toward the house.** Laval then requires a crown
that keeps water from pooling near the building, at section 4.01.03 of by-law
L-11870. Montreal treats the same vulnerability differently: it lists a hump
behind the sidewalk, a dos d'âne en arrière-trottoir, a watertight garage door,
a removable flood barrier, a batardeau amovible, and filling in the driveway as
the four measures to consider.

**The window well.** A window well packed with snow is a reservoir sitting
against an opening. The Bureau d'assurance du Canada, the Insurance Bureau of
Canada, puts the action in writing in its spring thaw advice: clear the snow out
of window wells and shovel snow away from the foundation. Before the first mild
spell, not during it.

## The roof, the ice dam, and the wall that stains underneath

A barrage de glace forms when snow melts on the warm part of the roof and
refreezes near the eaves, which are colder. Water builds up behind that dam and
looks for a way out sideways, under the shingles.

What that produces inside does not look like a classic roof leak. The mark
appears at the top of an exterior wall or around the edge of the ceiling. CMHC
restates the general mechanism: water gets into the house when there is a crack
or a leak in its foundation, its floors, its walls or its roof. A brownish stain
at the top of a wall, in March, deserves a question to the seller before it gets
a reassuring explanation.

Hold on to the insurance vocabulary, because it changes everything in the next
section: according to the Bureau d'assurance du Canada, an infiltration created
by an ice dam on the roof or in the gutters falls under the above ground water
avenant, the "eau au-dessus du sol" endorsement.

## The gutters, and the exact spot where they discharge

This is the fastest check of a showing, and the one most often skipped. The
question is not whether the house has gutters, but where they send the water.

**What Laval requires.** By-law L-11870 provides that water from a sloped roof
carried away by gutters must be discharged onto a permeable surface on the
property, lawn or flower bed, at least 1 metre from the building. The City also
names the three non compliant connections: hooking into the foundation drain,
discharging directly to the street, and dumping water onto an impermeable
surface sloped toward the street. Redirecting the water onto the neighbour's
land is not a way out: Laval points to articles 976, 979 and 983 of the Code
civil du Québec, and specifies that a dispute of that kind is not a municipal
matter.

**What Montreal asks for.** For a home with a sloped roof, the City wants
gutters that send water toward a permeable surface and that are not connected to
the foundation drain. Its prevention list adds a clean, working drain at the
bottom of a reverse slope driveway.

**The distance.** The Bureau d'assurance du Canada recommends that gutters and
downspouts direct water away from the foundation, 2 metres out, with extensions
where needed. CMHC says the same thing another way: install extensions on the
downspouts so that water from rain and melted snow drains well away from the
house.

## Infiltration or refoulement: two avenants, and only one will answer

Here is the belief to correct. Plenty of owners think they hold "the water
damage coverage". There is not one, there are several, and they do not stand in
for each other.

The base policy covers water damage caused by a plumbing fixture such as the
bathtub, the washer or the water heater, and the bursting of a potable water
pipe. For an infiltration, a refoulement d'égout or the overflow of a body of
water, the protection has to have been added by avenant, an endorsement, with
its own limit of coverage, separate from the policy's own.

| What happens | Avenant involved |
|---|---|
| Rain, snow or melting ice coming through the roof, the walls, a door or a window | Above ground water |
| Overflow or backup of the gutters and downspouts, barrage de glace | Above ground water |
| Infiltration through the foundation or through a basement door or window | Ground water and sewers |
| Refoulement d'égout in the basement | Ground water and sewers |
| Overflow of a body of water | Flood avenant |

Source: Bureau d'assurance du Canada, consumer information site.

Two practical consequences. The avenant that covers you for an ice dam does not
cover you for a foundation crack, and the reverse holds just as well. And in a
known flood zone, coverage against the overflow of a body of water can be
limited or unavailable. The numbers explain that caution: in 2024, 68% of home
insurance claims paid in Quebec involved water damage, at an average cost of
$23,550. Put the question to your insurer before the inspection period closes.

## Visiting in March: what a buyer sees and will not see in July

A melt showing is a free load test. What is visible then, and only then:

- Where water pools on the land, and which way it heads.
- The snow piled against the foundation, and how far up the wall it reaches.
- Ice at the foot of the downspouts, a sign that the water is not moving away.
- Icicles along the roof edge, which give the dam away.
- The window well full of snow, and the sealant around the window.
- Water running down the driveway toward the door rather than toward the street.
- In the basement: a fresh stain, a dehumidifier running, a musty smell near the
  exterior wall.

By July all of that has dried. It is the seasonal advantage set out in
[the blind spots of a winter purchase](/en/articles/buying-home-winter-montreal/).
None of it replaces a professional examination: the full list is in
[the pre-purchase inspection, item by item](/en/articles/home-inspection-checklist-montreal/).

## Déclarations du vendeur: what a past infiltration forces you to write down

The mandatory Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble form, the seller's
declarations on the property, DV 00001, version V24 of June 2022, leaves no grey
area.

- **D3.6**: does water accumulate periodically on the land?
- **D4.1**: has there ever been any water infiltration? If so, the seller ticks
  the location in a list that includes the basement or crawl space, the roof, the
  terrace, the balcony, the solarium, the skylight, the door, the window and the
  chimney.
- **D5.2**: have any problems ever affected the basement or the crawl space,
  including cracks in the foundation?

These questions are about what has already happened, not about what is left. An
infiltration repaired in 2019 is still an infiltration to declare in 2026. On
the buyer's side, a "yes" is not a reason to run: it is a reason to ask for the
invoices, the method used and the contractor's name. The form is explained in
detail in [the seller's declaration in Quebec](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/).

## Frequently asked questions

**The seller says the basement has never taken water. How do I verify that?**
Ask for the DV 00001 form filled in and signed, not a verbal summary, then
compare it against what you see at the bottom of the walls. Add a written
question about past insurance claims.

**My policy covers refoulement d'égout. Am I covered for a crack?**
Not necessarily. Infiltration through the foundation and refoulement fall under
the same avenant with several insurers, but water coming in through the roof or
a window falls under a different one. Have both confirmed in writing.

**Do I have to clear the snow away from the foundation every winter?**
The Bureau d'assurance du Canada recommends it, along with clearing window wells
and walkways, to give the meltwater somewhere to run.

**My gutters discharge onto asphalt. Is that compliant?**
In Laval, no: by-law L-11870 requires a permeable surface, at least 1 metre from
the building, and lists discharge onto an impermeable surface sloped toward the
street among the non compliant connections. Check your own municipality's rule,
it is not uniform.
