The pre-purchase inspection generates more questions than any other step in a purchase. Here are the answers, in the order buyers ask them.

## What it costs

In Quebec, a residential inspection for a normally sized single-family home generally runs between $500 and $900, taxes included.

The price rises with:

- The size and age of the building
- The number of units for a plex
- Add-ons such as a pyrite test, a water analysis for a well, a vermiculite test or a drain camera

Those additional tests are not included in the base inspection. The pyrite test in particular is often recommended in certain parts of the Montreal area and is billed separately.

It feels like a large expense at the exact moment you are draining your savings for the down payment. Measured against the price of the property, it represents roughly one tenth of one percent.

## Who pays

The buyer, in almost every case. You hire the inspector, you pay the inspector, and the report belongs to you.

That matters: the report is your document. You are not required to hand it to the seller, unless you use it to renegotiate or to withdraw, in which case you will generally have to provide the relevant portion.

Be wary of an inspection paid for or supplied by the seller. It is not necessarily dishonest, but the inspector was chosen by the other party. If the seller presents you with an existing report, read it, then get your own anyway.

## Before the offer or after?

That is the real question, and the answer is: after the offer, but with an inspection condition.

Here is why. In Quebec the normal approach is to make a promise to purchase conditional on a satisfactory inspection. You then have a window, generally seven to ten days, to have the property inspected. If the report reveals a significant problem, you can renegotiate the price, require corrections, or withdraw without penalty.

Inspecting before submitting an offer creates two problems. First, the seller has no obligation to let you in with an inspector while nothing is signed. Second, you pay $700 for a property you may well not get, especially if there are multiple offers.

There is one exception. In a bidding situation where several buyers are competing for the same property, some sellers organize an inspection window before the offer deadline, precisely so everyone can submit without a condition. If you are offered that opportunity, take it.

## Can you bring an inspector to the first showing?

No, and it is not a good idea to ask.

A showing is a showing. An inspection is two to three hours of work requiring access to the basement, the attic, the electrical panel, the mechanical rooms. No seller agrees to that during a thirty-minute visit, and the listing broker will decline.

What you can do at the first showing is look carefully yourself: water marks in the basement, cracks, the age of the roof and windows, the electrical panel, any smell of dampness. Those observations tell you whether it is worth going further. They do not replace an inspection.

## The mistake that costs the most

Waiving the inspection to win a bidding war.

When several buyers are competing for a property, the temptation to submit without an inspection condition to look more attractive is strong. It works. It is also the riskiest bet in the entire process.

A foundation needing rebuilding, a full roof, a non-compliant electrical system, soil contamination: those are five-figure amounts, sometimes six. You discover them after signing, and you have no recourse against the seller for a problem a normal inspection would have revealed.

If you absolutely must submit without a condition, do it only if you were able to inspect before the offer deadline, or if you have the cash to absorb a major bad surprise.

## How to choose your inspector

In Quebec the profession is not governed by a professional order the way architects or engineers are. Quality therefore varies a great deal.

What to verify:

- Membership in a recognized building inspector association
- Professional liability insurance in force
- Use of a service contract and a written standard of practice
- A detailed written report with photos, delivered within 24 to 48 hours
- Experience with your property type, which matters particularly for a plex or an older building

And above all: be present during the inspection. The written report is useful, but what the inspector tells you while pointing at things is often worth more. Set aside two to three hours.

## What an inspection does not cover

An inspection is visual. The inspector does not open walls, lift carpets or move your furniture. Nor does the inspector rule on zoning compliance, on the value of the property, or on latent defects in the legal sense.

It reduces risk, it does not eliminate it. That is why the seller's declarations, the certificate of location and the condo association minutes matter just as much.
