Behind every new home sold by an accredited builder in Quebec sits a mandatory
warranty plan. It does not cover every building, it does not pay for
everything, and it expires if you give notice too late. Here is its exact
content, taken from the Règlement sur le plan de garantie des bâtiments
résidentiels neufs (the regulation on the guarantee plan for new residential
buildings) and from the documents published by Garantie de construction
résidentielle (GCR), the organization that administers the plan.

## The GCR warranty: which new buildings are covered, and which are not

Section 2 of the regulation (B-1.1, r. 8) gives the list. Outside divided
co-ownership: the detached, semi-detached or row single-family house, and the
multifamily building from the duplex up to the quintuplex. In divided
co-ownership: the detached, semi-detached or row single-family house, and the
multifamily building containing no more than 4 stacked private portions.
Private parking and storage spaces do not count toward that total of four.

**It is the exclusion list that produces the disappointments.** GCR says it
plainly: the condo tower with more than four stacked units is not covered, and
neither is the conversion or renovation of a building, an addition,
construction on existing foundations, a detached garage, or self-building. So
the buyer of a condo on the eighth floor of a Montreal project has no GCR
warranty at all. Ask in writing what protection the developer offers instead,
and have it read before you sign.

The regulation also does not apply when the builder's client is a non-profit
organization, a cooperative or a housing bureau that receives, for that
building, financial assistance from the Société d'habitation du Québec, the
provincial housing corporation.

## The inspection préréception and the list of work to correct

Section 17 is clear. Every covered building must be inspected before
acceptance, jointly by the builder and you, using a pre-established checklist
supplied by the administrator. You may be assisted by the person of your
choice. GCR recommends a building professional: an architect, an engineer, a
technologist or a building inspector.

What is at stake that day is simple. You and the builder identify what is left
to finish and the apparent defects and poor workmanship to correct. You tick
the "AVEC RÉSERVE" box (with reservations) on GCR's inspection préréception
form, the joint pre-acceptance inspection. You then agree on a time limit for
that work, which cannot exceed 6 months. Photos and videos are recommended.

An apparent defect you do not note at that moment falls outside the warranty.
The only exception provided in section 10: written notice within the 3 days
following acceptance, and only as long as you have not moved in. That is the
whole purpose of this inspection. It draws the line between what you accepted
and what the builder still owes you. On choosing your inspector and what it
costs, see [what an inspection costs and when to do
it](/en/articles/home-inspection-cost-timing-quebec/).

## One year, three years, five years: the warranty on a new home in Quebec

Before acceptance, section 9 covers three things if the builder defaults: the
deposits you paid, completion of the work, and relocation, moving and storage
of your belongings if you cannot take acceptance on the agreed date.

After acceptance, section 10 organizes coverage by duration.

| What is covered | Period | Deadline to give written notice |
|---|---|---|
| Completion of the work | Noted at acceptance, or within 3 days if you have not moved in | Reasonable time following the agreed date for the end of the work |
| Apparent defects and poor workmanship | Same rule | Reasonable time following that same date |
| Non-apparent poor workmanship | Discovered within the year following acceptance | Reasonable time from discovery |
| Latent defects | Discovered within the 3 years following acceptance | Reasonable time from discovery |
| Design, construction and workmanship defects, soil defects | Appearing within the 5 years following the end of the work | Reasonable time from discovery, occurrence or first significant manifestation |

The words matter. For GCR, poor workmanship, the malfaçon, is work badly
executed, of minor importance, that compromises neither the durability of the
building nor the safety of its occupants. A latent defect, the vice caché, is a
defect not apparent on visual examination, existing before acceptance, unknown
to you at that moment, and serious enough to reduce the quality of the
building. The five-year major defect is a serious defect, apparent or not, that
can affect the durability of the building or the safety of its occupants.

The five-year warranty runs from the end of the work, not from acceptance. The
two dates do not always coincide. Write down both.

## The dollar caps, and what the GCR warranty never pays

| Item | Single-family house | Divided co-ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Deposits | $50,000 | $50,000 per fraction |
| Relocation, moving, storage | $6,000 | $6,000 per fraction |
| Completion and repair | Amount stated in the contract, not exceeding $300,000 | $200,000 per private portion, not exceeding $3,000,000 per building |

Relocation is reimbursed against receipts, with daily maximums for lodging and
meals: $95 for one person, $125 for two, $160 for three, $190 for four people
and more. For a multifamily building outside co-ownership, repair is capped at
$200,000 multiplied by the number of dwellings.

**The exclusions in section 12.** Excluded, among others: defects in the
materials and equipment you supplied and installed, repairs required by the
normal behaviour of materials such as cracks and shrinkage, repairs made
necessary by your own fault, by inadequate maintenance or by your own
alterations, normal wear, force majeure, contaminated soil, parking and storage
areas located outside the building, and any outdoor work such as the pool,
landscaping, sidewalks, walkways and surface drainage. Negative grading of the
lot, on the other hand, stays covered. Two of these exclusions, the normal
behaviour of materials and force majeure, fall away if the builder failed to
follow accepted practice or a standard in force.

That cap covers the building, not your total bill. Transfer duties, the notary
and taxes come on top of the builder's price: put numbers on them in the
[closing costs calculator](/en/tools/closing-costs/) before you settle your
budget.

## Written notice: the deadline that kills a claim

The regulation requires written notice to the builder, with a copy to the
administrator, within a "reasonable time" from discovery. GCR specifies that
case law sets that reasonable time at 6 months following discovery of the
problem. A discovery made in January and reported only in October therefore
starts out with a serious handicap, even if the defect itself is perfectly
real.

Two habits prevent the worst. Give written notice as soon as you discover the
problem, without waiting to find out whether the builder will cooperate, since
that notice also serves to interrupt prescription. And for relocation tied to
a delivery delay, the request must go out no later than 6 months after
acceptance of the building, with the supporting receipts.

Section 19.1 provides a safety valve. A missed deadline cannot be raised
against you if the builder or the administrator has itself failed in its own
obligations, or if its representations led you to go past the deadline. That is
an exception, not a plan.

## Claim, mediation and arbitration

The procedure in section 18 is a series of short deadlines.

1. You give written notice of the defect to the builder, copy to the
   administrator.
2. At least 15 days later, if nothing moves or the intervention does not
   satisfy you, you notify the administrator in writing. A file opening fee of
   $100 is required, which is $114.98 with taxes according to GCR. It is
   refunded to you if the decision favours you, even in part, or if a
   settlement is reached.
3. Within 15 days, the administrator asks the builder to intervene and to say,
   within the following 15 days, what it intends to do.
4. The administrator then inspects on site and produces, within the 30 days
   following the inspection, a detailed written report sent by registered mail.

Unhappy with the decision? You have 30 days from receiving it by registered
mail to submit the dispute to arbitration. Within that same period the parties
may instead agree to mediation before a mediator chosen from a list drawn up by
the Minister of Labour. If mediation fails, the 30-day arbitration period
starts again from the mediator's notice.

When the beneficiary is the applicant, the costs of arbitration are borne by
the administrator, unless you win on no aspect of your claim. The arbitrator
may also award reimbursement of your reasonable and relevant expert fees if you
win in whole or in part. The arbitration decision is final and without appeal.

## Before you sign, and what changes on resale

Four checks fit on one page. Is the builder accredited with GCR and does it
hold a licence from the Régie du bâtiment du Québec, the provincial building
authority? Does the building fall within the categories in section 2? Have you
received a duplicate of the duly signed warranty contract, knowing that section
138 releases you from your contractual obligations until you have it? And does
the contract contain a clause that would reduce your rights, which section 140
prohibits, since a beneficiary cannot waive them by private agreement?

I ask for that duplicate of the warranty contract every single time before
signing, because it is the document that carries the accreditation and licence
numbers. Have the rest reviewed by your notary or your lawyer, as the article
on [the notary in Quebec, step by
step](/en/articles/quebec-notary-process/) points out.

**On resale, the warranty follows the building.** Sections 16 and 32 provide
that it benefits every subsequent purchaser, for the term still left to run. A
buyer taking over a two-year-old house therefore inherits the rest of the
three-year protection on latent defects and the five-year protection on major
defects. On the seller's side, that relieves you of nothing: your seller's
declarations and the legal warranty of the Code civil du Québec keep applying
in parallel.

## Frequently asked questions

**My condo is on the fifth floor of a new building. Am I covered?**
No, if the building has more than four stacked private portions. Mandatory
coverage stops there. Require from the developer a written description of the
protection offered instead.

**I found an infiltration 14 months after acceptance. Too late?**
Not necessarily. Non-apparent poor workmanship is limited to one year, but the
latent defect covers three years and the major defect five years from the end
of the work. What counts then is the seriousness of the defect and how quickly
you give written notice.

**The builder tells me the crack is normal. Is that true?**
The normal behaviour of materials is excluded, cracks and shrinkage included.
But that exclusion does not apply if the builder failed to follow accepted
practice or a standard in force. That is a question for an expert, not a matter
of opinion.

**Do I have to pay to open a file?**
Yes, $100 under the regulation, which is $114.98 taxes included at GCR. The fee
is refunded to you if the decision goes your way, even partly, or if a
settlement is reached.
