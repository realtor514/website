Your property is worth $900,000 and you are wondering whether it belongs to a
different market than your neighbour's at $600,000. The answer is yes, but not
for the reasons usually given. This segment stands apart through a measurable
constraint: fewer buyers, more months of inventory, and fewer similar sales to
establish a price. Here are the figures and their date.

## In the statistics, the upper range has no fixed threshold

The APCIQ residential barometer, published every quarter, contains a table 3
titled "Conditions du marché par gammes de prix," market conditions by price
range. It splits each territory into five bands and gives, for each one, the
inventory, the sales and the number of months of inventory.

In the second quarter of 2026, for the single family home, the upper band does
not start at the same price everywhere: $950,000 in the Montreal metropolitan
area, $1,220,000 on the island of Montreal, $930,000 in Laval, $2,930,000 in
sector 9, central Montreal.

There is no million dollar threshold, then. The boundary is relative to the
local market and recalculated every quarter: three months earlier, Laval's was
at $920,000 and the island of Montreal's at $1,200,000. The APCIQ does not
publish in that document the method that sets them.

**The practical consequence.** Your property belongs to this segment according
to the city, not according to an absolute figure. A $1,000,000 house is in the
upper band in Laval. In sector 9, it is only in the second of the five bands.

## How many sales above the threshold, in Montreal and in Laval

Here is table 3 for the single family home, upper band, over the twelve months
ending in the second quarter of 2026. Sales and inventory are monthly averages
over the twelve months, and months of inventory is their quotient.

| Territory | Upper band | Sales per month | Inventory | Months of inventory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Montreal metropolitan area | $950,000 and up | 321 | 2,378 | 7.4 |
| Island of Montreal | $1,220,000 and up | 95 | 860 | 9.0 |
| Laval | $930,000 and up | 24 | 182 | 7.6 |
| Sector 9, central Montreal | $2,930,000 and up | 7 | 120 | 17.9 |
| Sector 20, Sainte-Dorothée | $1,120,000 and up | 4 | 41 | 10.3 |

In Sainte-Dorothée, four sales a month above $1,120,000, roughly fifty over
twelve months, for the whole sector and all house configurations combined. That
is the pool your comparables have to come out of.

## Months of inventory, the signature of the segment

The same table shows the spread inside a single territory. Laval, single family
home, same twelve months:

| Price range | Sales per month | Months of inventory |
|---|---|---|
| Under $310,000 | 5 | 1.9 |
| $310,000 to $470,000 | 20 | 2.2 |
| $470,000 to $780,000 | 141 | 2.6 |
| $780,000 to $930,000 | 25 | 4.1 |
| $930,000 and up | 24 | 7.6 |

The number of months of inventory goes from 2.2 to 7.6 between the second band
and the last: roughly three and a half times as long to clear the stock, at the
observed pace of sales.

The APCIQ sets the interpretation thresholds itself: below 8 months of
inventory the market favours sellers, between 8 and 10 it is balanced, beyond
10 it favours buyers. Laval's five bands are therefore all classified as a
seller's market, but the last one is the only one coming close to the line. In
Sainte-Dorothée, it crosses it: 10.3 months, a buyer's market, while the three
other bands where sales were recorded remain classified as a seller's market.
Same sector, same quarter, two markets.

## What time on market says, and what it does not

Watch out for a common confusion. The APCIQ writes, about the number of months
of inventory, that "this measure is different from the average selling time."
The first figure describes how the stock clears at the pace of sales; the
average number of days on the market counts the days between the signing of the
brokerage contract and the sale.

And here is what I did not find: **neither the barometer nor the APCIQ monthly
release publishes time on market by price range.** Selling times are released
by property category and by geographic sector, never by price band. Nobody can
quote you an official time on market for Laval's upper segment, then.

What does exist is time on market by territory, and it rises with the local
price level. In the second quarter of 2026, for the single family home:
32 days in the Montreal metropolitan area, 30 days in Laval, 41 days on the
island of Montreal, 48 days in Sainte-Dorothée where the median price was
$775,500, and 64 days in sector 9 where it was $1,980,000. That is not a
statistic by price band, and I will not present it as one. For the selling
timeline, see [how long a sale actually takes](/en/articles/how-long-to-sell-house-montreal/).

## Pricing in the upper range when comparable sales are missing

This is where the small number of transactions becomes your problem. The
comparables method starts from similar concluded sales and adjusts, and the way
to audit it is set out in the article on
[real estate comparables](/en/articles/real-estate-comparables-quebec/). What
changes when the sample is thin is written in the Manuel d'évaluation foncière
du Québec, the province's property assessment manual, published by the Quebec
government.

The Manuel says first that the comparison method "produces excellent results"
in a homogeneous environment "where sales are numerous enough to allow
statistically conclusive analysis." Then it names the limit: "The main
difficulty encountered in applying this method is the scarcity, if not the
absence, of transactions involving comparable properties." It even puts a
figure on the need: using statistical modelling requires "a sample of at least
50 to 60 observations." Sainte-Dorothée's upper band produces about fifty a
year, all configurations combined.

**What happens then.** The Manuel is explicit: comparison is to be preferred
where comparable sales are numerous enough, and "another method must be
considered for the remaining assessment units, in particular those of a unique
or uncommon character." That other method is the cost approach, which "may be
the only one detailed enough" for constructions that are hard to compare with
others, "such as highly individualized luxury residences."

The Ordre des évaluateurs agréés du Québec, Quebec's order of chartered
appraisers, describes the same logic: the chartered appraiser compares the
results of the three methods, cost, comparison and income, then keeps the value
obtained by the one "offering the highest degree of accuracy for the kind of
property." That is why two competent professionals reach two different figures
on the same property: they do not weight the same methods, because the data do
not settle it. Before a meeting, the [home value estimator](/en/tools/home-estimate/)
gives an order of magnitude, nothing more.

## Why the assessment roll drifts further in this segment

The same Manuel documents a bias few owners hear about. In describing the
residuals of a regression model used in municipal assessment, it writes that
this "reflects a fairly systematic reality in property assessment, namely that
low end properties are generally overestimated by the regression equation,
while high end residences are, on the contrary, underestimated." Hence the
instruction that follows from it: avoid grouping high end and low end
properties in the same analysis segment.

That bias bears on the statistical model, and no source consulted puts a figure
on the average gap between the roll and market value by price band. The roll
answers a different question from yours in any case, since it reflects the
market at an earlier reference date. For the procedure and the deadlines, see
[contesting your municipal assessment](/en/articles/contest-property-assessment-quebec/).

## Publishing a sale price: the rule does not change with the price

A belief circulates that high value transactions get published more freely,
sign and figure included. They do not. The OACIQ advertising guideline,
section 7.2, updated on June 17, 2026, calls it a prohibited practice to
advertise the sale price of a property or any percentage from which the amount
can be deduced. Nor may the broker state the price initially asked as entered
in the brokerage contract. Even with both parties' agreement, publishing the
price before the deed is signed "must remain exceptional."

The guideline on the protection of personal information, section 7.2.4,
completes it: until the deed is published in the Registre foncier, the price is
confidential, and the comparable sheets given to a client must be masked of
anything identifying the seller. No price band escapes it. The detail is in the
article on [the list price and the sold price](/en/articles/list-price-vs-sold-price-montreal/).

## Frequently asked questions

**My house is worth $950,000 in Laval. Am I in the upper range?**
Within the meaning of the APCIQ table for the second quarter of 2026, yes:
Laval's upper band starts at $930,000. That boundary moved from $920,000 to
$930,000 from one quarter to the next, so it will be different again in the
next barometer.

**How much longer does it take to sell in this segment?**
No official source publishes time on market by price band. What is published by
price range is the number of months of inventory: 7.6 months in Laval's upper
band against 2.2 in the $310,000 to $470,000 band.

**Why do two brokers give me two very different prices?**
Because the number of comparable sales is low, and the Manuel d'évaluation
foncière itself acknowledges that the limits of the comparison method "depend
on the number of sales and their representativeness." Ask for the list of sales
used, their dates and the adjustments, not only the final figure.

**The municipal assessment is well below what I am being told. Is that normal?**
Two causes add up: the roll's reference date is earlier than the current
market, and the Manuel notes the underestimation of high end residences by
regression models. That does not set your selling price.
