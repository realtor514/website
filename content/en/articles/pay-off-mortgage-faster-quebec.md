Your lender offers to switch you "to biweekly." Two options carry almost the same
name and only one of them actually puts extra money against your principal. Here
are the four levers that shorten an amortization, the arithmetic behind each one,
and the limits your contract imposes before a penalty gets involved.

## The four levers that let you pay off your mortgage faster

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada names four, and there is no fifth one:
make a lump sum payment, increase the amount of your regular payments, choose an
accelerated payment option, and keep the same payment when your rate drops at
renewal.

Here is what each one moves in a single year. **This table is an arithmetic
illustration, not an offer and not a rate.** Original loan of $400,000, monthly
payment of $2,000, and a contract that would allow 10% in the first two rows.
Your contract will say something else.

| Lever | Calculation | Extra principal in one year |
|---|---|---|
| Lump sum payment | $400,000 x 10% | $40,000 |
| Higher regular payment | $2,000 x 10% x 12 | $2,400 |
| Accelerated frequency | 26 x $1,000 minus 12 x $2,000 | $2,000 |
| Renewal | Measured in years, not in dollars | Not applicable |

The ranking by dollar amount is not the ranking by realism. The lump sum wins on
paper, but it assumes $40,000 in cash. Accelerated frequency comes third and
requires no decision at all once it is turned on.

## Accelerated or not accelerated: the only difference that counts

**FCAC publishes the six frequency formulas, and they settle the debate.** The
two accelerated formulas do not divide an annual total, they divide a monthly
payment. Arithmetic illustration again, with a $2,000 monthly payment chosen
because it keeps the math clean.

| Frequency | FCAC formula | Payment | Payments per year | Annual total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly | The payment | $2,000 | 12 | $24,000 |
| Semi-monthly | Payment / 2 | $1,000 | 24 | $24,000 |
| Biweekly | Payment x 12 / 26 | $923.08 | 26 | $24,000 |
| Weekly | Payment x 12 / 52 | $461.54 | 52 | $24,000 |
| Accelerated biweekly | Payment / 2 | $1,000 | 26 | $26,000 |
| Accelerated weekly | Payment / 4 | $500 | 52 | $26,000 |

The first four rows pay the same total, to the rounding. Moving from monthly to
ordinary biweekly therefore does not shorten your amortization: the same money
simply arrives in 26 pieces instead of 12.

**The reason accelerated works comes down to one division.** A year holds 26
two-week periods. You pay half a monthly payment 26 times, so 13 monthly
payments. With accelerated weekly, 52 payments of a quarter of a payment also
make 13. Twelve becomes thirteen. That is exactly what FCAC writes: accelerated
payments let you make the equivalent of one extra monthly payment each year.

That thirteenth payment goes largely against principal, and every dollar of
principal repaid today removes the interest it would have carried to the end of
the amortization. Before you change anything, put your real numbers into the
[mortgage calculator](/en/tools/mortgage/) and compare the two schedules. Paying
13 monthly payments instead of 12 is not neutral for a tight budget.

## The lump sum payment: a percentage of the original amount

**Prepayment privileges are not negotiated over the phone.** They are written
down. FCAC defines them as an amount you can repay on top of your regular
payments without paying a penalty, in two forms: increasing your regular payment
by a certain percentage, or making a lump sum payment up to a certain amount or
percentage of the original mortgage amount.

Hold on to "original mortgage." The percentage applies to the amount borrowed at
the start, not to today's balance. On an original loan of $400,000, a 10%
privilege is worth $40,000 a year, and it is still worth $40,000 once the balance
has come down to $250,000.

I cannot tell you the percentage that applies to you, and nobody can do that from
a distance: FCAC writes that privileges vary from one lender to another. You do
not have to guess, though. Banks that adhere to the code of conduct on prepayment
have committed to sending you an annual statement giving the total dollar amount
you may prepay each year without risking a penalty.

Two calendar rules, also from FCAC. The privilege is generally calculated on an
annual basis. And if it is not used within the year, it is lost: an unused amount
usually does not carry over to the following year. Your contract also states when
the payment is allowed, sometimes on fixed dates only.

## Increasing the regular payment: the lever you do not reverse

This is the second privilege, and it has a flaw the first one does not. FCAC is
direct: as a general rule, once you increase your payments, you cannot decrease
them before the end of the term. A term can run five years or more.

The practical consequence is simple. The lump sum is a one-time decision you can
choose not to repeat next year. The payment increase follows you until renewal.
If your income is variable, self-employed or commission-based, the logical order
is the lump sum first, the increase second.

## Paying off your mortgage faster at renewal

The fourth lever costs nothing and shows up once per term. When you renew and the
new rate is lower, your calculated payment drops too. FCAC says it plainly: if
you decide to keep the same amount for your regular payments, you can pay off
your mortgage faster.

You can also ask directly for a shorter amortization period. Renewal is in fact
the only moment when changing lenders triggers no penalty, which makes it the
window where everything gets negotiated at once. What gets decided before the
rate itself is covered in
[what gets decided before your mortgage rate](/en/articles/best-mortgage-rate-canada/).

One marker for context: the Bank of Canada was holding its target for the
overnight rate at 2.25% on September 22, 2026. A low policy rate is not in itself
a reason to repay faster. It only shifts the trade-off between principal and the
other uses of your money.

## Going past the limit, and what an insured loan changes

**Beyond the privilege, it is no longer a repayment, it is a penalty.** FCAC puts
four actions in the same category: paying more than the allowed amount,
cancelling your contract, transferring your mortgage before the end of the term,
and repaying the full balance before maturity, including when you sell. The
penalty is usually the higher of three months' interest on the amount owing and
the interest rate differential, and it can cost thousands of dollars. The full
calculation, with the official example where the bill goes from $3,000 to
$12,000, is in
[the penalty for breaking a mortgage in Quebec](/en/articles/breaking-mortgage-penalty-quebec/).

A nuance in the other direction: FCAC recommends using your privileges fully each
year, because a future penalty will be calculated on a lower balance.

**An insured loan adds a ceiling upstream.** SCHL (CMHC) sets the maximum
amortization period at 25 years for an owner-occupied purchase loan, or at 30
years under the SCHL Premier chez-soi program (CMHC Home Start). Its qualifying
thresholds are a gross debt service ratio (ABD) of 39% and a total debt service
ratio (ATD) of 44%, calculated at the higher of the contract rate plus 2% and a
floor rate of 5.25%. The concrete consequence: shortening the amortization raises
the payment, and that payment still has to fit inside those ratios if you change
lenders at renewal. Reversing course, on the other hand, is capped.

## When you should not pay off your mortgage faster

Three situations where the money works better somewhere else.

**There is still higher rate debt.** Credit card, personal loan, unsecured line of
credit: every dollar placed on the mortgage is a dollar that is not repaying the
most expensive debt. The order is decided by comparing rates, not by preference.

**The emergency cushion does not exist yet.** A lump sum payment is not liquid.
To get that money back out, you have to refinance, with the ceilings, fees and
notarial deed that implies, as explained in
[mortgage refinancing in Quebec](/en/articles/mortgage-refinancing-quebec/).

**A registered account would do better after tax.** CELIAPP (the first home
savings account), RRSP, a pension plan with employer matching: the comparison is
made after tax, on your own situation, and it belongs to your accountant, not to
an article. The plans open to first-time buyers are listed in
[the tax benefits for first-time buyers](/en/articles/first-time-buyer-tax-credits/).

## Frequently asked questions

**If I switch to biweekly, am I paying off faster?**
Only if the option is accelerated. The non accelerated formula multiplies your
monthly payment by 12 and divides it by 26, which reproduces exactly the same
annual total. The accelerated formula divides the monthly payment by 2 and pays
it 26 times, which is 13 monthly payments.

**What is my prepayment percentage?**
It is in the information box at the beginning of your mortgage agreement, which
federally regulated institutions must give you. The exact dollar amount also
appears in the annual statement required by the code of conduct.

**Does my unused privilege accumulate?**
Usually not. FCAC states that an unused amount does not carry over to the
following year.

**Once the mortgage is paid in full, is that the end of it?**
Not at the Registre foncier (Quebec's land register). The Chambre des notaires
recalls that the quittance, the discharge, is the deed by which the creditor
acknowledges having been paid in full and consents to the cancellation. Your
lender may charge discharge fees, from $0 to $400 according to FCAC, on top of
professional fees. The sequence is described in
[the notary's role in Quebec](/en/articles/quebec-notary-process/).

To go further, read [the penalty for breaking a mortgage in Quebec](/en/articles/breaking-mortgage-penalty-quebec/) and [mortgage refinancing in Quebec](/en/articles/mortgage-refinancing-quebec/).
