You will not find a list of the five best neighbourhoods in Montreal here. Not
because the exercise would be hard, but because it is not a broker's to make.
Declaring that one neighbourhood is better than another is passing judgment on
the people who live there, and that ground turns into discrimination very
quickly. My job is the opposite: give you verifiable criteria, the official
source behind each one, and let you draw the conclusion.

One useful clarification first. A Montreal neighbourhood is not an
administrative entity. By-laws, taxes and services follow the boundaries of the
19 boroughs and of the linked cities of the agglomeration. So the first
question is not "which neighbourhood?" but "which side of which boundary?".

## 1. Taxes are not the same from one borough to the next

In Montreal, tax rates vary according to the category of the building and the
borough it sits in. Each borough also levies its own local service and
investment taxes, which are added to the taxes adopted by city council. Two
properties carrying the same value on the assessment roll, on either side of a
street that happens to be a boundary, therefore do not produce the same bill.

The City publishes the full grid every year, under "Taux de taxes", on
montreal.ca. Take the residual residential category rate for the borough you
are looking at, not the average quoted in the newspapers. For the mechanics of
the calculation, read
[the tax bill explained](/en/articles/property-tax-explained/).

## 2. Transfer duties: Montreal plays by its own rules

This is the sharpest gap in Greater Montreal, and it is settled at the notary's
office. The 2026 provincial scale stops at 1.5% above $315,000. A municipality
may go up to 3% on the portion above $500,000, except Montreal, which the law
allows to go higher.

For 2026 Montreal applies 0.5% up to $62,900, 1% from $62,900 to $315,000, 1.5%
from $315,000 to $552,300, 2% from $552,300 to $1,104,700, 2.5% from $1,104,700
to $2,136,500, 3.5% from $2,136,500 to $3,113,000, and 4% above $3,113,000.
Crossing a municipal boundary therefore changes your closing costs before it
changes your mortgage. Details in
[the welcome tax explained](/en/articles/welcome-tax-quebec-guide/), and the
[transfer duty calculator](/en/tools/welcome-tax/) does the arithmetic.

## 3. School tax is identical everywhere

The rate is a single rate for all of Quebec: $0.07899 per $100 of assessment
for 2026-2027, with an exemption on the first $25,000 of the assessment of each
taxable building. Neither the borough nor the school service centre changes it.
That criterion settles nothing.

## 4. Transit: in Montreal the time varies, not the fare

The entire agglomeration of Montreal sits in zone A of the Autorité régionale
de transport métropolitain. Since July 1, 2026, the All Modes A monthly pass at
the regular fare costs $110. The price is the same everywhere on the island, so
it stops being a point of comparison between neighbourhoods.

What varies is time and reliability. Measure the real walking distance to the
station, not the straight line, and make the full trip at rush hour, in both
directions. Factor in a network that is still moving: the REM Deux-Montagnes
branch has been in service since November 17, 2025, and the Plan métropolitain
d'aménagement et de développement of the Communauté métropolitaine de Montréal
identifies 159 TOD areas, drawn as a radius of 0.5 or 1 km around access points
of the structuring transit network, toward which it aims to direct at least 60%
of new households from 2011 to 2031. The interactive map sits at the
Observatoire du Grand Montréal.

## 5. The building stock: age sets the checklist

The property assessment roll, produced every three years by the municipal body
responsible for assessment, carries owners' names, the characteristics of the
land and the building, their values and the cadastral lot number. Statistics
Canada's 2021 Census Profile adds the period of construction of dwellings at
the scale of a whole sector.

One Montreal-specific data point belongs here: the lead water service line. The
City targets buildings of 8 dwellings or fewer built before 1970 and publishes
an online map showing, by address, the known state of the water entry, possible
lead, confirmed lead or no lead, along with the history of interventions. Check
it before the offer, not after.

Add pyrite. The OACIQ documents cases in 91 cities of the Montreal region and
identifies the South Shore, eastern Montreal and the West Island as the highest
risk zones. A broker has a duty to inform a client when the property sits in
one. The rest plays out at the visit and the inspection:
[first visit checklist](/en/articles/first-visit-property-checklist/) and
[pre-purchase inspection](/en/articles/home-inspection-cost-timing-quebec/).

## 6. Zoning: what can happen next door, and what you will be allowed to do

Each borough has its own zoning by-law. The grids of uses and standards
published through Info-zonage are drawn from it, and the City offers a by-law
search service along with open data, including the height limits of the urban
plan.

Two concrete questions are settled there. What can be built on the vacant lot
behind your yard. And what you will be able to do with your own building: add a
dwelling, occupy the commercial ground floor, extend. A project refused at the
permit counter is a project that does not exist, whatever the seller implied.

## 7. Schools: a data point, never a ranking

The Ministère de l'Éducation calculates a socio-economic environment index
every year, from two census indicators: the share of families with children
whose mother holds no diploma, for two thirds of the weight, and the share of
households whose parents were not employed during the census reference week,
for the remaining third. Public schools are then sorted into deciles.

The index describes the environment, not the quality of teaching. And it says
nothing about which school will serve your address: the catchment area is set
by the centre de services scolaire and can be revised. Two houses on the same
street are not always in the same catchment, and a listing portal's map carries
no weight on this. Call the school service centre with the exact address. Also
worth reading:
[schools and property values](/en/articles/school-districts-property-values/).

## 8. Water and soil

The regulatory framework for water environments replaced the transitional
regime on March 1, 2026. The Quebec government's interactive map of flood zones
and watercourse mobility zones can be searched by address at
zonesinondables.mrnf.gouv.qc.ca, and remains indicative: the municipality
confirms the exact boundary.

For soil, the Ministère de l'Environnement's contaminated sites registry and
its Repère GTC tool list the files brought to the ministry's attention,
rehabilitated sites included. On an island with a dense industrial past, that
check takes three minutes. Keep in mind that the registry is not exhaustive:
the absence of a record proves nothing.

## 9. Market numbers, by borough

The Quebec Professional Association of Real Estate Brokers publishes monthly
statistics drawn from the Centris database, by metropolitan area, by
municipality and by borough. That is the number to quote. My
[market analysis](/en/articles/marche-immobilier-montreal-analyse-donnees/)
starts from that source.

## Verify instead of believing

A neighbourhood's reputation travels fast and never carries a date. The
opposite method takes one evening: open the assessment roll, the borough tax
grid, the zoning by-law, the lead map, the flood map and Repère GTC, then write
down the answer and the source. Add two visits on site, a Tuesday at rush hour
and a Saturday morning, and read the borough council agendas.

If a number cannot be found anywhere, it does not enter the decision. That
single rule removes most of what people repeat about Montreal neighbourhoods,
and what remains is a short, dated, sourced list you can actually act on.
