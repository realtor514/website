A major renovation is not planned on the price of the cabinets. It is planned on
two calendars: the one for the job site and the one for the money. The two never
line up, and it is that gap that empties accounts. Here is where the sum comes
from, at what moment each dollar leaves, and what Quebec law leaves in your hands
the day it runs short.

## Financing a major renovation in Quebec: four taps, four ceilings

The first figure to establish is not the cost of the work. It is your home
equity, which the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) defines as the
difference between the appraised value of the home and what you still owe on the
loans it secures. The ceilings below are calculated on that, never on your job
site invoices.

| Source | Ceiling published by the FCAC | How the money arrives |
|---|---|---|
| Second mortgage | 80% of appraised value | lump sum |
| Home equity line of credit | 65% of appraised value | as needed, at a variable rate |
| Home equity loan | 80% of appraised value | lump sum, fixed payments |
| Reverse mortgage | 55% of appraised value | limited to age 55 and over |

The FCAC adds the fees that come with these arrangements, appraisal, title
search, title insurance and legal fees, then the warning nobody reads: you could
face serious consequences, such as having your home seized, if you cannot repay.

A fifth tap exists, refinancing the current loan, with its own legal ceiling: the
calculation is in
[mortgage refinancing in Quebec](/en/articles/mortgage-refinancing-quebec/).
Unsecured credit depends only on your file, and no open official source lets me
put a rate on it here.

**If the work is added to a purchase**, the SCHL (Canada Mortgage and Housing
Corporation) has a dedicated product, SCHL Améliorations, whose lending value is
the lesser of the as-improved value or the current value plus the cost of the
work, supported by cost estimates, plans or a permit. Your quotes therefore do
not only serve to budget: they set the amount of the loan. The full comparison
with a turnkey purchase is in
[fixer upper or turnkey home](/en/articles/turnkey-or-fixer-upper-quebec/). Put
the total amount into the
[mortgage calculator](/en/tools/mortgage/), because it is the payment with the
work included that has to hold.

## Financing a major renovation means paying before you are reimbursed

An SCHL advance is paid out after validation: a single one when the cost of the
work represents 10% or less of the as-improved value, and up to four staged
advances beyond that. The contractor, meanwhile, gets paid during the job. In
between, it is you.

| Moment | The money move | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Before the job starts | The smallest possible deposit | RBQ |
| Before each instalment | Demand the statement of sums already paid to subcontractors | art. 2122 C.C.Q. |
| Lender's advance | Paid after validation, so after the expense | SCHL |
| End of a phase | The price of that phase becomes payable | art. 2114 C.C.Q. |
| Final acceptance | Holdback for apparent poor workmanship | art. 2111 C.C.Q. |
| Last payment | 10 to 15% of the total cost | OPC |

**The trap in article 2114.** Where the work is performed in successive phases,
it may be accepted in parts, and payment of the price of a part creates a
presumption that that part has been accepted, unless the sums paid are to be
considered as mere instalments on the price. That last clause is something to
write into your contract. Without it, every payment you make to stay afloat looks
like acceptance of the phase paid for.

Article 2111 runs the other way: you are not bound to pay before acceptance, and
on payment you may retain a sufficient amount to satisfy the reservations made as
to apparent poor workmanship, unless the contractor furnishes sufficient security.

## The licence bond: a shared ceiling, not a reimbursement

The licence bond is a financial guarantee required of contractors licensed by the
Régie du bâtiment du Québec (RBQ), the Corporation des maîtres mécaniciens en
tuyauterie or the Corporation des maîtres électriciens. Its amount depends on the
category: $40,000 for a general contractor, $20,000 for a specialized contractor.

**Those amounts are not yours.** The RBQ writes that the bond serves to indemnify
all clients who filed compliant claims for the period covered. As soon as a first
compliant claim is allowed, a file opens for 6 months, during which other
citizens can join it, and if the total exceeds the bond, the money is distributed
pro rata to the amounts claimed. A claim received later may receive nothing, and
if the bond is exhausted, there is no more compensation.

What it covers is precise: deposits paid for work not performed, failure to
complete, defects and poor workmanship noted within the 12 months following the
end of the work, court registry fees, bailiff's fees and the interest awarded in
the judgment. What it excludes matters more: the claims of persons who took part
in the work, for example a subcontractor the contractor failed to pay, damages
caused by a delay, moral injury, punitive damages and lawyers' fees.

The road is expensive. The RBQ warns that your chances of being compensated drop
greatly without a favourable judgment, and it places the courts according to the
amount claimed: $15,000 and under at small claims, from $15,000.01 to $74,999.99
at the Civil Division of the Court of Québec, from $75,000 to $99,999.99 at your
choice, and $100,000 and over at the Superior Court. On a $150,000 job that goes
wrong, you therefore plead in Superior Court, with lawyers' fees the bond does
not reimburse, to go after at best $40,000 shared with others. Verifying the
licence beforehand remains the best investment:
[choosing a renovation contractor](/en/articles/choosing-renovation-contractor-quebec/)
sets out the method.

## The construction legal hypothec ranks ahead of your lender

Article 2724 of the Code civil lists the only claims that give rise to a legal
hypothec, and the second on the list is that of persons who took part in the
construction or renovation of an immovable. Article 2726 names who benefits from
it, from the architect to the subcontractor by way of the supplier of materials,
and adds that it exists without its being necessary to publish it.

**What it secures is not the invoice, it is the added value.** Article 2728
limits it to the increase in value added to the immovable by the work, materials
or services, and, for those who did not contract with you, to what follows
written notice of the contract to the owner. The amount of that added value gets
argued, and it gets argued in court.

The calendar comes from article 2727: it subsists for 30 days after the end of
the work without publication, it is preserved by registering within that period a
notice designating the immovable and stating the amount of the claim, and it is
extinguished six months after the end of the work unless the creditor publishes
an action or registers a prior notice of the exercise of a hypothecary right.

That leaves the article that explains the reaction of the lender and the notary.
Article 2952 gives these hypothecs a rank ahead of any other published hypothec,
for the value added to the immovable. Your bank published its own. The unpaid
supplier's ranks ahead of it. And the bond, as the RBQ says, does not indemnify
the claims of persons who took part in the work: the two protections do not
overlap.

## Overruns: what you owe, and what you can refuse

The most expensive clause in your contract is the one that says how the price is
established. The Code civil treats the two cases differently.

**The lump sum contract.** Article 2109 is blunt: you pay the agreed price, and
the contractor may not claim an increase on the grounds that the work cost more
than expected. The lump sum price remains the same despite changes to the
originally contemplated conditions of performance, unless the parties have agreed
otherwise. That reservation is the door change orders come through: sign them
before the work, not after.

**A price based on an estimate.** Article 2107 reverses the burden: the
contractor must justify any increase, and you are bound to pay it only to the
extent that it results from work, services or expenses that the contractor could
not have foreseen when the contract was entered into. Something a professional
should have foreseen is not unforeseen. And where the price follows the value of
the work performed, article 2108 obliges the contractor, at your request, to
account for the progress and the expenses already incurred.

The Office de la protection du consommateur (OPC) goes the same way: the
contractor cannot bill more than the amount indicated at the outset, and must
obtain your authorization before carrying out unplanned work. Billed anyway, you
can pay under protest by writing the mention on the cheque, then demand letter,
small claims and a complaint to the Office.

As for the contingency reserve, no open official source puts a figure on a
typical overrun percentage in Quebec, so you will not get one here. The only
official signal remains the SCHL's, which asks the lender to assess your
financial capacity to cover cost overruns. The arrangement therefore assumes you
have money outside the loan.

## When the money runs short: the three exits, and their price

**Resiliate.** Article 2125 allows you to unilaterally resiliate the contract,
even if the work is under way, with no reason to give. Article 2129 sets the
price: you pay, in proportion to the agreed price, the actual costs and expenses,
the value of the work performed before the notification, and that of the property
supplied which you can use. The contractor returns advances received in excess of
what was earned.

**Phase it.** Article 2114 becomes a cash flow tool rather than a trap if the
split is written down in advance: one phase accepted, paid, released, then the
next when the money is there.

**Sell.** A registered legal hypothec will have to be settled before signing at
the notary. And if the job went very far, taxation changes regime: the Canada
Revenue Agency speaks of substantial renovation when all or substantially all of
the existing building has been removed or replaced, with foundations, exterior
walls, load-bearing walls, floors, roof and staircases excluded from the
calculation. All or substantially all is read as 90% or more, and only the living
area counts. I am a broker, not a tax specialist: the calculation is in
[house flipping in Montreal](/en/articles/house-flipping-montreal/), and what
comes back in the price is in
[your renovation will not add what you think](/en/articles/renovations-dont-add-value/).

## Frequently asked questions

**My contractor is asking for a 50% deposit. Is that normal?**
No law sets a percentage. The RBQ recommends the smallest possible deposit, and
the OPC recommends paying in instalments according to progress with a final
payment of 10 to 15% at the end. A deposit of half the price is also, exactly,
the sum the bond would have to cover if the company disappeared.

**Does the $40,000 bond reimburse me if my contractor walks away?**
Not necessarily. It indemnifies all clients who filed compliant claims for the
period, the file stays open 6 months to let other clients join, and the split is
pro rata. Exhausted, it gives nothing more.

**I paid my contractor and a supplier is claiming its invoice from me.**
If it had given you written notice of its contract, article 2728 opens a legal
hypothec for the added value, and article 2952 makes it rank ahead of your
lender's. Call a notary or a lawyer the same day: it is the immovable that is
targeted.

**Can I stop the job if I run out of money?**
Yes, article 2125 allows it during performance. But article 2129 says what you
will have to pay: the actual costs and expenses and the value of the work
performed, in proportion to the agreed price.
