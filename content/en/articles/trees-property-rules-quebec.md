A forty year old maple in the back yard sells a photograph. It does not push a
price up by a percentage I could name for you: no Quebec source lets me put a
figure on what a tree is worth on a property, and I am not going to invent one.
What can be verified, on the other hand, comes down to three things: who owns
the tree, what you have to ask the city before you touch it, and who pays when
it comes down.

## The tree belongs to whoever owns the ground the trunk comes out of

The Code civil du Québec (Quebec's civil code) devotes no article to the
question "whose tree is this". It answers it another way. Article 900 classes
plants among immovables "as long as they are not separated or extracted from
the land", and article 951 adds that "ownership of the soil carries with it
ownership of what is above and what is below". The practical result: the tree
follows the lot its trunk emerges from. Branches hanging over the neighbour's
yard and roots crossing the line change nothing.

**The rule on fruit surprises everyone.** Article 984 is short and blunt:
"Fruit that falls from a tree onto neighbouring land belongs to the owner of
the tree." So the apples that fell in your yard are not yours.

**A tree straddling the line belongs to both owners.** If the trunk grows on
either side of the dividing line, each owner owns the soil that carries it, and
the tree is held in undivided co-ownership. Article 1026 then becomes the rule
of the game: decisions of administration are taken by a majority, but decisions
to alienate the property or to make substantial alterations to it are taken
unanimously. Nobody takes down a shared tree on their own. The logic matches
article 1003: "A fence on the dividing line is presumed to be common." And be
careful, the dividing line is not the fence: it is what the certificat de
localisation (the surveyor's location certificate) shows, or failing that the
boundary determination of article 978.

## Branches and roots: what the Code civil allows

Article 985 governs here, and it deserves to be read in full:

"An owner may, where branches or roots extend over or upon his land from the
neighbouring land and seriously obstruct its use, request his neighbour to cut
them and, if he refuses, compel him to do so. Where a tree on the neighbouring
land is in danger of falling on his land, he may also compel his neighbour to
fell the tree, or to right it."

So there has to be a physical encroachment, and that encroachment has to
**seriously** obstruct the use of your land, which is a higher bar than "it
annoys me". Article 976 sets out the other half of the equation: neighbours
must suffer the normal inconveniences of neighbourhood. Shade on a corner of
lawn, leaves to rake, pollen: those are normal inconveniences.

What is not in the article is the right to cut the branches yourself. Éducaloi
(Quebec's public legal information nonprofit) is categorical: "You cannot take
the law into your own hands, even if the branches (or roots) of your
neighbour's tree that you want to cut are on your land." You ask, you send a
formal demand letter, you try mediation, then you go to court. If you cut
anyway, the court could order you to compensate your neighbour for having
damaged or killed the tree. Only the owner of agricultural land escapes that
process, under article 986, and over a maximum width of five metres.

## Cutting down a tree requires a permit almost everywhere in Quebec

This is the step owners skip most often. The Code civil says the tree is yours;
the municipal by-law says what you are allowed to do to it.

In Montreal, the city writes that a felling permit, also called a certificat
d'autorisation (authorization certificate), is required, and the thresholds
vary from one borough to the next. In Ahuntsic-Cartierville, the certificate is
required not only to fell a tree whose trunk reaches 10 cm in diameter measured
1.3 m above the ground, but also to raise the soil under its canopy by more
than 20 cm, to cut 40% or more of its branches, and to cut 40% or more of its
roots. That last line is the one that catches people who dig.

In Laval, the city goes even wider: "Before felling a tree on your property,
even if the request comes from a Hydro-Québec recommendation, obtaining a
certificat d'autorisation is mandatory." The threshold is 30 mm in diameter for
a deciduous tree and 150 cm in height for a conifer, "regardless of where the
tree sits on your land".

| Territory | What triggers the authorization | Fee |
|---|---|---|
| Laval | Deciduous tree 3 cm in diameter, conifer 150 cm tall | $84 per tree |
| Montreal, Ahuntsic-Cartierville | Trunk of 10 cm at 1.3 m from the ground, or 15 cm at 15 cm from the ground | $136 per application and per lot |
| Montreal, Côte-des-Neiges and Notre-Dame-de-Grâce | Trunk of 10 cm at 1.3 m from the ground, except cedar, juniper and buckthorn | $175 per application, whatever the number of trees |

**The fine belongs to no borough in particular.** The city's procedure page
shows the same warning everywhere: felling without a permit costs $600, and up
to $15,000 depending on the number of trees and the area cleared. One caution
when reading those pages: the borough values are loaded by your browser, so two
different boroughs show identical text to an automated reader. Have yours
confirmed at your borough's permit counter before you hire an arborist.

**The reason matters as much as the permit.** Montreal lists the grounds it
refuses, and they cover just about everything I hear during a showing: you
cannot have a tree taken down because it drops flowers, leaves, sap or twigs,
because it produces pollen, shelters animals or insects, casts shade, or
because its roots are visible at the surface of the soil. The grounds that are
accepted are a dead tree, irreversible decline, serious damage to property,
danger to public safety, the spread of a disease, and work authorized by a
building permit. Laval publishes an equivalent list. In both cities the felled
tree has to be replaced, and an application involving an ash tree is free
because of the agrile du frêne (the emerald ash borer).

## What insurance covers when a tree comes down

The Bureau d'assurance du Canada (the Insurance Bureau of Canada) answers three
questions owners ask after every storm.

If your tree falls on the neighbour's house: "Even if the tree belongs to you,
you are not responsible for the damage it may have caused during a windstorm."
You do not pay their deductible. The exception is negligence, and the example
the Bureau gives says it all: failing to cut down a tree that had been rotten
for some time, even though it risked falling on the house next door.

If a tree falls on the house, "the owner's insurance policy covers removal of
the tree and repairs to the house". And here is the sentence nobody has read
before calling their insurer: "If the tree did not damage any insured property,
the owner will be responsible for the cleanup costs." A mature tree lying
across the yard, having damaged nothing, is therefore a bill for you. Your own
contract may provide better; it is your insurer, not me, who will confirm that.

## A tree near the foundation, the drain or a power line

For a buyer, a large tree three metres from the wall is not a defect. It is an
item to document, because it touches on work that is regulated.

The roots first. If your plan involves excavating to redo the drain français
(the perimeter drain), pouring a slab or widening the driveway, you are already
inside the by-law: in Montreal, cutting 40% or more of the roots of a protected
tree requires an authorization exactly as felling does. In Laval, felling
required "in order to build or lay out a structure" has to be covered by a
certificat d'autorisation from the planning department. The right reflex is to
check before you have the work priced, not after.

The wires next. Hydro-Québec assigns each species a safe planting distance to
respect near a medium voltage line, and it periodically prunes dangerous
branches, which deforms the tree. Its directory of trees and shrubs lets you
check the species before you plant.

Then the inspection. A building inspector is not an arborist: they note cracks,
a drain that backs up, soil that moves, without passing judgment on the health
of a tree. If the tree worries you, what you need is a separate expert report,
often the same one the city requires before it issues the permit. The full list
of items examined is in the guide to the [pre purchase inspection, item by
item](/en/articles/home-inspection-checklist-montreal/).

## Selling or buying a property with a large tree: what gets declared

The Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble form (the mandatory seller's
declaration) is required in Quebec for a residential immovable of fewer than
five dwellings, and it has to be filled in and signed at the same time as the
brokerage contract. Yet it contains no question about trees. A tree file is
therefore declared elsewhere, and that is where things get forgotten: at D2.8
for servitudes and encroachments, at D3.5 for landscaping work, at D9.4 for the
drain français, at D14.1 for damage suffered as a result of ice storms or wind,
at D14.5 for any other element that could have an impact on the value or the
use of the immovable, and at D15 for the details.

Behind the form stands article 1726 of the Code civil, which obliges the seller
to warrant the immovable against latent defects, and article 1733, which stops
the seller from excluding liability for defects they knew about or could not
have been unaware of. A neighbour dispute under way over a tree, a demand
letter received, a felling done without a permit: those get said, and they get
said in writing.

**On value, I stop here.** I can tell you what a tree costs to remove, what
permits it imposes and what it changes in the contract. I cannot tell you what
it adds to the price, because no public Quebec data measures it, and because my
professional regulation requires me to be able to demonstrate the accuracy of
what I put forward. For an estimate anchored on comparable sales rather than on
an impression, start from the [estimate of your property's
value](/en/tools/home-estimate/) and have it validated. The same caution
applies to renovation work: that is exactly the reasoning in the piece on [what
renovations really add](/en/articles/renovations-dont-add-value/).

## Frequently asked questions

**My neighbour refuses to cut the branch rubbing on my roof. Can I cut it
myself?**
No. Article 985 gives you the right to compel them, not to do it in their
place. You show that the branch seriously obstructs the use of your property,
you send a formal demand letter, then you go through mediation or the court.
Cutting it yourself exposes you to having to compensate the neighbour.

**The tree is on my land and it is healthy. Do I still need a permit to take it
down?**
Yes, in the very large majority of cases in Montreal and Laval, and the permit
will only be issued if your reason appears on the list of accepted grounds. A
healthy tree that casts shade is not on that list.

**A tree is leaning over my house. What do I do first?**
You write to the neighbour, even briefly, because article 985 lets you compel
them to fell or to right a tree that is in danger of falling. That written
record also becomes your proof if the tree comes down and the question of
negligence is raised with the insurer.

**The seller had three trees cut down before listing. Is that my business?**
Yes. Ask for the certificats d'autorisation and verify the replacement
obligation, which follows the property. A non compliant felling leaves an open
requirement with the city, and you are the one who inherits it.
