The wall is open, the crew is on site, and behind the drywall there is a crack
nobody saw before the purchase. The decision that counts gets made in the hours
that follow: do we close it up and carry on? Here is the sequence, in order, and
the move that loses solid files.

## Closing the wall back up erases the proof of the hidden defect

Educaloi is blunt: if you do not want to bear the cost of the work, do not begin
repair work at your own expense. The seller must have the opportunity to verify
the existence and seriousness of the defect, to observe the damage, and to repair
or replace. A single exception is named: urgent repairs, if the defect is
dangerous or risks seriously damaging the building or leading to its loss.
Stopping a leak belongs there. Pouring a new slab does not.

A renovation adds a pressure that a defect discovered in ordinary times does not
carry: the contractor has a schedule, an accepted quote and a crew paid by the
day. Everyone around you has an interest in closing it up fast. Yet it is usually
the person making the claim who bears the burden of proof. A wall closed back up
does not testify on your behalf.

## The first hours: photograph, date, get it in writing

The small claims rules of evidence say exactly what a photo has to carry. Note the
date it was taken, what it shows and the name of the person who took it, then
annotate a copy. Quality counts more than quantity: one or two photos that
demonstrate the extent of the damage can be enough.

**Have the contractor write it down before leaving the site.** What was found,
where, on what date, in what condition. The written record matters for money too:
testimony alone is generally not enough to prove a contract or a payment of more
than $1,500 entered into with the opposing party.

**Bring in an expert while it is open.** In a hidden defect file, a building
inspector can testify as an expert to explain to the judge the cause of the defect
and the work needed to repair it. He can only explain what he saw. At the same
time, pull out your [Declarations du vendeur sur
l'immeuble](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/) and your pre-purchase
inspection report: the question is not only "what is it", but "was it declared".
If the opening reveals suspect insulation rather than a crack, everything goes
through a laboratory, as explained in [vermiculite and asbestos in a Quebec
home](/en/articles/vermiculite-asbestos-home-quebec/).

## Three possible parties, three different clocks

Before writing to anyone, you have to know to whom. The same crack can fall under
three different regimes.

| Avenue | What must be shown | The clock |
|---|---|---|
| The seller | A serious defect, existing at the sale, not apparent, unknown to you | Give written notice without delay, 3 years to sue |
| A past contractor | Work badly done, a contract, a valid licence | The finding within 12 months after the end of the work |
| Wear or apparent defect | Nothing: there is no recourse | None |

On the first avenue, article 1726 of the Code civil du Québec (Quebec's civil
code) is clear, and the OACIQ sums it up: the seller warrants that the immovable
is free of defects, existing at the time of the sale, that render it unfit for the
use for which it is intended or that so diminish its usefulness that the buyer
would not have paid the same price. It does not cover a defect known to the buyer,
nor one that a prudent and diligent buyer could have discovered without needing to
resort to an expert.

The third avenue is the one people forget. Educaloi specifies that not having an
inspection done, or not having it done in a prudent and diligent manner, amounts
to accepting the immovable with all the defects that could have been detected. A
1978 baseboard heater at the end of its life is not a hidden defect.

## Give written notice of the hidden defect, and only then repair

Article 1739 of the Code civil is explicit: the notice to the seller must be in
writing, and when the defect appears gradually the reasonable delay runs from the
day the buyer could suspect its seriousness and extent. That "reasonable"
delay is assessed according to the circumstances: Educaloi indicates that a delay
of 6 months to a year will generally be considered reasonable, exceptionally
longer if the defect appears seasonally. A stopped work site does not leave you
that comfort: the letter goes out this week.

You may send a formal demand letter directly instead of a simple notice. It
contains the description of the defect, your demands, your conditions and the
deadline to reply, and it is the prerequisite to a lawsuit. The law requires
writing, a verbal notice is not enough, and if the deadline you grant is too
short, the person will still be entitled to a reasonable delay. Educaloi
recommends facts rather than suppositions, and a page and a half at most.

One limit often surprises people: the OACIQ forbids a broker to provide a legal
opinion, for example on the time limits to bring a recourse, to draft a formal
demand letter, and to substitute for a building professional in proposing
corrective work. The broker can observe the damage with you and direct you to a
legal adviser, an architect or a building engineer. I am a broker, not a lawyer:
the letter itself gets prepared with a lawyer or a notary.

## What happens to your work site in the meantime

**Stopping the work is not stopping the payments.** If the contractor is not paid,
article 2726 of the Code civil gives him a legal hypothec on your immovable, and
the same hypothec exists for the architect, the engineer, the supplier of
materials, the worker and the subcontractor, without any need to publish it. The
protection exists upstream, in how you pay: the smallest possible deposit,
payments as the work progresses, and
an amount held back until all the planned work is completed.

**The correction can change the scope of the work, and therefore the permit.** In
Laval, a permit becomes required for an interior renovation as soon as there is a
change to the dimensions of the rooms, to the number of rooms or to the structure.
The City announces a 30 day timeline and a cost of $92 for the first $20,000, plus
$2.50 per $1,000 bracket. A change to the layout or to the structure requires an
architectural plan sealed under section 16 of the Loi sur les architectes and a
structural plan sealed by an engineer. Work done without the required permit
exposes you to a fine.

Before restarting the site with a bigger invoice, put two numbers side by side:
what the property is worth once the defect is corrected, and what the correction
costs. The [home value estimator](/en/tools/home-estimate/) gives a starting point
for the first, the contractor for the second.

## If the defect comes from work, not from the seller

An open wall often reveals an earlier renovation badly done rather than an
original flaw. The regime then changes, and the Regie du batiment du Quebec
(Quebec's building authority) describes the sequence in four steps: try to reach
an agreement with the contractor, send him a formal demand letter by registered
mail, obtain a judgment, then claim against the licence bond. The Regie warns that
your chances of being compensated drop sharply without a judgment.

The bond is $40,000 for a general contractor and $20,000 for a specialized
contractor. As soon as a first compliant claim is admitted, a file stays open for
6 months for the other clients, and if the total exceeds the bond, the money is
distributed pro rata. The conditions count as much as the amount: a valid licence
at the time of the contract or of the work, a contract where the name and licence
number match, an amount already paid, work badly done observed within 12 months
after the end of the work, and a first judicial step within 3 years following the
finding.

Two details decide files. The judgment must target the business whose name and
licence number appear on the contract, not the natural person who owns it. And if
your home insurance compensates you for damage caused by the work, you will not
receive a second compensation. The checks to make before the next contract are in
[choosing a renovation contractor in
Quebec](/en/articles/choosing-renovation-contractor-quebec/).

## The right court, and the two clocks to watch

The Regie du batiment publishes the tiers: $15,000 or less at the Small Claims
Division, from $15,000.01 to $74,999.99 at the Civil Division of the Court of
Quebec, from $75,000 to $99,999.99 at either one at your choice, and $100,000 and
up at the Superior Court.

At small claims, you act alone, without a lawyer, and the limit is $15,000 not
counting interest. If the work costs $21,000, you may choose to claim only $15,000
in order to get there. Mediation is free up to three hours per file, and it should
be favoured before filing an application. One deadline is often discovered too
late: the originals of your documents and photos must be filed in the Court record
at least 30 days before the trial, failing which the judge could refuse to take
note of them.

Two clocks are therefore running, and it is not the long one that loses files.
Three years run from the discovery of the defect to sue the seller: that is the
prescription of article 2925 of the Code civil, and article 2926 starts that clock,
for a prejudice that appears gradually, on the day it first appears. The reasonable
delay to give written notice runs, for its part, from the moment the contractor
opened the wall.

## Frequently asked questions

**The contractor wants to close it up tomorrow morning. Can I let him?** Not
before writing to the seller and having what is visible observed. The seller must
be able to verify the existence and seriousness of the defect, and it is you who
will have to prove what was behind the drywall. If the site really cannot wait,
document heavily before closing it up.

**Can I have it repaired and send the invoice to the seller?** That is the
riskiest scenario. Educaloi asks that you not begin repairs at your own expense,
because the seller must have the opportunity to verify, to observe and to repair.
Only urgent repairs escape that rule.

**The seller swears he did not know. Does that change anything?** Little in one
direction, a lot in the other. According to the OACIQ, the fact that the seller
was unaware of the defect, or that the defect only appears after the sale, does
not diminish the warranty in any way. If he knew about it or could not have been
unaware of it, article 1728 of the Code civil requires him to repair the prejudice
suffered by the buyer, on top of returning the price.

**I bought "at my own risk".** You then bought the immovable in the state it was
in, with or without defects, and in principle you have no protection against a
hidden defect. Article 1733 of the Code civil draws the line: a seller cannot free
himself from defects he knew of or could not have been unaware of, and a purchase
at your own risk is an exception to that only when the seller is not a professional
seller. Have the exact clause of your deed read by a notary before writing to
anyone.
