A real estate myth survives because nobody goes and reads the text. Here are
eight beliefs that persist in the Montreal market, and the written rule that
corrects each one, every rule verifiable at its source.

## Myth 1: you need a 20 per cent down payment

The federal rule is tiered, and 20 per cent is not the entry point. The minimum
down payment is 5 per cent up to $500,000. Above that, it is 5 per cent on the
first $500,000 and 10 per cent on the portion beyond. At $1.5 million and up,
20 per cent becomes mandatory, because CMHC caps the lending value it will
insure at $1,500,000.

What is true is that a down payment under 20 per cent means buying mortgage loan
insurance, whose premium runs, according to the Financial Consumer Agency of
Canada, from 0.6 to 4.5 per cent of the loan amount. CMHC also requires housing
costs to stay within 39 per cent of gross household income, and total debt
within 44 per cent. The arithmetic, with numbers, is in [the down payment
myth](/en/articles/down-payment-myth-debunked/).

## Myth 2: the buyer pays the broker's remuneration

Remuneration is set neither by the Real Estate Brokerage Act nor by the OACIQ.
It is negotiated. When the seller is represented, his broker offers a share of
his remuneration to the buyer's broker. The amount stipulated in the brokerage
contract to purchase is then deducted from that share, and the OACIQ says it
plainly: the buyer may have no remuneration to pay directly to his own broker.

Three useful details. The buyer's broker must disclose the share being offered
before the promise to purchase is even drafted. Payment happens at the signing
of the deed of sale, once financing is in place, and the notary distributes the
sums. And where a purchase brokerage contract does leave something payable,
clause R2.5 of the mandatory Annex R form allows it to be added to the purchase
price and financed. The possible forms, percentage, lump sum or hourly rate, are
laid out in [commission explained](/en/articles/commission-explained-quebec/).

## Myth 3: new construction is always safer

The mandatory warranty plan administered by Garantie de construction
residentielle covers detached, semi-detached and row houses, buildings of two to
five dwellings not held in divided co-ownership, and co-ownerships with four
superimposed private portions or fewer. Condo buildings with more than four
superimposed units, meaning condo towers, are excluded. So are building
conversions, renovations such as an extension or construction on existing
foundations, and self-building.

Where it does apply, the warranty is real: one year for existing non-apparent
poor workmanship, three years for latent defects, five years for design,
construction or soil defects, and it transfers to the next buyer for the
remaining term. It also protects the deposit paid to the contractor up to
$50,000, and covers up to $6,000 in rehousing, moving and storage costs if
delivery is late. Check first that the contractor is accredited and the project
registered, in [the new construction
guide](/en/articles/new-construction-guide-quebec/).

## Myth 4: a preapproval guarantees the loan

It does not. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada is explicit: a preapproval
does not guarantee you will get a mortgage for that amount, and a lender can
refuse someone who has been preapproved. Before approving, the lender verifies
that the property meets certain standards, and the final amount depends on the
value of the property as much as on your down payment.

In other words, a preapproval qualifies the borrower, not the house. The
mistakes that sink a file between preapproval and the notary are gathered in
[preapproval pitfalls](/en/articles/mortgage-preapproval-mistakes/).

## Myth 5: the asking price reflects value

The asking price is a marketing decision. Two reference points make that clear.
First the municipal assessment, routinely mistaken for value: the Act respecting
municipal taxation sets its reference date at July 1 of the second fiscal year
preceding the roll's coming into force, eighteen months earlier. The same Act
defines real value as the exchange value on a free and competitive market, the
most probable price in a private sale where neither party is under any
obligation to act. A roll therefore describes a past market, correctly measured.

Then the lender. Since the amount granted depends on the value of the property,
a price paid above that value does not get financed: the gap is filled in cash.
That is exactly what plays out in [the truth about bidding
wars](/en/articles/bidding-wars-truth-montreal/).

## Myth 6: you can cancel an accepted promise by changing your mind

In principle, no. Educaloi puts it bluntly: an offer to purchase presented to
the seller is itself a contract. During the acceptance period it is irrevocable,
and you can only withdraw it if your notice reaches the seller before the offer
does. The Civil Code adds that once the promise is accepted, both parties are
bound to conclude the sale.

What remains are the exits written into the contract, such as the inspection
condition, which assumes a significant problem rather than a change of heart, or
an agreement with the seller, bearing in mind that brokers who did their work
may claim their remuneration.

One genuine right of withdrawal exists, and it belongs to new construction.
Where a builder or a developer sells a residential immovable, built or to be
built, to a natural person acquiring it to live in, the sale must be preceded by
a preliminary contract, and that contract must contain a stipulation letting the
promissory buyer withdraw within 10 days. Any indemnity the contract provides
for exercising it cannot exceed 0.5 per cent of the agreed sale price.

## Myth 7: the seller must accept the highest offer

No rule requires it. The OACIQ writes that the seller has the right to refuse a
promise to purchase even where the price and conditions offered meet his
expectations, and that it is ultimately up to him to accept one offer over
another. An occupancy date, financing already in place or the absence of
conditions sometimes weighs more than the number.

What is regulated is the procedure. The broker must present every promise as
soon as it is received, regardless of the order of arrival, the identity of the
buyers or the circumstances. He cannot reduce his share of remuneration to
favour one buyer. And the OACIQ warns against the strategy of collecting all
offers at a set date and time, which runs against the duty to present each
proposal without delay.

## Myth 8: an inspection guarantees there is no problem

It guarantees nothing, and it does something more. Educaloi points out that the
legal warranty does not apply to what a pre-purchase inspection, an up to date
certificate of location or a notary's search of the land register could have
revealed. The Civil Code runs the same way: the seller does not warrant an
apparent defect, the kind a prudent and diligent buyer can detect without an
expert.

An inspection is still essential, but as a decision tool, not as an insurance
policy. What it actually covers is set out in [the checklist, item by
item](/en/articles/home-inspection-checklist-montreal/).

## What is left once the myths fall

Each of these eight corrections comes from a text you can read yourself, and
none of them rests on an opinion. The useful reflex is always the same: ask for
the source before adjusting an offer, and distrust any advice that starts with
you should always. The same reflex is what spots [the red flags that justify
walking away](/en/articles/red-flags-walk-away-property/).
