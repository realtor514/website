On a Centris listing, the mention fits on one line: sold without legal warranty
of quality, at the buyer's own risk and peril. Many read it as an admission that
something is wrong. Others sign it without realizing they are giving up a remedy
they would otherwise have had without asking for anything. Both are mistaken,
and the difference between those two wordings decides what you will have left if
a defect appears.

## What the legal warranty covers, without you having to ask for it

Article 1716 of the Civil Code sets the base: the seller is bound to deliver the
property and to warrant the right of ownership and its quality. These warranties
exist by operation of law, with no need to stipulate them in the contract.

The warranty of quality is article 1726. The seller warrants that the property
is, at the time of the sale, free of latent defects which render it unfit for
the use for which it was intended, or which so diminish its usefulness that the
buyer would not have bought it, or would not have paid so high a price, had he
known of them. Two limits are written into it: a latent defect the buyer already
knew about, and the apparent defect, the one a prudent and diligent buyer can
notice without calling in an expert.

If the seller knew of the defect or could not have been unaware of it, article
1728 requires him, on top of restoring the price, to make reparation for the
injury suffered. The OACIQ adds the point sellers forget: the fact that the
seller does not know of the defect, or that it only shows up after the sale,
takes nothing away from the warranty.

## Excluding the garantie légale de qualité or selling at the buyer's risk and peril: two different clauses

Article 1732 allows the parties to add to the obligations of the legal warranty,
to reduce its effects or to exclude it entirely. One limit only: the seller may
not, in any case, exempt himself from his own personal acts.

Article 1733 has to be read twice. First paragraph: the seller may not exclude
or limit his liability if he failed to disclose defects he knew of or could not
have been unaware of. Second paragraph: this rule admits of an exception where
the buyer buys at his own risk and peril from a non-professional seller.

**That is where the two wordings stop being synonyms.** Excluding only the
garantie légale de qualité (the legal warranty of quality) deprives you of the
ordinary latent defect remedy, but leaves the first paragraph in play: if you
show that the seller knew of the defect and did not disclose it, the exclusion
does not protect him. Add the words "aux risques et périls de l'acheteur" (at
the buyer's own risk and peril) in front of a non-professional seller, and the
second paragraph knocks that protection out.

The Court of Appeal has spelled out how far this goes. In Blais c. Laforce, 2022
QCCA 858, confirmed by Tremblay c. Immeubles Perron ltée, 2024 QCCA 719, it
holds that the complete exclusion clause is a serious warning that the property
is sold without any warranty whatsoever despite the defects that may affect it,
and that it allows a presumed knowledge of their existence to be attributed to
the buyer. The OACIQ draws the consequence: a seller's silence about a known
defect no longer automatically amounts to fraud in a sale "aux risques et
périls".

That complete clause also breaks the chain. According to the OACIQ, the buyer
then gives up the warranty of his immediate seller and those of the previous
sellers. The warranties are reset starting from that sale.

| Wording in the contract | What falls away | What remains |
|---|---|---|
| Exclusion on certain components | The quality of the named elements | The warranty on everything else |
| Exclusion aimed at the immediate seller | The remedy against the current seller | The warranties of previous owners |
| Without any warranty, at the buyer's risk and peril | The remedy and the whole chain | Proof of fraud, a heavier burden |

The OACIQ strongly recommends that brokers stop using the general mention "sans
garantie légale à ses risques et périls", so as not to needlessly wipe out
earlier warranties.

## What the seller can never erase

**His personal acts.** Article 1732 allows no exception. A seller who repaints
an infiltration stain the night before the showings, or who answers falsely on
the Déclarations du vendeur form (the seller's declarations on the immovable),
does not get to hide behind the exclusion.

**Fraud.** The exception in the second paragraph of 1733 does not cover deceit.
After Tremblay, the buyer has to prove more: the seller's knowledge of the
defect, but also that his silence was intentional and determinative in the
decision to buy. Heavier, not impossible.

**The seller's status.** The exception only plays with a non-professional
seller. A professional seller stays under the first paragraph, and article 1729
adds a presumption against him: the defect is presumed to have existed at the
time of the sale if the property deteriorates prematurely compared with property
of the same kind.

The OACIQ points out, finally, that the exclusion does not cover bodily or moral
injury caused to others: a buyer can claim for health problems caused by mould
the seller concealed.

## Why estates and repossessions sell this way

The clause is not a confession. The OACIQ lists the situations where a complete
exclusion can be justified, and the first is the seller's limited knowledge: a
repossession or an estate. Then come the building of generally poor quality or
slated for demolition, the seller who refuses to warrant the compliance of a
change of use planned by the buyer, and the elderly person who wants to avoid a
claim against her estate.

In an estate, the liquidateur (the liquidator of the succession) has often never
lived in the building. The Déclarations du vendeur form nonetheless remains
mandatory, the OACIQ specifies, and a liquidator who cannot answer must write at
clause D15 who he is and why. A form with justified blanks is not a missing
form. The rest of the file is in [inheriting property in
Quebec](/en/articles/inheritance-property-quebec/).

A financial institution, on the other hand, is not a natural person. The form is
mandatory for the sale, by a natural person, of a mainly residential immovable
of fewer than five dwellings. For a seller that is a legal person, it is only
strongly recommended, the OACIQ citing the case of financial institutions
repossessing a building: so you will not always have declarations to read. And
if the sale takes place sous contrôle de justice (under judicial authority),
article 1731 settles it: it gives rise to no obligation of warranty of quality.

## What a sale without legal warranty in Quebec changes for inspection and financing

**The inspection stops being a formality.** With the warranty, the line between
an apparent defect and a latent defect carries real stakes. Without the
warranty, it loses its point: anything not found before signing will be yours.
The OACIQ asks the broker to insist on a detailed pre-purchase inspection and to
warn the inspector about the exclusion.

The inspector must meet four regulatory requirements: professional liability
insurance covering faults, errors or omissions, a recognized inspection service
agreement, a recognized standard of practice, and a written report. Stretch the
inspection deadline, since specialized tests have to be scheduled: the costs and
the timing are in [home inspection in Quebec: what it costs and when to do
it](/en/articles/home-inspection-cost-timing-quebec/).

**Financing follows its own rules.** What is verifiable: the Déclarations du
vendeur form and the documents attached to it form an integral part of the
promise to purchase and must be sent to the mortgage lender, who then sets the
terms of the loan in full knowledge. What I cannot claim: no official source
lets me say that a lender automatically refuses a property sold without legal
warranty. Ask the question before you file the offer, not after.

## What to demand in exchange

Giving up a right has a price. The OACIQ asks the seller's broker to inform him
that the exclusion could, depending on market conditions, reduce buyer interest
and affect the price, and the buyer's broker to tell him that the price offered
should take that into account. No official source sets a percentage: the
discount is justified by the estimated cost of the work you will carry alone.
Set your benchmark with the [property value
estimator](/en/tools/home-estimate/), then subtract that costed risk.

- **A tailored clause rather than a general one.** Exclude only the immediate
  seller's warranty, or only on the pool, the fireplace, the heating or the air
  conditioning. The OACIQ's 3.5 model clauses are laid out that way.
- **The warranty of the right of ownership, kept.** The OACIQ writes that a
  licence holder must not recommend excluding it, save in very rare exceptions.
- **Written questions, with the answers on file.** A seller who is asked should,
  the OACIQ notes, break his silence and declare the defects he knows of.
- **Legal advice before the promise.** Given how serious the consequences are,
  the broker must recommend that the parties consult a legal adviser.

## When to pass

Three signals justify stepping back. The seller refuses the tailored clause
without explaining why the exclusion has to be total. The inspector flags an
area he could not examine and the seller refuses to let it be tested. The
discount granted does not cover the worst case costed by a contractor. In that
last case, you are not buying a house, you are buying an option on work whose
price you do not know. The other signals are gathered in [walking away from a
property: seven red flags](/en/articles/red-flags-walk-away-property/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Does "without legal warranty" mean the house has a problem?** No. The OACIQ
ranks estates and repossessions among the cases where the exclusion is justified
by the seller's limited knowledge. The clause says who is selling and what he
agrees to warrant, not what condition the building is in. It is the inspection
that answers that second question.

**Does the seller still have to complete the seller's declarations?** Yes, when
a natural person sells a mainly residential immovable of fewer than five
dwellings, estates included. The OACIQ specifies that a broker cannot sign a
brokerage contract with a seller who refuses to complete and sign that form.

**If I find a defect after the sale, do I still have a remedy?** It depends on
the text you signed. With an exclusion of the warranty of quality only, the
first paragraph of article 1733 leaves you a remedy if you prove the seller knew
of the defect and did not disclose it. With the words "aux risques et périls" in
front of a non-professional seller, you have to prove fraud. In both cases, give
written notice within a reasonable time, article 1739.

**Can I resell with the legal warranty later on?** Yes, but it will bind only
you. A complete exclusion breaks the chain of transmission, and your buyer will
not be able to turn against the previous owners. The broker must in fact check
the earlier sales, which the title search reveals.
