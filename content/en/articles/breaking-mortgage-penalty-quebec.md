A lender offers you a full percentage point below your current rate. Before you
say yes, one number is still missing, the one nobody puts in the ad: the
penalty. It cannot be guessed, it has to be calculated, and two institutions can
land on wildly different amounts for the same loan. Here is the mechanism, then
the Quebec fees that get added on top.

## Open or closed: where the penalty for breaking a mortgage comes from

**The penalty does not punish a late payment, it pays for a broken promise.** The
Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation describes an open mortgage as one you can
repay in full or in part at any time without penalty, usually in exchange for a
higher rate. A closed mortgage does not let you repay the full balance before
maturity, and its rate is lower. You traded flexibility for rate, and the penalty
is the price of buying that flexibility back.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada gives the order of magnitude: cancelling
a closed mortgage usually means paying a prepayment penalty, which can run into
thousands of dollars.

One point about long terms. Section 10 of the Interest Act provides that when a
mortgage on real property is not repayable before more than five years, the
debtor may, once that period has passed, repay the principal and accrued interest
by adding three months' interest in lieu of notice. With a term of five years or
less, that ceiling does not protect you during the term.

## The two penalty formulas for breaking a mortgage

**FCAC states the rule in one sentence: it is the higher of the two amounts.**
Either three months' interest on the outstanding balance, or the interest rate
differential. Not the average, not the one you prefer. The higher one.

| | Three months' interest | Interest rate differential |
|---|---|---|
| Calculation base | Outstanding balance and your rate | Gap between two rates, over the time left |
| What makes it grow | A large balance | A current rate well below yours |
| When it applies | Always calculated | When your rate exceeds current rates |
| Sensitive to time left | No | Yes, strongly |

FCAC adds that the differential comes into play when your mortgage rate is higher
than prevailing rates and the contract is less than five years old. Hold on to
the consequence: if current rates are equal to or higher than yours, the
differential collapses toward zero and only the three months' interest remains.

Your prepayment privileges do not apply to a full cancellation. They work
before it: FCAC advises using them in full and making a lump-sum payment before
you break the mortgage, because the penalty will then be calculated on a lower
balance.

## Mortgage penalty calculation: formulas and a worked example

**Each formula fits on one line.**

- Three months' interest = outstanding balance x contract annual rate / 12 x 3
- Interest rate differential = outstanding balance x (contract rate - comparison rate) x months left / 12

For the differential, the method FCAC describes has five moves.

1. Take the outstanding balance.
2. Take the number of months left in the term.
3. Calculate the interest still owed at your rate.
4. Recalculate that same interest at the comparison rate the lender uses.
5. Take the difference. That is the differential.

**The agency's worked example shows the size of the gap.** An outstanding balance
of $200,000, a rate of 6%, 36 months left on a five-year term, a current posted
rate of 4% for a 36-month term. Three months' interest gives $3,000, that is
$200,000 multiplied by 6%, divided by four. The differential gives $12,000, that
is $200,000 multiplied by the 2-point gap, multiplied by the three remaining
years. The penalty payable is $12,000, plus possible administration fees. Four
times the expected amount.

**These formulas give an order of magnitude, not the bill.** FCAC notes that
financial institutions offer online calculators, and recommends checking the
actual amount with your lender. A generic mortgage penalty calculator knows
neither your discount nor the method set out in your contract: the next section
shows that this method alone can multiply the result by four.

Before you compare anything, put a number on the real payment difference with the
[mortgage calculator](/en/tools/mortgage/). If the savings accumulated over the
months you have left do not cover the penalty and the fees, the decision is
already made.

## Posted rate or discounted rate: the discount that inflates the penalty

**This is the part that surprises even experienced borrowers.** FCAC explains
that the lender compares two rates, and each one can be picked in two ways. The
first is either the posted rate at the time you signed, or your actual or
discounted rate, depending on what the contract provides. The second is either
the current posted rate for a term of similar length, or that posted rate minus
the discount you were originally given. Four combinations, four results. And the
more generous the discount you negotiated at signing, the wider the gap between
the original posted rate and the reduced comparison rate becomes.

An arithmetic illustration, with invented figures meant to show the mechanism.
These are not market rates. A balance of $300,000, 30 months left, a contract
rate of 5%, a posted rate at signing of 6.5%, so a discount of 1.5 points.
Current posted rate for a comparable term: 4%.

| Comparison method | Gap used | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three months' interest | Not applicable | $300,000 x 5% / 4 | $3,750 |
| Contract rate against current posted rate | 1.00 point | $300,000 x 1% x 2.5 years | $7,500 |
| Original posted rate against current posted rate minus the discount | 4.00 points | $300,000 x 4% x 2.5 years | $30,000 |

Same loan, same day, same balance. The method chosen changes the number by a
factor of four. So the question to ask is never "how much", it is "which two
rates did you use". The posted rate against the rate you actually got is worked
out in
[what gets decided before your mortgage rate](/en/articles/best-mortgage-rate-canada/).

## Fixed or variable: what your contract decides

**The two products are not penalized the same way, but not for the reason people
repeat.** CMHC defines a fixed-rate mortgage as one whose rate does not change
during the term, and a variable-rate mortgage as one whose rate fluctuates with
financial market conditions. Now, the differential compares the remaining
interest at two rates set for the time that is left. On a variable rate, your
rate is precisely not set, which deprives the formula of its term of comparison.
Many variable-rate contracts therefore provide for the three months' interest.

That is not a general rule, though. FCAC publishes no
separate scale for variable rates, and its sample information box for that
product repeats the same wording as the fixed-rate one: the higher of the two
amounts. Your contract decides, not the product's reputation.

## The Quebec fees: notarial deed, discharge and cancellation

**Changing lenders in Quebec goes through a public officer.** The Government of
Quebec indicates that registrations in the Registre foncier (Quebec's land
register) are generally carried out by a notary or a lawyer, and that sales,
mortgages and mortgage cancellations are among the common deeds published there.
Your new lender wants a published security: a deed received before a notary, then
registered.

**And the old mortgage does not vanish on its own.** Still according to the
Government of Quebec, cancellation is not automatic, it has to be requested, and
only notaries and lawyers may prepare the discharge that allows the registration
to be cancelled. The registration fees for that discharge are normally yours to
pay.

Two items have a public price. Here is the Registre foncier tariff in force since
April 1, 2026.

| Item | Fee |
|---|---|
| Registration of a right, including a mortgage | $145 per application |
| Cancellation: discharge, release | $165 per application |
| Each additional registration to cancel | $101 more |
| Certified statement of rights | $17 |
| Consulting a document | $1.50 |

The rest is not tariffed. Notary fees are not set by any public schedule: ask for
them in writing before you retain anyone. On the full sequence, see
[the notary's role in Quebec](/en/articles/quebec-notary-process/).

Now add it all up. Penalty, administration, appraisal, reinvestment, discharge
and registration fees: that is the list FCAC asks you to have quoted before you
cancel. If you received cash back at signing, a portion of it may also have to be
repaid.

## Three questions to ask in writing, and the cases where breaking pays

### The three questions

**1. What is the exact amount today, and until what date is it valid?** The code
of conduct applicable to federally regulated institutions provides that once your
intention to repay is confirmed, the lender gives you a written statement setting
out the applicable charges, the calculation method, how long the amount stays
valid, the factors that could change it and any other amount owing. The same
code also provides for annual information explaining the calculation method and
the factors that affect the charges, with no prior confirmation needed. A number
given over the phone is worth nothing.

**2. Which two rates did you use, and why those?** The same code provides that if
the calculation rests on the differential, the written statement specifies the
outstanding balance, the annual interest rate, the comparison rate used and the
time left in the term. Demand those four numbers before you compare two offers.

**3. Which fees get added to the penalty, item by item?** Administration,
appraisal, reinvestment, discharge and registration, in the same document.

### When the move still holds up

**You are selling.** CMHC describes an assumable mortgage as an existing loan the
buyer takes responsibility for, which can avoid appraisal costs and fees.
Portability to your next property deserves the same question. The other items are
in
[the real cost of selling a house in Quebec](/en/articles/real-cost-selling-home-quebec/).

**The couple separates.** Buying out the other party's share often requires a new
loan: the penalty becomes an exit cost to budget for, not a decision. The
framework is explained in
[separation, divorce and the home in Quebec](/en/articles/divorce-real-estate-guide/).

**You need the equity.** The math changes, because you are no longer buying only
a rate. Ceilings, lost insurance and fees are detailed in
[mortgage refinancing in Quebec](/en/articles/mortgage-refinancing-quebec/).

**You may not have to break anything to move.** FCAC describes the blend and
extend option, which extends the loan before the end of the term without a
penalty, the lender blending the old rate with the rate for the new term. Its
example: $200,000 at 5.5% with 24 months left, a new rate of 4% over five years,
a blended rate of 4.6% over 60 months. Administration fees may apply.

**You can wait for maturity.** At maturity, switching lenders triggers no
penalty, because there is no promise left to break. What remains is the setup
costs and, in Quebec, the deed and the cancellation.

**The policy rate does not set the penalty.** The Bank of Canada raised its
target for the overnight rate to 2.50% on October 28, 2026, with the next
announcement on December 9, 2026. The differential, for its part, is calculated
on posted mortgage rates for a comparable term. When those rates fall, the gap
with your rate widens and the differential grows; when they climb back toward
your rate, it shrinks.
