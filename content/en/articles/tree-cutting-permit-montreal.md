A maple dying in the backyard, a tree service quote for Thursday, and the
surprise: the City decides. In Montreal, the tree cutting permit is requested
from the borough, and the published fees range from $58 to $268 depending on the
address. In Laval, it is $84 per tree and a paper form sent by mail. Here is the
real process, the reasons that get accepted, the ones that get refused, and what
cutting a tree without authorization costs.

## In Montreal and Laval, a tree cutting permit is the rule

The City of Montreal is explicit: to cut down a tree on your property, a felling
permit, also called a certificate of authorization, is required. The threshold,
however, belongs to the borough. In Ahuntsic-Cartierville, the certificate is
required for a trunk 10 cm in diameter or more, measured 1.3 m above the ground.
In Lachine, it is also 10 cm, but cedar, juniper and buckthorn can be cut
without a permit whatever their diameter.

And it is not only about the saw. In Ahuntsic-Cartierville, the certificate is
also required to raise the soil under the crown by more than 20 cm, to cut 40%
or more of the branches and to cut 40% or more of the roots. That is the line
that catches owners who have excavation done.

In Laval, the threshold is lower and the City leaves no grey area: "Before
cutting down a tree on your property, even if the request comes from a
recommendation by Hydro-Québec, you must obtain a certificate of
authorization." The requirement covers deciduous trees 30 mm in diameter and up
and conifers 150 cm tall and up, wherever the tree stands on the lot.
Section 412 of the urban planning code also treats as felling the removal of
more than 50% of the living crown and the severing of more than 40% of the
anchoring roots. Who owns the tree is a separate question, settled in the Code
civil du Québec: it is covered in the piece on
[the tree, the neighbour and the by-law](/en/articles/trees-property-rules-quebec/).

## The accepted reasons are few, the list of refusals is longer

The permit is not a formality. Montreal publishes the admissible reasons: the
tree is dead, it is in a state of irreversible decline, it is causing serious
damage to property, it endangers public safety, it risks spreading a disease or
an invasive exotic species, or it stands in the footprint of a future
construction or less than 3 metres from that footprint.

**The reason most often given is precisely a prohibited one.** Montreal lists
what gives you no right to anything: falling flowers, leaves, sap or twigs,
pollen, animals and insects, shade, roots visible at the surface of the ground,
and the fact that the tree hinders the growth of more valuable trees. That last
point deserves a warning, because the opposite is often written: in its online
form, Ahuntsic-Cartierville specifies that you must not select this reason,
because no certificate can be issued on that basis.

Laval keeps an equivalent list in section 410 of its urban planning code, and it
adds a burden of proof: considerable damage to buildings and major structural
weaknesses must be demonstrated by a report from an arboriculture professional
of the City.

## The file, the processing time and the fee for a tree cutting permit

The file looks the same from one borough to the next: a scale plan, ideally the
certificat de localisation, on which each tree is numbered with its diameter and
species, photos of the trees involved, and in most cases the report of an expert
recognized by the borough, a forest engineer or an agronomist. In a condo, add a
letter of authorization from each co-owner or a resolution of the syndicate. An
inspector then visits the site. In Laval, if the trunk straddles the property
line, the form requires the neighbour's name and written authorization.

The certificat de localisation is therefore the document to pull out before you
call the tree service. It is also one of the first documents I gather when
[I prepare a listing](/en/seller/): it is what shows where the boundaries are.

| Territory | Published fees | Published processing time |
|---|---|---|
| Laval | $84 per tree | not published |
| Rivière-des-Prairies, Pointe-aux-Trembles | $58 per property | not published |
| Villeray, Saint-Michel, Parc-Extension | $64 per tree, residential | not published |
| Pierrefonds-Roxboro | $70 per request | 4 weeks |
| Ahuntsic-Cartierville | $136 per request and per lot | not published |
| Rosemont, La Petite-Patrie | $162 per tree | 6 to 8 weeks |
| Outremont | $177.80 per tree | 10 business days |
| Verdun | $268 per tree | not published |

The real difference is not the amount, it is the unit. A fee per request caps
the bill whatever the number of trees, a fee per tree multiplies it. Le
Sud-Ouest grants a 50% reduction to owner-occupants aged 65 and over, and
Saint-Laurent issues the certificate free of charge when the tree is dead. The
same logic of prior authorization applies to other structures on a lot, as the
guide to the [shed, carport and garage permit](/en/articles/shed-carport-garage-permit-quebec/)
shows.

## Ash trees follow a separate regime, and the permit is free

The emerald ash borer has its own framework, still in force: the Règlement
relatif à la lutte contre l'agrile du frêne (the by-law on the fight against the
emerald ash borer). The procedure is separate from the one for other trees, and
a request for a certificate to cut an ash tree is free, in Montreal as in Laval.
If the inspection reveals that the tree is not an ash, the analysis fees become
payable again.

An ash tree owner has obligations, not just rights. Montreal writes that a
healthy ash must be treated against the borer every 2 years, a service offered
free of charge on request made between May 1 and July 31. A dead ash, for its
part, must be cut down, at the owner's expense.

**Three deadlines to remember.** If you receive a felling notice from the City,
you have 30 days to file the permit application, or face a fine. Once the
certificate is issued, you have 365 days to cut the tree, or face a fine.
Replacement is mandatory, and failing to replace is also subject to a fine.
Montreal pays a grant of $10 per centimetre of diameter for each ash tree cut
down, to be requested within 365 days of the felling, but only if you let the
City plant the replacement tree. And it is prohibited to keep unprocessed ash
wood, and therefore to keep it as firewood.

## The fine is not set by the City, it is in the law

This is the number almost no one knows. Laval's urban planning code puts no
amount in its table of fines for the line "illegally cutting down a tree": it
refers to the Act respecting land use planning and development. According to the
ministère des Affaires municipales et de l'Habitation, that Act sets a minimum
fine of $500, to which is added, for felling on an area of less than one
hectare, an amount of $100 to $200 per tree illegally cut, up to a maximum of
$5,000. For one hectare or more, it is $5,000 to $15,000 per full hectare
cleared. These amounts are doubled for a repeat offence. Lachine puts a figure on
the same thing its own way: the fine is $600 and can go up to $15,000 depending
on the number of trees cut and the area cleared.

And the proof is easier to make than people think. In Laval, section 410
provides a presumption of illegal felling as soon as the stump left in place
measures at least 4 cm in diameter, measured no higher than 15 cm above the
ground. The stump talks.

## Mandatory replacement follows the land

The permit is not the end of the obligation, it is the beginning. In Montreal,
every tree cut down must be replaced within 12 months of the certificate being
issued, by a single trunk deciduous tree at least 3 cm in diameter and 2 m tall,
or by a 1 m conifer. Within 100 m of an eco-territory or a wooded area, the
replacement tree must be native.

In Laval, replacement is due within 6 months of the certificate being issued,
not counting December, January, February and March. It is required when the
remaining number of trees drops below a ratio of one tree per 100 m² of lot, but
also, and this is the part that matters for a buyer, when a tree was cut in
contravention of the by-law, or when the work that justified the felling was
never carried out. This obligation does not follow the owner who did the
cutting. It stays attached to the property.

## What gets declared at the sale, and what the insurer looks at

The mandatory Déclarations du vendeur sur l'immeuble form, DV 00001 in its
version 24 of June 2022, asks no question about trees. Irregular felling is
therefore declared elsewhere, and two boxes are enough. D2.12: "Have you ever
received a notice of non-compliance from an insurance company, the municipality
or the government?" D14.4 b): "Were the required permits issued for each of
these works?" That leaves D14.5 for any other element that may have an impact on
the value or use of the property, and D15 for details. How the form works is
explained in the piece on
[the seller's declaration](/en/articles/seller-declaration-form-quebec/).

On the insurance side, the Insurance Bureau of Canada points out that a tree's
owner is not liable for damage caused by a windstorm, unless they were
negligent, and its example is the rotten tree that was not cut down. Hence a
simple reflex: if you apply for a permit for a tree you consider dangerous and
the City refuses, keep the refusal in writing. As for what your contract covers
after that, your insurer answers, not me.

## Frequently asked questions

**My tree service is coming Thursday. Can I cut now and sort it out afterwards?**
No. Montreal writes that the certificate must have been obtained before
proceeding, and the published processing time ranges from 10 business days in
Outremont to 6 to 8 weeks in Rosemont, La Petite-Patrie. Felling done before
the certificate is an offence, and the replacement obligation is added to the
fine.

**The tree casts shade and hinders my other trees. Is that a reason?**
No, in both cases. Shade appears on the list of refused reasons in Montreal as
in Laval, and hindering the growth of more valuable trees is also a refused
reason, to the point that Ahuntsic-Cartierville asks you not to select it in
its online form.

**If the City refuses, do I get my fees back?**
Not necessarily. Villeray, Saint-Michel and Parc-Extension specifies that the
amounts paid for reviewing the file are not refundable if the permit is refused
or the request is cancelled. Le Sud-Ouest and Pierrefonds-Roxboro also write
that their fees are not refundable.

**I am buying a house where three trees were cut down last year. What should I
ask for?**
The certificates of authorization, proof of replacement, and a written answer
to D2.12. If the property is in Laval and stumps of 4 cm or more are still
visible, the presumption of illegal felling applies, and the obligation to plant
stays attached to the land you are buying.
